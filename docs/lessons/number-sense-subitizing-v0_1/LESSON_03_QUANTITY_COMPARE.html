Teach Same Number, Different Shape?

Reader-friendly educator and family guide. The complete reviewed technical plan and facilitator resource are in the other tabs. This is a written design, not a child-tested result.

What the child is learning

Children notice that groups can contain the same number of objects even when they look different. They explore how changing spacing, shape or color does not automatically change quantity. They also practice seeing an unknown single group's amount without counting each item in sequence. Correctly saying “same” about matching pictures alone does not demonstrate that second skill.

Prepare safe, unnumbered patterns

Gather large, safe counters or washable shapes, two separate blank spaces/mats, and several unfamiliar small arrangements. Vary shape, spacing and object color while keeping exact amounts under adult control; keep any answer sheet away from the child. Do not flash cards, use a stopwatch or make speed into the score. Allow a comfortable accessible view and responses in speech, sign, home language or a communication aid.

For the 3-year-old group, begin with one and two when needed, then three and mixed one-to-three. For the 4-year-old group, revisit smaller groups, then explore four and later five and six. The full one-to-three or one-to-six recognition check belongs later, at a suitable level.

Teaching sequence

1. Look at two new groups

Show two small, unfamiliar arrangements with no numerals.

Say: “What number do you notice in each? Do these groups have the same number? How can you tell?”

Record the child's first amounts and their same/different idea separately. A child might notice color or overall length rather than quantity; that is a useful teaching starting point, not a reason to hurry.

2. Show what stays the same

Put the first examples away. Arrange another group and change where the pieces sit without adding or removing any.

Say: “I moved the pieces. Did I add any? Did I take any away? What number do you notice now?”

Use a different example where one object actually is added or removed, so the child can contrast changed placement with changed amount. You can demonstrate looking at small visible clusters instead of tapping every object in order.

3. Let the child compare fresh pairs

Offer new pairs whose quantities may be equal or differ by one. Include a pair with different colors but equal numbers, and another with matching colors but unequal numbers.

Say: “Choose which pair to look at. Do they have the same amount? What are you noticing?”

Accept thoughtful counting as another legitimate way to check, while explaining that the separate learning goal is noticing an amount without serial counting. If the child simply matches shapes, show an alternative practice pair that separates appearance from number.

4. Invite recognition of a completely new single group

Put all comparison pairs away. Show a genuinely new, unnumbered single group at the child's current learning level.

Say: “What number do you notice in this group?”

Record the first amount before any hint, then ask how they noticed. Notice whether a child visibly counted each object; a correct quantity from serial counting is counting evidence, not proof of group perception. When the child gives a correct but unexplained answer, the strategy remains uncertain.

The complete later technical check uses new one-to-three groups for the 3-year-old range or new one-to-six groups for the 4-year-old range. Split these over sessions as appropriate, and never reuse a pattern that particular child has already seen.

5. Revisit on a genuine later day

Use new material and spatial arrangements, without telling the correct quantity. Record actual later responses; don't assume a delayed visit occurred.

Teach Same Number, Different Shape? with a Class

Classroom teaching pathway for preschool educators working with multiple children. The companion Teach 1-on-1 path serves one adult and one learner. These are educational written designs, not evidence of observed classroom effectiveness. Classroom structure and group size may be adapted to real staffing and children's needs.

What children are learning

Children notice that groups can contain the same number of objects even when they look different. They explore how changing spacing, shape or color does not automatically change quantity. They also practice seeing an unknown single group's amount without counting each item in sequence. Correctly saying “same” about matching pictures alone does not demonstrate that second skill.

Prepare the classroom

Prepare pairs of unnumbered dot groups with changed spacing or arrangement, safe manipulatives and an adult-only demonstration. Retain novel comparison pairs for selected children's checks. Start with sizes learners can genuinely perceive before exploring larger sets.

Introduce the idea together

Show two differently shaped small groups and ask, “Do they have the same number? What did you notice?” Allow individual silent answers, then discuss possible perceptions. Model with a different pair why moving dots apart need not change how many there are.

Give children meaningful practice

At four comparison tables, one child arranges two groups and another privately notices quantity before a partner explains. Let children change layout without changing actual pieces. A teacher samples a few first responses and strategies; an echoed group answer remains practice, not evidence of whole-group no-count perception.

Observe individual learning fairly

For a focal learner, present previously unseen pairs, elicit the two group amounts separately if needed, then ask same or different. A child who counts serially, guesses, or follows a partner is showing something distinct from immediate whole-group recognition. Keep those distinctions in notes rather than one generic “correct” check.

Include, respond, stop and revisit

Use larger spaced shapes, tactually accessible arrangements or extra processing time without mandating a timed flash. Preserve a valid child-chosen layout and allow counting as an alternative learning strategy, even when the particular no-count evidence is not established. Return with different pairs as learning progresses.

Teacher's recording rule: Whole-group talk and collaborative practice count as learning opportunities, not automatic proof of every child's independent understanding. Record only the action, context, support and developmental stage actually observed. For children whose independent opportunity did not occur, use not yet observed and schedule a suitable later chance; a planned later-day revisit is not already completed. No child should be deprived of meaningful participation just because the educator cannot assess everyone at once.

Subitizing Lesson 03 — Same Number, Different Shape?

Required developmental teaching sequence: PK3 foundation → PK4 extension. PK3: notice 1–2, then unfamiliar 3, then mixed 1–3; PK4: revisit 1–3, then new 4, then 5–6, then mixed 1–6. Never replace no-serial-count evidence with speed or correct serial counting. The existing full-grade EXIT is a later source-range check, not the first teaching session. Apply this ordered sequence in SOLO, CO-PLAY, ADULT-LED and CLASSROOM routes; SOLO remains a written specification only.

Essential child question: “When the same number of things is arranged in a completely different way, can we still notice the quantity without counting one by one?”

True academic target: Recognize small number quantities as a whole or perceptual subgroups across different unfamiliar layouts, before explicit serial counting, and explain an observation when possible. A correct matching decision, count after clicking all dots, quick guess, a familiar domino face or an accurate typed numeral does not establish the no-count action.

Time: approximately 18–23 minutes adult-led; flexible breaks and a genuine later-date follow-up. No stopwatch, flashing cards, ranking or forced speech. Adults should prepare new physical arrangements when earlier pictures are familiar.

Academic fairness: PK3 trials across 1,2,3; PK4 across 1–6, including 4–6. A reduced sample (only 5 and 6) is partial evidence, not full-range PK4 proficiency. Accept speech, home language, sign, AAC, pointing to original unnumbered group configurations, or a child-directed motor proxy; record access adaptations. Visible counting or a child saying “I counted” is legitimate counting evidence but not documented independent subitizing.

B. Four separate task phases and true later revisit

ENTRY E-COMP — what does the child already notice?

PK3: Present two safe equal large groups, each 2, but one arranged as diagonal and the other as a bent offset pair; then briefly show another single unfamiliar 3 in a loose acute triangle. PK4: Two previously unseen groups of 4 and 5, both irregular and not dice-like, plus a single unfamiliar 6 grouped as two lopsided triples. Don't show source answers or draw linking arrows.

Say to child: “Do you think these two groups have the same number, or different numbers? What number do you notice on the first? What about the second?” Capture first quantities separately, not just same/different. Only after child first responds ask “How did you notice?” Observe serial count if overt, volunteered grouping, initial answer, access/hints. If child points at two items and serial-counts, that's a valid alternate strategy but not proof of instantly recognizing without counting.

MODEL M-COMP — contrast quantity and physical arrangement

Use different two-token patterns PK3: two in a short line vs two stacked far apart; separately show two vs three different object groups as a deliberate near-one contrast. For PK4 show four as two near two on one mat and as an irregular scattered four on the other, then five vs six where six has two groups of three. Say, “Changing the pattern doesn't create another object. I can notice this whole small group still has the same number. When one new object is added, the group number changes.” Adult demonstrates perceptual grouping (e.g., two next to two) without giving a word for every object. Adult words in model are not independent source performance and are not an automatic conservation-of-number, addition or decomposition standard.

DURING D-COMP — investigation and responsive teaching

PK3 practice includes new pairs 1 vs 1, 2 vs 3, 3 vs 3 in noncanonical layouts. PK4 practice includes new pairs 4 vs 4, 5 vs 6, 6 vs 6, plus smaller 1–3 pairs if not yet observed. Swap card placements so “left has more” is not a positional rule and identical shape never reveals same answer.

Teacher first asks: “What number do you notice on each side? Same number or different? What did you see?” Child may manipulate original large neutral tokens after the first observation to explore why a different spatial arrangement doesn't change quantity. If child says “same” because the shapes look alike, present two same-shaped but different-size sets as a new model and show the difference; do not imply child knew the amounts. If child individually counts every dot, validate the count and explain grouped perception on a different card, then offer a fresh unmodeled practice pair, marking conceptual support H.

EXIT X-COMP — genuine no-cue novel transfer

Put away all prior arrays. Use single new unseen physical groups as the source-aligned independent core exit, not a pair comparison alone: PK3 one, two and three objects in individually unfamiliar arrangements; PK4 all one through six in six new arrangements, with six irregular and not a memorized die pattern. Use varied nonsequential order to prevent guessing the next number. Say only “What number do you notice?” for each before any hint/count. Record first number, array ID, observable serial count/no visible count, optional self-reported grouping, response mode and support separately. After each first answer ask “How did you notice?” without retroactively upgrading a guess. A subsequent same/different pair challenge is optional teaching extension, not a substitute for the independent fresh single-set exit.

PK4 full-range caveat: If only 4–6 can be observed this day because of fatigue, record PARTIAL_SOURCE_RANGE; return on another actual session for missing 1–3 observations without treating supported prior pairs as unmodeled exit. Never claim global mastery from these short internal tasks.

REVISIT R-COMP — actual later meeting, genuinely new material

At a future real date use physically different safe objects (large washable buttons only if truly non-swallowable; prefer blocks), new irregular groups up to 3 or 6, and ask “What number do you notice in the group?” Offer a second different layout of same amount after first response to explore transfer. No adult names quantity first. Record date/first response/method/support; if never performed, NOT_OBSERVED, no invented long-term growth.

C. Complete adult-led one-child script and teaching moves

0–4 minutes ENTRY: Adult silently sets E-COMP pair and single new target. “What number do you notice on each? Do you think the two have the same number? How do you know?” Wait and record the quantities first and same/different separately; do not start counting aloud or sort child into a “fast thinker” group.

4–8 minutes MODEL: Clear entry. Show M-COMP unfamiliar objects, explain equal quantity despite shape relocation, then close near-one comparison with one added/removed object. A child who notices quantity without serial count may explain freely; child who counts slowly is not penalized. Adult identifies small subgroups on a different practice pattern, avoids pre-telling exit answer.

8–15 minutes DURING: Rotate D-COMP same/different and near-one pairs. Child chooses which pair to compare and whether to speak/sign/AAC. If child says all same because colors match, use different-color equal quantity and same-color unequal quantity new examples: color or shape is not the number. If child says changed placement means “more,” ask whether any object was actually added, then allow deliberate after-first-answer verification. When model/help reveals count, mark subsequent attempt H, not independent source knowledge. Stop/simplify when interest fades.

15–21 minutes EXIT: Present new single-group sets X-COMP (PK3 1–3, PK4 1–6), not previously paired or seen. No hints, flashing, time pressure, or serial-count markers. Record each first quantity and whether a method was observed. If the child counts one by one and gets the right total, say “You counted carefully; that's one useful way. Let's see another kind of arrangement next time,” without marking source subitizing mastered. If strategy cannot be inferred, S1/STRATEGY_UNKNOWN, not S2.

21–23 minutes close: Optionally child rearranges the same two/three/four pieces while adult notices whether amount stayed; no compulsory extension. A separate later R-COMP is actually observed only when it happens.

D. Four distinct written teaching routes

SOLO — comparison app instructional specification, NOT IMPLEMENTED

Future independent child app needs original replayable narration with captions/non-reading/assistive options, large isolated groups and no speed score. ENTRY shows two unfamiliar patterns (visual/tactile alternative), allows child to separately select a quantity for each using neutral spoken options, then same/different (before adult supplies an answer). MODEL explains shape-variation equality vs one-new-piece difference using perceptual subgroup overlays only after first-response diagnostics. DURING lets child actively rearrange identical physical-looking digital pieces and compare new original sets without count highlight; allow counting as a valid different strategy but don't mislabel it subitizing. EXIT switches from pair matching to new single-set quantities PK3 1–3 / PK4 1–6 in novel arrangements, first number before feedback, candidate perceptual strategy if child-origin grouping is indicated, strategy unknown if only tapped a correct number. REVISIT must be a real different encounter via appropriate authorized system; no fake automated retention. Fully independent narration, access, rule checking and child comprehension unbuilt.

CO-PLAY — two human observers independently contrast the same scene

Child and adult each privately choose quantities for both E-COMP groups before discussing similarities, and only then explain different possible perceptual groupings. For M-COMP adult models a new pair. At D-COMP the child may rearrange physical items into a different layout while the adult hides eyes voluntarily or simply looks away (never blindfold child); each viewer independently responds before maker reveals actual number, and then swaps. For X-COMP adult presents a fresh single array without telling answer; child responds first, adult only then shares. A parent's numeral cue makes the response H. No household app or child data required.

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD — live object comparison with individualized feedback

Use B/C exact script and original printable METHOD_03_COMPARE_MATS.html. Child controls whether comparison starts with smaller/larger perceptual sets, adult changes layout without changing number, and genuine independent source evidence still requires unseen single group quantity responses. Provide safe tactile/non-speech alternatives without pretending a serial tactile count establishes instant visual recognition.

TEACHER-LED CLASSROOM — many participate; individual recognition stays attributable

Illustrative 20 learners: teacher projects two original nonnumbered group windows, prepares four station mats, four safe sets of six large objects (24 total reusable) and 20 private answer slips or AAC channels. Only observed individual first quantity/method may count toward source candidate; chorus or collective “same” never proves 20 children's no-count recognition.

  • 0–4 min: E-COMP pairs projected; everyone has first-response opportunity; 2–3 focus learner verbal/gestural method observations recorded honestly.
  • 4–8 min: M-COMP with grouped perceptual equality despite rearrangement and controlled near-one foil; physical manipulation afterward.
  • 8–15 min: Four groups of five rotate station comparisons, peer view first without revealing partner's count, teacher samples 2–3 further individuals.
  • 15–21 min: New X-COMP single unseen quantity cards; learner privately communicates first amount before peers, teacher records only truly observed response and whether serial counting was apparent. If classroom time allows only some PK4 amounts, source range remains partial; follow-up small groups across later centers for the others.
  • 21–23 min: Optional original arrangement puzzle, safe cleanup; later separate-day R-COMP with fresh objects and actual educator observations.

E. Assessment rubric, misconception repair and inclusion

Candidate source-rubric S2/S1/S0/S? (unvalidated): S2 tentative evidence of correct whole small-set recognition across multiple new layouts with no observed serial counting, plus genuine child-origin explanation when possible, acknowledging hidden strategy uncertainty. S1 some correct answers but uncertain origin, memorized patterns, or partial size coverage. S0 attempted but visibly serially counted for this no-count action or misidentified quantity; not a statement about counting deficiency. S? unobserved/access barrier/opt-out. Record each first quantity, same/different supplemental decision, actual count, layout IDs, strategy observation, after-answer verification, U/H/A/X support, date and source-bound scope. A child who matches “same” correctly without independently naming quantity has not yet provided source evidence.

Responsive cases: - “Same because both are green” → Show same-colored groups with different quantities, then new independent pairing after teaching; not source evidence at first. - “Different because one is stretched wider” → Check actual additions after first observation and contrast moving objects vs adding, then transfer to different unseen set; don't automatically certify conservation. - “Both have six” after visibly counting each → Useful counting; still not no-count subitizing. Offer a novel grouped perceptual example, tag hint support. - Child says five quickly on a die-like arrangement → Try an irregular unseen five, no timer; never equate speed with learning. - Child perceives group in 2+3 and explains five without serial count → candidate conceptual recognition; do not automatically give decomposition or all-range PK4 source credit. - Child cannot see, declines, or uses home language/AAC → Respect access/choice, larger/tactile or longer view, mark ? if cognitive origin unobservable, never infer disability.

A1 universal: non-color-dependent number patterns, fair viewing time, large original objects, quiet/tactile/signed/AAC/home-language response, non-speaking choices, seated participation and adult-directed motor access. A2: begin with smaller actual groups for additional practice, reach full PK4 1–6 only with opportunities, no forced speedy flashes. A3: REVIEW_NOT_COMPLETED—educator reviews individual relevant IEP/IFSP/504, communication and visual/sensory supports before real use; do not diagnose or make sensitive child profiles. Materials adult-prepared, no small unsafe pieces or magnets; no copied publisher media or camera/microphone capture.

Companion facilitator guide 03 — Different layouts, same perceived number

Pair: Lesson 03 for PK3.V.A.4 and PK4.V.A.4. Companion teacher guidance, not fourth counted plan. Objective educator guide retains exact official source wording.

Different teaching mechanism and source-bound exit

Contrasting spatially different equal-quantity or near-one unequal arrays helps the child notice that shape/spacing alone does not determine quantity. The extra same/different comparison is an instructional scaffold, not by itself the original subitizing action. Independently scoring the actual quantity of a FRESH individual unnumbered group without observed serial counting is required for a candidate. Do not infer hidden no-count strategy from fast answer or a correct equality choice.

Material setup and five phases

Adult prepares original distinct unnumbered neutral dot sets, accurate hidden quantity key, high-contrast/tactile accessible versions and safe large washable physical pieces; avoid repeating same die arrangement. Keep ENTRY pair(s) separate from adult MODEL equal/different pair, child DURING familiarization, untouched EXIT SINGLE-group novel arrangements, and later calendar REVISIT. PK3 bounds ≤3; PK4 up to 6, including 1–3. ENTRY (match the paired main plan's actual sets): For PK3, show an unknown equal 2-vs-2 pair in two unlike arrangements, then a separate new 3-object group; for PK4, show an unknown unequal 4-vs-5 pair, then a separate new 6-object group. First ask the child to identify each actual quantity separately, recording the raw answers and any observed serial counting before asking whether the paired groups have the same number. If another truly unseen pair is instructionally helpful, record it as a separately prepared additional task, not the already-defined E-COMP; the MODEL and DURING phases intentionally teach both equal and unequal patterns. Feedback and access differentiation

Adult says “they look the same” and child echoes: don't score child's perceptual quantity; ask new single group later. Child counts five serially: counting is real math but not evidence of subitizing; model a two-and-three grouping on DIFFERENT practice array and re-probe a novel one. Child names four from four-dot familiar die card: try irregular, unfamiliar positions. Child is slower but clearly notices two-and-two: don't impose stopwatch; interpret expression with qualified team input, preserve uncertainty. Sight, hearing or motor limits require careful accessible non-answer-supplying material; no false equivalence.
