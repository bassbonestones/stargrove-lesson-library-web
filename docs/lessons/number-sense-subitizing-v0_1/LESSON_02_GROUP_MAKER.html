Teach Make a Group, Notice a Group

Practical teaching guide. See the technical and facilitator tabs for original card/setup references, later full-range checks and recordkeeping. No child outcomes or functioning solo app are claimed.

What the child is learning

Children take turns making a small arrangement and looking at a different, unfamiliar arrangement. The goal is to notice how many objects are present without deliberately counting them one by one. Making three objects because someone said “make three” is fun design practice, but does not itself demonstrate recognizing the quantity of an unknown group.

Gather and decide where to begin

Use large, safe, unnumbered shapes or blocks, a clear mat and an optional cover so an adult can arrange objects out of the child's sight. Use a different arrangement when switching from child-as-maker to child-as-viewer. Let children respond by speech, sign, home language, aided communication or an access route suited to their needs. Give comfortable viewing time—no timed flash tests.

A child in the 3-year-old group can start by noticing one or two, then explore three and eventually unfamiliar mixes of one to three. A child in the 4-year-old group can revisit one to three before progressing to four, five and six. The age-group maximum is for later source-range checks, not an immediate demand.

Five steps

1. Let the child see an unfamiliar group

Quietly prepare a small new set on a mat and reveal it at a comfortable distance.

Say: “Look at this group. What number do you notice?”

Wait. Observe their first answer, whether they pointed and counted each item, and whether they described a group. Only then ask how they knew. Do not tell them how many objects you placed.

2. Demonstrate on other objects

Put the first arrangement away. Show a different two-object pattern.

Say: “I see two together. I didn't have to touch each one to notice two.”

When ready for a larger example, show a new group of five with a visible cluster of two and three and explain noticing the clusters. You may count afterward to check; your model is not independent child evidence.

3. Let the child create—and then switch roles

Say: “Would you like to make a shape for me to notice?”

The child arranges one, two or more safe pieces, depending on readiness. Comment on their choices without scoring their knowledge from the amount they deliberately built.

Now clear that group. Without telling the child the amount, prepare another fresh pattern privately and show it to the child as the viewer.

Say: “This one is mine. What number do you notice? What helped you see it?”

If they count one by one, accept the counting and teach grouping on a different practice display.

4. Offer a fresh viewer-only opportunity

Clear all earlier sets and secretly make a new, unseen group suitable to the child's current level.

Say: “What number do you notice in this new group?”

Record the first response before any clue. Afterward, ask how they noticed it. A quick correct answer alone cannot establish the child's hidden strategy. Keep adult assistance distinct from the first independent reply.

For later full-range evidence the detailed plan specifies three distinct new amounts (one to three) for the 3-year-old group and six distinct new amounts (one to six) for the 4-year-old group, spread over sessions if needed.

5. Return on another day

Let someone else assemble a genuinely unfamiliar group using large household objects. Invite the child to notice, and record only a real later attempt rather than assuming retention.

Teach Make a Group, Notice a Group with a Class

Classroom teaching pathway for preschool educators working with multiple children. The companion Teach 1-on-1 path serves one adult and one learner. These are educational written designs, not evidence of observed classroom effectiveness. Classroom structure and group size may be adapted to real staffing and children's needs.

What children are learning

Children take turns making a small arrangement and looking at a different, unfamiliar arrangement. The goal is to notice how many objects are present without deliberately counting them one by one. Making three objects because someone said “make three” is fun design practice, but does not itself demonstrate recognizing the quantity of an unknown group.

Prepare the classroom

Arrange four shared mats with large safe shapes, a teacher demonstration and private response choices. Avoid tiny counters or dense visual clutter. The intention is to make and perceive small group arrangements, not to recite a memorized picture or race a stopwatch.

Introduce the idea together

Make a new two- or three-piece group and invite, “What number do you notice when you see these together?” Give everyone a quiet first chance; then explain how you recognized a different teacher-made group without counting one by one.

Give children meaningful practice

Rotate maker and viewer roles in groups of four or five. Makers arrange their own patterns; viewers first notice and communicate their own response before the group discusses it. The educator can join one station to watch several individually attributable first looks and move to other groups in later rounds.

Observe individual learning fairly

When a child is the viewer, record the amount and whether they used whole-group noticing, combined subgroups or serial counting; group agreement does not reveal one child's strategy. A protected unseen pattern at the child's taught size is a stronger independent check than recognizing the same arrangement the teacher modeled.

Include, respond, stop and revisit

Let children choose shape placement and a comfortable sensory or communication route. If peers announce totals too early, treat that round as helped practice, not independent observation. A legitimate new arrangement can be assessed at a later rotation without forcing an entire class into simultaneous private tests.

Teacher's recording rule: Whole-group talk and collaborative practice count as learning opportunities, not automatic proof of every child's independent understanding. Record only the action, context, support and developmental stage actually observed. For children whose independent opportunity did not occur, use not yet observed and schedule a suitable later chance; a planned later-day revisit is not already completed. No child should be deprived of meaningful participation just because the educator cannot assess everyone at once.

Subitizing Lesson 02 — Make a Group, Notice a Group

Required developmental teaching sequence: PK3 foundation → PK4 extension. PK3: notice 1–2, then unfamiliar 3, then mixed 1–3; PK4: revisit 1–3, then new 4, then 5–6, then mixed 1–6. Never replace no-serial-count evidence with speed or correct serial counting. The existing full-grade EXIT is a later source-range check, not the first teaching session. Apply this ordered sequence in SOLO, CO-PLAY, ADULT-LED and CLASSROOM routes; SOLO remains a written specification only.

ID: MATH-PK-SUB-02 • 2026-10-30 • Original theme-neutral, INTERNAL DRAFT ONLY. Distinct from Lesson 01's prepared dot-card observation: this method uses active physical construction, conceal-and-reveal, and learner-as-observer role reversal. Constructing a correct group by counting is an instructional activity, not automatically a demonstration of subitizing. The published source action is only supported by the learner noticing a different, unfamiliar group before serially counting each item, with uncertainty documented.

A. Real objectives, materials and teaching boundaries

Official sources: Texas 2022 Prekindergarten PK3.V.A.4 — recognizes quantities up to 3 without counting, official cell SHA 2bcfc37ee04f8c77b148bbc6a7315447a7c65912bb38d358953e678b0fa7fbdf; PK4.V.A.4 — recognizes quantities up to 6 without counting, SHA 708f91809299d7f817915c5c0754ee5761eeba72dbae589df192ec52f25697ea. Full publication: https://tea.texas.gov/academics/early-childhood-education/2022-texas-pkg-comprehensive-guide.pdf . Exact extracted text and hashes are preserved in the local EDUCATION_TX_MATH_SOURCE_OUTCOME_INVENTORY_V0_1.csv and EDUCATION_E2_PK3_PK4_TEN_DOMAIN_SOURCE_FOUNDATION_V0_1.csv (source extraction review still pending).

Central learning question: “When someone else makes a small group in a new shape, can you notice how many there are together without needing to count one at a time?”

Time/pacing: About 18–22 minutes for adult one-on-one, with a genuinely different-day 5–7 minute revisit. No flash timing, stopwatch, speed pressure or compulsory speech. A quick guessed answer does not establish a hidden mental method.

Original materials: METHOD_02_GROUP_MAKER_MAT.html is an original blank two-zone table/floor mat, unnumbered. Adult prepares ten large non-swallowable washable discs/blocks, a simple opaque ordinary adult-held cover (not worn, not a blindfold; can omit if access requires) to keep a layout hidden until reveal, optional child-selected AAC/sign/home-language quantity responses, and a separate adult-only observation card. No small loose buttons/magnets or risky sensory exposure. For each trial use a different set/placement layout; no object has a written numeral/quantity marking that would cue the count.

Two different roles with different academic evidence: - MAKER: may build a collection by counting or by choosing a pattern, explain its design and ask viewer to notice it. This teaches structure and agency but is not independent evidence they can subitize the total. - VIEWER: confronts an unseen genuinely new group, communicates first perceived quantity before overt serial count, optionally explains “I saw two over here and one there,” and may afterward count to check. Only viewer responses are candidates for the source action; observation cannot prove an unspoken internal cognitive route.

Scope of claims: PK3 tasks cover one/two/three; PK4 includes one/two/three and four/five/six when feasible. Never infer complete PK4 source coverage from showing only grouped five or six. Grouping into 2+3 is an instructional scaffold, not automatically decomposing numbers, one-to-one counting, numeral recognition, or future-grade addition evidence.

C. Full adult-led one-child instructional script

0–4 min — Entry before teaching. Arrange covered E-MAKE privately. Put the cover away and allow a fair comfortable visual/tactile presentation. “What number do you notice?” The adult waits, notes the first answer, any visible one-by-one tracking, and any actual child-described grouping. Only then ask how they knew. Do not praise response speed or diagnose a slower response.

4–7 min — Model seeing the whole. Remove E-MAKE, create M-MAKE with different pieces and positions. Adult illustrates how two dots can be noticed together; at PK4, compare two objects in one cluster and three in another to see five as a structured whole, not as five tap/click events. Say “Counting them one at a time is also useful, but today we're noticing how whole little groups can look.” If child counts during model, that's a reasonable way to verify later.

7–14 min — Create, conceal, exchange. First child chooses maker role, designs a small group with supported number and free arrangement; parent can physically place under child instruction, but do not claim this makes subitizing observable. Adult describes child's artistic pattern without stating it makes a source pass. Swap roles; adult creates a novel unseen arrangement as D-MAKE, then child views and gives first perceived quantity. If child quickly says an incorrect number, reply “What group did you notice?” and on a different practice set show 2+1 or 3+2 as appropriate, then invite another new example. This correction is H. If child correctly serial-counts, acknowledge the mathematics but preserve subitizing status as uncertain or not shown for that trial.

14–20 min — New unmodeled viewer exit. Put away known material; child no longer the maker. Adult prepares X-MAKE groups with new, unannounced quantity/placement configurations using safe objects (which may be the same reusable type as earlier stages). For PK3 three novel amounts 1–3; for PK4 a full 1–6 candidate requires six novel groups—offer across short sessions if needed, logging each actual amount separately. “What number do you notice?” only. Keep count hidden. After first choice record whether serial counting occurred and whether a child-originated perceptual pattern was described. Validate effort without misleading “you mastered it” labels.

20–22 min — Reflection and later plan. Invite child to design a surprising optional pattern for the adult, or stop. Later date only use R-MAKE new material; never treat same-day repetition as retention.

D. Four truly different instructional routes

SOLO — planned child-only maker/viewer app, NOT IMPLEMENTED

An accessible future offline program must have independently playable original narration (plus nonaudio equivalent), no required reading or oral speech, large unnumbered objects, tap/move alternatives and explicit break/not-sure/replay. At ENTRY present one novel E-MAKE (randomized original group) and let child choose a spoken quantity option without correct-answer highlighting, record first response and any optional child-originated grouping action. At MODEL, show separate two and PK4 five as two perceptually grouped subsets, no initial serial count animation. At DURING, child freely places safe digital pieces as MAKER, then the app switches to a different unseen layout that the child did not build as VIEWER. At EXIT, only new unseen object patterns and randomized quantities 1–3 / 1–6 appear; record first response before feedback, grouping vs serial taps and uncertainty, not just correctness. Any counting animation/help is tagged supported. REVISIT requires real later session, appropriate permitted storage or parent-assisted offline note; synthetic timers do not establish memory. This implementation and child usability remain unbuilt, so no parity or source-grade achievement claim.

CO-PLAY — child leads maker, adult responds, then adult makes genuinely unseen puzzle

Start with caregiver's unseen E-MAKE; child privately notes first perceived quantity before adult names it. Adult models M-MAKE on a different group. During practice, child chooses original group shape/position (MAKER) and adult honestly describes what they see; child may change it and hear whether the perceived group changed, without presuming adult's perceptions prove child's learning. Adult then builds an unseen D-MAKE without showing its amount and child takes VIEWER role, names the quantity and describes possible grouping. At EXIT the adult must not supply any pre-knowledge or praise-cue; child observes fresh X-MAKE first and retains ownership of answer. Parent records help and access; if they reveal count early, mark H and choose a different independent trial later.

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD — full physical construction route

Use C script: child makes one group to explore pattern design, adult models a distinct perceptual group, then adult offers an unrelated hidden group to child as viewer. A child-directed adult motor proxy is an access aid but cannot choose quantity or count strategy for child. Avoid non-mathematical performance demands; no memorized dot arrangements. Record each blind new viewer response separately.

TEACHER-LED CLASSROOM — rotate maker/viewer and preserve individual evidence honesty

Illustrative 20 children: teacher prepares four reusable large blank mats, four adult-prepared packs of six large safe shapes, one large model, and 20 private accessible answer slips/AAC routes, not 20 expensive full prop kits if group rotations suffice. No child need leave seat, and no shared group-created pattern counts as 20 individual source observations.

  • 0–4 min: On projected/unlabeled teacher E-MAKE, all learners may give a private response, but teacher only records 2–3 individual focus learner first answers/methods actually heard or observed.
  • 4–7 min: Explicit teacher M-MAKE perceptual groups and modeling, without stopwatch or mandatory eye contact.
  • 7–14 min: Four groups of five rotate maker and viewer roles with shared large props. Group peers may discuss how to build, but a child's independent VIEWER response must precede group discussion; if peers disclose count first, mark helped.
  • 14–20 min: Novel X-MAKE single groups are projected or offered in small groups at a fair pace. Each child gets first independent chance at unfamiliar source-relevant amounts; teacher captures only individually observable methods, with later small groups for the others and accurate record of which sizes were actually presented.
  • 20–22 min: Choice of optional creative display and normal class cleanup; schedule separate date R-MAKE with new real objects. Twenty raised correct cards do not prove twenty no-count perceptual strategies.

E. Precise assessment and responsive adjustments

Observation rubric S2/S1/S0/S? (original, unvalidated): S2 = across several unfamiliar patterns the child accurately identifies quantity with no observed serial counting and plausible child-origin perceptual explanation/grouping, but hidden internal cognition remains uncertain → candidate evidence, never automatic mastery; S1 = partial or uncertain strategy/possible guessing/rehearsal; S0 = observable serial count, clear incorrect attempted perception (not an indictment of counting skill); S? = no usable response/unknown due access. Record separate actual quantities/layouts, first vs coached reply, observed strategy versus speculative, U unprompted / H concept hint / A access-only / X uncertain, time/date and next teaching move. A correct number after counting is not the same S2 subitizing evidence.

Specific instructional feedback:

  • Child built five correctly by placing one at a time → Great maker reasoning; not source evidence yet. Offer novel viewer group; don't recycle their same layout.
  • Child counts an unseen six meticulously → Accept correct counting; teach noticing grouped 3+3 on a different example; recorded exit trial not independent subitizing.
  • Child guesses a familiar 5-dot pattern → Show another irregular 5 with different spacing; a memorized shape alone insufficient.
  • Child says “I saw two and two” on an unfamiliar four → Note volunteered grouping; on a new four-spot array see whether perceptual noticing generalizes. Not automatically PK4 decomposition.
  • Child cannot see distant poster or needs tactile access → Use close, high-contrast oversized objects/raised safe dots or communication support; record presentation changes and retain meaningful learning, don't impose short flashes or infer disability.
  • Child says “I don't know” or declines a turn → Offer another day/card or pause and label ?, not a compulsory fail.
  • Child names different total after an adult count hint → Record original answer, H retry and unresolved independent recognition; never overwrite.

A1 universal accessibility: large stable high-contrast and tactile objects, flexible viewing time/distance, home language, sign/AAC, non-speech and child-directed motor proxy, no imposed fast movements or forced emotions, child-controlled choice and break. A2 specific: smaller actual sets for practice (PK3 one/two; PK4 may begin 1–3) then progress to source upper bounds only when useful; real 1–6 coverage cannot be declared from only 4–6. A3 individualized plan: REVIEW_NOT_COMPLETED—the actual educator must consult applicable IEP/IFSP/Section 504, sensory, motor and communication plans before real deployment. Avoid high-stakes disability/placement inference.

Risks/rights: Adult cuts any paper; all pieces large, washable, stable and non-swallowable. No blindfold, flash timer, cameras, speech recordings, child identity, external artwork or copied vendor curriculum. Real printer and specialist accessibility review PENDING.

Companion facilitator guide 02 — Maker and unfamiliar viewer

Pair: Lesson 02, PK3.V.A.4 / PK4.V.A.4. Not a counted fourth lesson; see objective guide.

Pedagogy: agency does not equal subitizing

The learner builds an original small quantity and discovers how patterns can be designed, then takes a genuinely different VIEWER role on a new hidden adult-created layout. Building with serial counting may be meaningful mathematics, not independent evidence of perceiving an unknown group without serial count. Do not score learner's own remembered amount as fresh subitizing. PK3 covers unseen 1–3 patterns, PK4 unseen 1–6 including real six and not merely 5–6.

Preparation and scripted transitions

Prepare a safe mat, large washable nonchoking tokens, ordinary optional opaque cover (never blindfold), child-chosen accessible language, and distinct adult-only original material. Separate ENTRY unknown arrangement, MODEL different 2/5 grouped objects, DURING child maker construction THEN a different unknown viewer group, untouched EXIT multiple new arrays, and a later-date REVISIT on distinct physical objects. No numerals/teacher answer colored cues visible. Novelty is about an unseen, unannounced total and spatial configuration—not a brand-new object material for every trial. Safely reusable identical large blocks/discs may be used for successive different groups; do not show a viewer their own earlier maker arrangement, and do not treat a mere rotated familiar constellation as genuinely novel. Vary object types over time when it supports transfer, not as a mandatory per-trial resource burden. Record which quantities/layouts were actually unseen. ENTRY: Show never-seen adult-made group and ask first perceived quantity; record observable count behavior and uncertainty. MODEL: on separate objects say “I see these two together” or PK4 “I see two and three together as five,” not item-by-item counting animation. DURING: Let child construct safe group using any legitimate strategy (even counting), then remove and hide it. Adult creates a completely new, not copied pattern as learner becomes VIEWER. Child gives first own quantity before revealing answer. Fade grouping outlines, count words, adult-preselected clusters; if child merely remembers maker quantity, change arrangement and amount. EXIT: New independent VIEWER arrays PK3 1,2,3 or PK4 1–6, counterbalanced and not familiar/previously created. Record each first quantity, overt serial tracking, possible grouping strategy, access and help; construction not scored as source. REVISIT: New actual date and new unknown pattern, NOT_OBSERVED.

Diagnosis, uncertainty and safe access

Maker counts 5 to build: celebrate number construction separately but no viewer-source claim. Viewer remembers a recognizable arrangement: avoid familiar dice and old cards. Viewer serial counts: model perceptual subgroups on another set without speed pressure, then different unmodeled probe. Viewer is correct with no observable counting: source-action candidate, not certainty about hidden cognition. Child cannot see/access array: adapt safe large high contrast/raised shapes and consult qualified team; if sequential tactile counting is needed, do not imply it is equivalent visual instantaneous recognition.

Seven differentiated needs and four routes

Attention/executive: clear “maker/viewer” voluntary role markers, fade them; language: own meaningful sign/AAC/home-language quantity; literacy: no reading/count label; numeracy: perceptual grouping after building, don't conflate methods; autism/sensory: optional cover, no blindfold/surprise or timer; intellectual/developmental: one/two novelty and gradual bigger arrays; physical/motor/hearing/vision: child-directed neutral token placement plus unbiased sensory access, record uncertainty. SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: virtual new unseen viewer pattern distinct from learner construction and honest first-response log are needed. CO-PLAY: parent constructs truly new unseen array after child maker role. ADULT-LED: observe strategy before teacher verification. CLASSROOM: peer-built pattern seen by learner is practice, not source EXIT; use fresh individual cards.
