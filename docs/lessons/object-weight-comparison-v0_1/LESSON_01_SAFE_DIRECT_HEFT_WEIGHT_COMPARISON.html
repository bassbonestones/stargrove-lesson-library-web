Comparing Object Weights · REVIEWED LESSON 01

Safe hands-on weight comparison

Preschool instructional-design materials · PK3 & PK4

PK3.V.D.3PK4.V.D.3
Written design reviewedNo classroom outcomes implied

Compare how heavy two objects feel

One adult and one child

Help your child notice that objects can weigh different amounts and compare them safely. Start wherever their curiosity is. Younger children may show that weights differ and can be compared; older preschoolers can recognize weight and make a valid comparison. These are learning directions, not entry tests.

Get ready

Choose two large, intact, smooth, lightweight objects that this child can comfortably support just above a low resting surface. A suitable foam block and solid classroom block may work, but inspect the actual items and your setting's safety rules. Avoid detachable parts, magnets, batteries, splinters, breakables and homemade loose-fill bags. No body weighing or strength competitions. Privately check that the weight difference is clear. You need no numerical scale, four sets of objects or worksheet. Use a mat and leave space to set each object down.

Respect the child's movement plan. Ask before handing over an object; do not put weight onto unwilling hands. Offer watching or stopping. Adult-safe does not automatically mean child-safe.

Follow a question

“Would you like to compare how these feel?” Let the child choose among approved items and whether to touch, watch or pause. Compare low over the surface, with a gentle lift or comfortable support, setting each down between turns. Keep posture/support comparable and rest as needed. No carrying objects around.

Ask “What do you notice?” A prediction based on looks is a useful starting idea. If needed, show how you support one then the other: “This feels heavier to me. I'm checking weight, not just size.” Use the same objects if practical; record that you supplied the result. Your demonstration is teaching, not evidence that the child discovered the comparison.

Notice and respond

Let your child try and communicate by gesture, home language, sign, speech or communication aid. A point to the blue block can mean “that one is heavier” when the question makes the meaning clear. If uncertain, ask “Show me what you mean,” not “Say heavier.” Do not require a sentence or an unfamiliar pair.

If the answer surprises you, check the actual weights, fatigue, grip and support before assuming misunderstanding. Rest or simplify. Model again when useful and let the child respond without pressure. If the adult or another person announced the winner, describe the response as supported practice rather than independent evidence.

A neutral grip or communication adjustment is welcome even if you offered it first. But if you carry most of one object's load, the child's feeling cannot fairly compare it. Use another comfortable method or defer. A suitable safe guarded balance is an optional different route: check empty level, stable free pans and a settled result the child can actually perceive. Do not tell the winner and call it the child's own judgment. No balance purchase or improvised device is required.

Finish well

Stop at discomfort, declining interest or a satisfying discovery. “You checked both and showed me which felt heavier.” Keep the claim as small as what you saw. A private note can record pair, action/words, help, access and what might be useful next. An unseen or ambiguous response is not a failure. An adult check of a balance does not mean the child used it. Note that the balance was prepared but unused if the child safely compared by hand; there is no second child attempt to invent. If accounts disagree about actual use, keep both and leave the comparison unresolved. If you switch methods, note each attempt separately: which method the child used and what it showed. A response from an obstructed or unsettled balance is not a hand comparison. Keep an actually separate safe hand comparison on its own. Neutral help and answer cues are different. Identify separately whether an adult or another child supplied the answer for this attempt; ordinary conversation about something else is not a cue. Keep the child’s actual action even when someone already gave the answer. If you are unsure whether anyone gave away the answer, or which method the child used, keep that uncertainty rather than assuming no cue or a hand comparison. If you saw a child act or show a relation without speaking, record that contribution. When notes about one attempt disagree, keep both accounts. If one says no stop and the only reported stop clearly came after an otherwise supported, completed comparison, keep that earlier result and do not reoffer. If a stop might have preceded the response, leave its interpretation unresolved. If the child declines or asks to stop, end that attempt and do not reoffer it to get a result. Pause immediately if a gesture may mean stop. If you note the attempt, distinguish yes, no and unsure about whether a stop happened. For a known or possible stop, record whether it is confirmed in this attempt, confirmed in another, or unclear, and note timing if known. If the only possible stop is confirmed to concern another attempt or to come after a completed comparison, keep the otherwise valid comparison while still pausing. Do not guess the scope. Leave the interpretation unresolved if a possible stop may have come first. Do not count a response after that stop. Keep an already completed genuine comparison separate.

Return another time if your child wants to compare something else or you need clearer evidence. Familiar objects are fine. Different objects may help explore whether size predicts weight; equality and tricky-size pairs are optional extensions. Do not turn a successful familiar comparison into a failure for lacking novelty. Later learning and retention remain unknown until observed.

What this does and does not show

Texas PK3.V.D.3 concerns understanding that object weights vary and can be compared. PK4.V.D.3 concerns recognizing and comparing weights. Neither requires grams, a timed test, exact equality or weighing people. This guide supports real object exploration; a picture cannot supply actual weight evidence. It is a written design, not proven classroom effectiveness or mastery.

Compare weights together, one safe turn at a time

One teacher with a class

Children explore how actual objects feel to support and compare weight, rather than assuming the biggest object is heaviest. PK3 children can show that weights vary and can be compared; PK4 children can recognize weight and make a valid comparison. Do not require all learners to begin with the same prior experience.

Prepare one attended comparison spot

Use two large smooth intact lightweight objects, a low stable surface and mat beside you, within your normal whole-group sightline. Check actual comfort and safe handling for the participating learner, not only your own strength. A suitable foam block and solid classroom block are examples, not guaranteed safe products. Reject loose or detachable parts, magnets, batteries, cracks, splinters, breakables and homemade filled bags. Privately confirm a clear comfortable contrast. No numerical weighing or multiple object sets are necessary.

Do not set out unattended weight stations. Keep unused items with you. Other children may watch, discuss or continue an already familiar safe activity that you can genuinely supervise. If that arrangement is not safe with your actual group, conduct only the brief shared demonstration and defer individual handling. This plan never assumes a second adult.

Invite curiosity without a chorus test

“Could these feel different to hold? How might we check?” Listen to different ideas. A group answer or a child's prediction from appearance is not individual demonstrated comparison. Model a gentle low support, rest and comparison if helpful: “I am checking how heavy each feels.” Explain the relation you feel as teaching, without pretending later echoes are independent discoveries.

Offer one or a few voluntary turns when supervision permits. The child can choose an approved pair, watch first, ask for help, decline or stop. Keep loads just above the resting surface, away from feet, never overhead; do not pass weights around a crowded circle. Attend to the whole group and put materials aside immediately if another child needs you.

Make each contribution visible

Ask the participating child what they notice while they safely compare. Accept meaningful pointing, gesture, sign, home language, speech or a communication aid. “Blue one” may answer “Which feels heavier?” correctly. Clarify neutrally if meaning is uncertain. Invite peers to share questions, not rate the child or compete to be strongest.

If a peer has already announced the answer, keep the shared learning but note the cue. Do not demand a hidden test in an unsupervised corner. A later natural opportunity without that cue can clarify ownership; familiar materials are allowed. Record only children you actually observed. Spread turns over days instead of claiming that everyone demonstrated the outcome in one sitting.

Respond without forcing

For an unexpected comparison, check weight contrast, fatigue, grip, unequal adult support and communication before reteaching. Rest, offer a clearer safe comparison or model again. Conceptual help is useful instruction, not an unprompted child judgment.

Offer neutral access proactively; the child need not independently request it. If you support nearly all the weight, do not attribute your sensation to the child. A safe guarded commercial balance can offer a different comparison route only if its actual settled relation is accessible to the child. Check empty level, stability, no pinch risk, free pans and no contact; do not improvise a hanging beam. If no safe perceptible route is available today, defer that observation and arrange one, not a deficit label.

Close and plan the next opportunity

End at fatigue, reduced interest or a useful discovery. Briefly describe what children actually did; do not rank them. Keep private notes by child code: pair, actual action/words, relation, assistance or peer cue, access and next useful invitation. Worksheets are optional. Unobserved learners stay unobserved. If accounts disagree about actual use, keep both and leave the comparison unresolved. If you switch methods, note each attempt separately: which method the child used and what it showed. A response from an obstructed or unsettled balance is not a hand comparison. Keep an actually separate safe hand comparison on its own. Neutral help and answer cues are different. Record separately whether an adult or a peer supplied this attempt’s answer. An unrelated remark is not a cue. Keep the learner’s real action and neutral help, but don’t call the answer independent if a peer supplied it. If you are unsure whether anyone gave away the answer, or which method the child used, keep that uncertainty rather than assuming no cue or a hand comparison. If you saw a child act or show a relation without speaking, record that contribution. When notes about one attempt disagree, keep both accounts. If one says no stop and the only reported stop clearly came after an otherwise supported, completed comparison, keep that earlier result and do not reoffer. On another occasion invite another safe comparison if useful. Exact equality, deceptive-size puzzles, new objects and independent choice of the weight dimension are optional extensions, not official prerequisites. You can name the dimension; the child's observed contribution must still establish the claimed relation rather than echo your answer.

Safe hands-on weight comparison

Purpose and official source

Help a child discover that real objects can weigh different amounts and compare how heavy they feel, without judging by size alone. This is a written instructional design, not an observed child study. The historical DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md is not this candidate’s teaching route and is excluded from its delivered resources. The optional branches below replace its fixed-stage assumptions for Lesson 01.

The 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, Mathematics, printed page 56 (PDF page 56), places these outcomes in separate columns: - PK3.V.D.3: “Child understands that weights of objects can vary and be compared.” Research-cell SHA: 931b1d91ef0238fbf7bf842eaa50c26dbb887fee414c051e406220e0167ab247. - PK4.V.D.3: “Child recognizes and compares weights of objects.” Research-cell SHA: 8282e9b400319c075dfc6d53e778e5743916c9b0e42135934ebf1e2111ef5d6e.

The publisher gives hand comparison and weight vocabulary as examples, not compulsory tests. No official requirement mandates novel objects, an exact number of trials, independently choosing the dimension, misleading-size puzzles, equality judgments, grams, a balance, or comparing children's bodies. Different objects can help investigate a suspected memorized answer; they are not an automatic admission gate. Reading the original PDF's two columns confirms the targets, not the effectiveness of this lesson.

PK3 evidence: a child meaningfully shows that object weights may differ and can be compared, perhaps trying both and showing a difference. Do not require accurate ranking of every pair. PK4 evidence: a child recognizes weight as the dimension and makes a valid actual comparison. A teacher may name the dimension or invite comparison; the child must contribute the observed relation, not merely echo an announced winner. Familiar objects, home language, sign, pointing and augmentative communication can all provide relevant evidence.

Choose a useful invitation

During voluntary building or tidy-up, a child may notice that two similarly sized objects feel different. Follow that question: “Would you like to find out how these feel to hold?” Alternatively invite a brief investigation of two approved objects for a pretend delivery. Do not require a storyline, forced surprise, speed, competition, or public performance. Carrying is pretend; comparisons stay at the low surface. Let the child select among safe items, request help, predict, change a prediction, watch or stop.

Prepare only what is necessary

For one adult and one child, start with two intact large smooth lightweight objects, one low stable table or floor mat, and room to set each down safely. Examples are two appropriately sized solid classroom blocks of different materials, or a large intact foam block and a manageable solid wooden block. These are examples, not automatically safe approved products. Inspect each for cracks, detachable parts, sharp edges, choking hazards, splinters, dirt, insecure coatings, magnets or batteries. No loose-fill homemade containers, beanbags, food-filled bags, breakable objects, heavy weights or openable filled bottles. Follow local safety policy and the child's existing movement restrictions. Clean shared materials normally.

Privately test both objects by comparable gentle support and confirm that the weight difference is clear with the intended method and comfortable for the particular child. If uncertain, choose a clearer pair or verify with a suitable safe balance. Numerical weighing is not required. An adult's ability to lift something does not establish a child's comfort: offer touch, watch, or decline first; never place a load on unwilling hands. Keep objects just above the resting surface, away from toes, not at shoulder height. Stop at discomfort, fatigue, uncertainty or unsafe play. If a child declines or signals stop, end the attempt immediately. Pause immediately if a possible stop signal is unclear. Do not reoffer a comparison to get a result.

The same pair can support invitation, model and practice. For a check of a relation just announced by an adult, record the prompt history rather than calling an echo independent evidence. An optional third object helps investigate a cue or offer further curiosity. There is no four-set materials requirement. Equal-weight or opposite-size pairs are optional extensions, not mandatory supplies.

An adult may offer or arrange neutral motor/communication support without requiring the child to independently request it. Support must not covertly choose the conclusion. If adult support carries most of an object's weight, the child's hand sensation cannot fairly establish relative heaviness. Instead use a comfortable comparable method or a safe guarded equal-arm balance whose movement is accessible to the child. Do not simply tell the child what the adult feels and label that direct sensory evidence. A balance route can show source-level comparison, but is a different mechanism from direct heft. Record that distinction.

For a balance: use an appropriate stable commercial device, no exposed pinch point, no overload, no improvised hanging beam. Adult handles setup; verify empty level, objects supported freely and pans settled without contact. Let the learner observe the relation visually or through an actually available safe accessible indicator. Do not invent tactile hardware or assume a verbal description of the winner is neutral access. If no safe perceptible comparison is available, defer that observation and arrange an accessible opportunity, not a failure label.

A responsive teaching sequence

ENTRY: Invite a safe comparison and listen. “What do you notice about how these feel?” The child may already show the source action. Do not erase that evidence because it came before a model or used familiar materials. A guess from appearance is a prediction worth testing, not wrongdoing.

MODEL when useful: With the same pair for teaching or another pair if available, show a small low lift and rest: “I support this one, set it down, then this one. This one feels heavier to me. I am checking weight, not just looking at size.” Do not infer the child has learned from watching. If naming the result, record that later matching answers may reflect the model.

DURING: Let the child compare comfortably, with rests and comparable support. “What did you notice?” A child can indicate an object while communicating a clear comparative meaning. “Blue one” can be a valid response to “Which feels heavier?”; it is not automatically a vocabulary error. Clarify meaning neutrally when needed: “Show me what you mean.” Do not force formal terminology. Describe a genuine action accurately and invite an optional follow-up question the child cares about.

EXIT means stop and summarize, not a mandatory test: Note what the child actually contributed and what help occurred. If the child gives an unexpected relation, first check comfort, actual weights, grip, unequal support, fatigue and interpretation of language. Teach the relation with another demonstration as needed; allow another meaningful response without pressure. Keep supported learning as useful evidence of participation, not an unprompted comparison. Reuse is permitted; whether an independent inference is justified depends on actual answer cues and child reasoning, not merely a fresh object label.

REVISIT: Return when the child is interested or further evidence would inform teaching, on another actual occasion. A changed pair/context may investigate transfer; the source action does not require transfer first. Record later-date retention only if later observed. Here REVISIT is NOT_OBSERVED. Stop after a satisfying exploration, at fatigue or declining interest; no timed completion or compulsory repeated checks.

Optional developmental branches, not a fixed progression

PK3 W3-1: Notice a comfortable clear contrast. PK3 W3-2: Try comparable supports and rest. PK3 W3-3: Explore “feels about the same” if an appropriate pair is available; subtle equality by hand is uncertain, not a conceptual deficit. PK3 W3-4: Apply curiosity to another purposeful pair if wanted. These are planning choices, not prerequisites.

PK4 W4-1: Reconnect to a known weight contrast. PK4 W4-2: Compare a meaningful chosen safe pair. PK4 W4-3: Investigate a size-based prediction with a clear contrast if relevant. PK4 W4-4: Explore approximately equal weight or another perceptible relation without forcing exact equality from hand feel. PK4 W4-5: Try another real context when a transfer question is useful. Accurate comparisons on ordinary familiar pairs remain valid without progressing through all five branches.

One teacher with a class

Use a brief group invitation with the teacher controlling the single pair. Invite ideas without taking chorus responses as individual evidence. Keep one comparison spot beside the teacher and within the normal whole-group sightline. Other learners may observe, discuss or continue the setting's already safe, familiar low-risk activity; do not establish unattended weight stations. Offer one or a few voluntary turns when full-group supervision permits. Do not pass objects around a crowded circle or require a waiting child to hold a load.

Observe the learner's actual handling/communication and note any peer-announced winner. A peer's answer does not erase participation, but may make independent attribution uncertain. Do not isolate children out of the teacher's sight to hide answers. Where appropriate invite a later natural comparison without an answer cue. Spread observations over ordinary sessions; unobserved children remain unobserved. If group needs require full attention, set the objects aside and resume later. No second adult is assumed. Classroom guide gives the specific flow; one-on-one guide provides responsive individual pacing.

Record and interpret without overclaiming

Neutral help does not establish whether someone supplied the answer earlier. Keep relevant earlier or current answer cues separate from what the child actually did. Note separately whether an adult or a peer supplied the actual answer for this attempt; an unrelated peer comment is not a supplied answer. If the method, cue or result is unknown, record it that way, not as absent, safe or incorrect. A short private note is enough: child code/date, actual pair, grade target, comparison mechanism, observed action/words, actual help or peer cues, safety/access confounds, the limited interpretation and next useful invitation. Do not collect medical details or publish child names. Not observed means no child action or relation was observed in that attempt, not merely no spoken answer. Record adult preparation separately from the child’s actual method. A prechecked balance can remain unused; record a verified hand comparison as such without creating a second child attempt. If people disagree about what the child actually used, retain both reports and withhold the comparison claim. Keep conflicting accounts and separate attempts. If one account says no stop and the only stop reported is clearly after an already completed comparison, keep both accounts and pause without reoffering; the earlier comparison stands on either account if otherwise supported. If the disagreement could place a stop before the response, leave its interpretation unresolved. If they declined or stopped before this response, do not count anything that followed as a valid comparison. Keep a genuinely complete earlier comparison if the stop came after it, or applied to a separate attempt. If the only possible stop is verified to concern a different attempt or to come after a completed comparison, keep occurrence unknown and preserve the otherwise valid comparison. Pause without reoffering. If the possible stop could affect this response, hold the interpretation. Paper is optional; drawings of blocks cannot establish the blocks' actual weights. If you try both methods, make separate brief entries tying each response to its own method and conditions. An obstructed or unsettled pan is not valid evidence and must not be counted as a hand comparison; keep any separately observed safe hand comparison on its own.

Codes are optional shorthand: U child-origin relation without answer-bearing help; A child-origin relation with neutral access; H meaningful supported teaching with conceptual help; X attribution uncertain. Do not strip ongoing accessibility to manufacture U. Missing observation, unsafe/incomparable method, uncertain meaning, and an observed inaccurate comparison are different states. A correct choice with only an appearance guess is insufficient to attribute weight reasoning; however the teacher need not demand a verbal justification if the child's action gives clear evidence. Do not diagnose disability, rank children, or claim mastery from a single turn.

The synthetic evidence.py helper only challenges declared records. It does not observe children, establish truth of flags, or replace educational interpretation. Partial PK3 understanding must not be converted into PK4 accuracy; PK4 accuracy must not be demanded for every PK3 case.

Adversarial false-positive and false-rejection checks

False-positive: size prediction happens to be right. Value the hypothesis but invite a real comfortable comparison; do not credit observed heft if none occurred. Optional opposite-size example can expose the shortcut, not become a new compulsory test.

False-positive: fatigue or unequal adult support makes one object feel heavier. Rest, equalize feasible support or change method; do not reward stamina or assume object weight changed.

False-positive: teacher announces the winner, child echoes. Keep as teaching, not child-owned comparison; seek later meaningful evidence only when useful.

False rejection: child points to the blue item accurately after safely comparing. Meaningful pointing is sufficient; no forced sentence or new pair.

False rejection: adult selected the dimension or offered a grip adjustment. Invitation and neutral access are compatible with source action; inspect who supplied the conclusion.

False rejection: child repeats comparison with a familiar block. Familiarity alone does not negate a real comparison. Record cues and evidence rather than enforcing novelty.
