Feelings in a familiar activity

One adult and one child

Help a child recognize and express feelings, or begin to connect a feeling with an action. This is a small conversation during familiar art or reading, not a test of whether a child will share a secret. Some children will want to talk; others will show meaning through a sign, gesture, home language or their usual communication system. Passing is fine.

Get ready

Choose a familiar book, or one sheet of paper and age-safe broad crayons. Keep the child's usual communication aids available. Find a comfortable space with an easy pause option, still in your sight. No printout or second adult is needed. You can use the optional original emotion cards for ideas, but children need not read or fill in anything. Start with a few minutes and follow the child's interest rather than a timer.

1. Join their choice

“Book, drawing, or keep doing what you're doing?” Listen first. “You can tell me something, ask for help, or just enjoy this.” If they ask for help, give it immediately. Do not withhold something or create upset to get a feeling response. A request for help alone does not prove which emotion they feel.

2. Offer a little model

Use a truthful mild example of your own: “I'm pleased looking at this page. I might keep looking or share it.” If it is not your feeling, make it an openly fictional character. You can name both possibilities yourself. The child need not repeat them. “All feelings are okay. We still keep people safe.”

3. Follow their meaning

For a younger learner, welcome a voluntary feeling word, meaningful symbol choice, gesture or sign. Check your understanding gently and accept correction: “You said excited. Tell me more, or keep drawing?” Offer words as options, not assignments. Notice different meaningful emotions across available experiences; one response is not a whole range. You can explore fictional feelings without asking for personal disclosure. Label what you actually observed, rather than saying a character's feelings prove the child's own state.

For a learner beginning to connect feelings and behavior, ask one optional question: “Nia feels pleased with her picture and wants to show it. How might those go together?” A familiar story is fine. A meaningful gesture toward sharing, or a brief answer in context, can show a beginning connection. They do not need a formal explanation or a second alternative. Offer other possibilities for learning: “She could enjoy it quietly too.”

If it gets difficult

If they say quiet means sad, offer “Quiet can mean enjoying something, wanting space, or many other things. We can ask rather than guess.” Never decide a person's feeling from a smile or eye contact. If a child proposes hitting, validate the feeling while stopping harm: “Angry feelings are okay. I won't let anyone get hurt. We can ask for help.” Natural distress means stop the lesson and provide comfort. Follow your setting's safeguarding process for concerning disclosures. Do not promise secrecy.

Maintain communication and physical assistance. Lots of help with access is not the same as supplying the answer. Do not require English, eye contact, a facial performance, new materials or disclosure. Describe pictures, use familiar routines, allow processing time and offer quieter conditions. Accept the child's own meaning, not an adult-chosen answer disguised as their response.

Finish and notice

Stop when the child has had enough, declines or needs care. If welcome, notice one last meaningful response in the same activity. A familiar response can still be genuinely theirs; copying an answer without meaning cannot. Privately note what the child did, the help you provided and what remains unclear. Recognition without expression, or one emotion without range, is useful partial evidence. A beginning feeling–action connection is not a claim of mastery.

Return during another ordinary routine if useful, using familiar or different materials. A later-day observation stays “not observed” until it happens; same-day practice is not retention. Never create sadness or anger just to fill a record. Keep identifying notes out of public documents.

What this does and does not show

PK3.I.B.2.a concerns recognizing and expressing a range of emotions. PK4.I.B.2.a concerns beginning to understand connections between emotions and behaviors. Feelings during our ordinary classroom day

One teacher with several learners

Use familiar art or reading to welcome voluntary feeling communication and beginning emotion–behavior connections. Do not run a public feelings-confession circle. Children may participate, continue their activity, listen or pass. Evidence belongs to the individual child, never automatically to everyone who heard a group answer.

Set up for supervision

Use your normal supervised art/reading area. Each participating child needs a familiar book OR paper and broad age-safe crayons; keep normal communication aids accessible. A pause spot stays in your sight and within the room's supervision arrangements. No second adult, printer, device or special equipment is assumed. Use a group size and layout you already supervise safely. If supervision cannot be maintained, skip individual conversations now and return during routine interactions later.

Plan a brief whole-group invitation, then ordinary activity. You may listen closely to only one or two willing children today; rotate opportunities over days rather than keeping a queue waiting for tests. Do not leave the group to seek a private assessment. Record minimal confidential notes afterward if writing would distract from supervision.

Invite without requiring disclosure

“Some people like to talk while they draw or read; others like quiet. You can share an idea, ask for help, or keep working.” Model with an openly fictional character: “Nia says she feels pleased with her garden picture. She might show it, or enjoy it quietly.” One teacher voices the alternatives. No child must announce a feeling or act out a face. A chorus is a teaching activity, not evidence for each learner.

Follow normal activity

As you scan and circulate, listen for a voluntary contribution. Ask one short optional question when it will not interrupt supervision. With a younger learner, welcome meaningful feeling expression and recognition in home language, sign, gesture, words or their usual communication system. Build varied emotional meanings across genuine opportunities, not a quota of cards, days or public disclosures.

For a learner exploring connections, ask “Nia feels pleased and wants to show her picture. How might those go together?” Or follow their volunteered feeling with “What might you do?” A brief contextually meaningful answer or communication can be enough for a beginning connection. Familiar stories and continuing access assistance are valid. Offer alternatives as teaching, not a mandatory advanced test.

If another child answers for them, do not credit that response to the first child. Give no-pressure space or return later. If the child declines, record no opportunity rather than inability. A meaningful child-chosen symbol may communicate; an adult-chosen tap does not establish the child's meaning.

Respond safely

“Quiet means sad” calls for “People can be quiet for many reasons. We can ask rather than guess.” All feelings are allowed; hurting people is not. If harm is proposed or occurs, stop it, support everyone and follow classroom procedures rather than turning conflict into an assessment. Never induce distress, confiscate favored items or delay help to elicit evidence. Natural distress means comfort and supervision come first. Follow safeguarding policy for concerning disclosures; do not solicit details publicly or promise secrecy.

Maintain visual, tactile, language, sensory and communication access. Do not require eye contact, facial imitation, English or an unfamiliar setting. Children may choose quiet participation. A child who avoids personal disclosure can explore fictional feelings; distinguish that expression from autobiographical self-report rather than refusing the learning opportunity.

End and keep honest individual evidence

End the invitation when interest fades or the room needs your attention. Everyone can continue ordinary activity. Privately record the child's actual contribution, context and support only when feasible. Partial recognition or expression is useful; it does not establish the whole PK3 range. A beginning PK4 connection does not require sophisticated verbal reasoning, naming two alternatives or saying “might.” Do not count group products, copied answers, peer responses or adult interpretation as that child's achievement.

Revisit in another routine, with familiar or different materials, if helpful. Later retention remains unobserved until a genuinely later date. No observation today may simply mean no opportunity. Keep names and private disclosures in approved confidential school records, never the lesson repository or website.

Genuine voluntary feeling expression

Purpose, source and fit

During familiar child-chosen art or books, notice and welcome meaningful emotion communication without requiring a personal disclosure. For PK3, build recognition AND expression of a range of emotions across meaningful examples. For PK4, help the child begin to connect emotions and behaviors. These are separate targets, not a single ladder or a diagnosis. One response is useful partial evidence, not mastery of either age's whole target.

Preparation and choice

One adult and one child need one familiar book OR one sheet of paper and age-safe broad crayons per participating child, a comfortable visible place to pause, and the child's usual communication supports. Offer a real option to continue normal activity instead. No purchased kit, device, printer, second adult, facial performance, or reading ability is needed. Optional ORIGINAL_EMOTION_COMMUNICATION_AIDS.html offers vocabulary and fictional stories; adults read it aloud or describe it accessibly. Optional ORIGINAL_FEELINGS_ACTIONS_EVIDENCE_RECORDS.html is for confidential adult notes, not a child's worksheet. Paper notes work equally well.

Use the child's agreed communication system, home language, sign, gaze, gesture or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC). Keep symbols within reach and wait for that child's usual processing time. Ask family about comfortable feeling words privately when helpful, not as a prerequisite to participation. Never infer a feeling from eye contact, smile, quietness, disability, or culture. Do not remove a favorite object, create conflict, induce upset or delay comfort to generate evidence. A pause area must remain in the responsible adult's sight, not an unsupervised destination.

ENTRY: join a real activity (about one minute)

Invite: “Would you like your book, some drawing, or to keep doing what you're doing?” Join their choice. Say “You can tell me something, ask for help, or just enjoy this.” Listen before supplying a feeling label. A child asking “Help” gets help immediately; the request alone is not proof of an emotion or a connection. Silence, declining or no meaningful opportunity means NOT_OBSERVED, not failure. Spontaneous play may supply the opportunity without this exact invitation.

MODEL: one adult, one small example (under one minute)

Offer a truthful, mild adult-owned example: “I feel pleased looking at this page. I might keep looking, or share it with someone.” If this is not your feeling, use an openly fictional character instead; do not pretend a feeling. One educator can name two possible choices. No second adult is needed. Explain “Feelings are okay. We still keep people safe.” A child need not repeat either phrase. Do not present your answer as the child's evidence.

DURING: respond to meaning, not performance (two to five minutes, flexible)

For PK3, reflect tentatively: “You said excited. Would you like to tell me more, or keep drawing?” Accept correction and silence. Offer useful words without assigning them: “Some people say pleased, unsure, or something else.” An independently chosen meaningful symbol can communicate; a decorative or adult-selected tap does not establish meaning. Support recognition through a benign fictional moment: “Nia says she feels pleased with her garden picture.” Invite noticing a different feeling in a familiar book only when its text or context supports that interpretation. Own-feeling expression is voluntary; a fictional role or story can teach and demonstrate expression without pretending it is an autobiographical disclosure. Document what actually happened. A range concerns varied emotional meanings, not a number of cards, contexts, days or spoken English words.

For PK4, follow a child's volunteered feeling with “What might you do?” Or offer: “Nia feels pleased with her picture and wants to show it. How might these go together?” A child's sign toward sharing paired with meaningful communication can show a beginning connection; no formal causal speech is required. Familiar stories and supported responses remain useful when the child contributes meaning. “Because she's pleased” can be a beginning connection in context, not necessarily deficient because it lacks a second alternative. Offer alternatives for teaching, not as a compulsory extra test: “She could enjoy it quietly too.”

If a learner says quiet people must be sad, gently counter: “Someone might be quiet because they enjoy looking, want space, or feel sad. We can ask; we cannot know just by looking.” If they propose hitting, validate the feeling but block harm: “Angry feelings are okay. I won't let anyone get hurt. We can ask for help.” Do not teach cheerful compliance as emotional competence. If distress emerges naturally, stop the lesson and provide ordinary trusted support; scoring waits. Follow the setting's safeguarding procedures for a disclosure; never promise secrecy or record identifiable details in public material.

EXIT: notice the child's contribution, not novelty (one optional minute)

The existing book or activity is suitable. Ask one non-leading question only if welcome, or observe a meaningful spontaneous contribution. Familiarity does not disqualify it; copying the adult's supplied answer without child meaning does. Record the actual child action, context, support and what it supports. Never pressure a child to prove a feeling. PK3 may have recognition evidence but no observed expression, or expression without sufficient range: retain that partial evidence. PK4 may show a beginning connection without explaining every alternative or using an uncertainty phrase. If provenance is unclear, mark uncertainty and seek another gentle opportunity rather than inventing success or inability.

REVISIT and stopping

Stop when the child declines, is tired, needs comfort, or has had enough meaningful engagement. Return in normal routines on a genuinely later day if useful, using familiar or different materials. Same-day practice is not later retention. Later observation remains NOT_OBSERVED until it occurs; no mandatory waiting interval blocks useful current evidence. Accumulate meaningful examples only as naturally available. Do not engineer negative emotions to complete a range.

Responsive progression, not entry gates

PK3 E3-1: welcome an optional meaningful feeling communication. PK3 E3-2: recognize different emotions in understandable contexts. PK3 E3-3: support the child's chosen expressive channel. PK3 E3-4: examine genuine recognition and expression of varied emotions over available examples. These can overlap; no forced ordering or autobiographical quota. See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for family methods and reconcile older novelty language before publication.

PK4 E4-1: connect a feeling with a possible action. PK4 E4-2: avoid claiming appearance proves feeling. PK4 E4-3: explore different choices as enrichment. PK4 E4-4: respect voluntary real communication. PK4 E4-5: extend meaningful connections when ready. A beginning learner is not required to complete all advanced comparisons before their source-valid beginning evidence is acknowledged.

Human delivery routes and evidence

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: use the flexible ENTRY, MODEL, DURING, EXIT and REVISIT above. The adult can continue shared art or reading without continual questions. CO-PLAY: caregiver joins the child's genuine activity and responds conversationally; it need not become a test. CLASSROOM: one educator organizes normal visible art/reading, gives a brief fictional model, then listens to one or two willing children while maintaining sight of everyone. Others continue familiar independent activity. No compulsory circle confession or chorus as individual evidence; rotate opportunities over days. If supervision requires attention, stop collecting notes. Never send a child away unsupervised for a private assessment. False credit and false rejection

False credit: a teacher assigns “sad,” makes the child repeat it, and marks range; a chorus explains Nia but one quiet child's mastery is inferred; an AAC tap chosen by the adult is credited as child expression. Preserve only genuinely attributable meaning. False rejection: familiar play, home-language response, sustained access assistance, no forced disclosure, or a brief contextually meaningful PK4 connection is denied for not being novel, English, unsupported or complex enough. Protect those alternatives. The optional evidence.py helper handles synthetic teaching examples, not diagnosis or automatic child evaluation. Real evidence requires human interpretation; support duration alone cannot establish who supplied meaning.

Facilitator: genuine voluntary feeling expression

Use one adult with one child, or one teacher supervising ordinary visible art/reading. The complete operative steps are in the lesson; no truncated historical excerpt is instruction.

Prepare

One familiar book or one paper and broad age-safe crayons per child, ordinary communication aids, visible comfortable pause space. No second adult, purchased printable, digital tool or forced disclosure. Optional first-party aids teach vocabulary; confidential adult notes are optional. Review the setting's safeguarding and supervision procedures.

ENTRY, MODEL and DURING: begin and respond

Offer book, drawing, continuing their activity, or declining. Listen first. Model one truthful mild adult feeling or openly fictional character, with two possible actions spoken by the same educator. Join meaningful practice for two to five flexible minutes. A request for help receives help, never a test first. If a child corrects an adult's interpretation, accept it. If there is no emotional communication, record not observed rather than diagnosing.

PK3: notice genuine recognition AND expression across varied emotional meanings. Welcome partial evidence without pretending one feeling proves a range. Personal disclosure is optional. Story/role expression must be labeled accurately rather than falsely treated as the child's private state.

PK4: notice a beginning meaningful feeling–behavior connection through speech, sign, gesture, home language or AAC. No mandatory alternative enumeration, formal causal sentence, novel context or occasional-only-support cap. Distinguish a child's meaningful contribution from adult-supplied content regardless of support duration.

Correct without coercion

“Quiet means sad” needs a gentle alternative: quiet can accompany enjoyment, uncertainty or wanting space; ask rather than diagnose. Validate emotions while blocking harmful actions. Do not induce upset for range evidence. Natural distress means stop instruction and provide care. Follow safeguarding procedures for concerning disclosures; keep identifying notes private.

Finish and revisit

Familiar activity is fine for EXIT. Listen for the child's meaning rather than demanding an unfamiliar task. Record context, action, actual help, partial or unclear evidence and an optional next opportunity. A same-day response is not later retention. REVISIT remains NOT_OBSERVED until a later actual date; use familiar materials if appropriate. Stop or defer at refusal, fatigue, distress or supervision needs.

One teacher with a class

During ordinary art/reading in the teacher's sight, give a short fictional model without inviting public disclosures. Let children keep working or pass. Listen briefly to one or two willing children while scanning the group. Rotate over normal routines, not everyone serially while others wait. No collective product, chorus or peer answer counts for another learner. If the room needs you, put notes aside. No unsupervised quiet corner or second adult assumption.

Access and valid alternatives

Maintain the child's communication, sensory and physical supports throughout. Describe visual story content; offer tactile or spoken alternatives; allow processing time and familiar routines. Home language and culturally different expression are valid. A neutral adult-assisted communication movement can preserve child meaning, but an adult choosing the feeling cannot. Avoid compulsory eye contact, facial enactment or English vocabulary. A declined autobiographical question is neither a skill failure nor a barrier to fictional learning.
