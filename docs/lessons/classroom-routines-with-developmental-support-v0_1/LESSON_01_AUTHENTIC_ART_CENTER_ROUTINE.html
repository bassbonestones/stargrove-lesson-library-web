Teach 1-on-1: Make something and leave the art place ready

Help a child begin an art activity, enjoy making something of their own, and leave the tools where they can find them again. This is about a useful routine, not neat artwork, fast cleanup or obedience.

For a younger preschooler, helping with one meaningful step is a good starting point and adult assistance is welcome. An older preschooler may follow a familiar classroom routine with occasional reminders. Neither age group has to do everything without help. At home, record the actual home routine: it can show a younger child's simple routine-following, but it does not show how an older child follows classroom routines at school.

Get ready

Set out two usable sheets of paper, one intact age-safe broad drawing tool, a shallow tool tray, and a separate dry place to save finished or unfinished artwork. An extra color is optional. A household tray or open box is fine. You do not need a printout, device, special mat or another adult.

Use a stable surface and reachable materials. Check broken pieces, mouthing risks and clear space to move. Offer the child's familiar communication and physical supports from the start. A real object or child-understood tactile marker can show where tools belong; introduce it before expecting use. A printed word alone need not make sense to a prereader.

Allow roughly 10–20 minutes during ordinary art time, not as a deadline. Short participation or continuing later is fine. Have a book handy if you want a different example to demonstrate, but it is optional.

1. Invite the child to make something

Try: “Would you like to make something here? You choose what to make. The tools live in this tray so we can find them again. Your picture can go here when you want to save it.”

Let the child choose the paper, tool, position, marks or help. Show where the supplies are; this is not a secret test. Notice what the child actually controls. They may watch, decline, ask for a break or return later. If they cannot begin, first check understanding, interest, comfort and access rather than assuming they cannot follow routines.

2. Show the idea if it helps

Using your own book, show choosing it and putting it back when finished: “Now I can find it next time.” Then return attention to the child's making.

If the child needs the art routine demonstrated directly, show it. Do not withhold useful teaching to keep a test “pure.” Just note what you demonstrated, and do not call a copied answer independent.

3. Make, help and respond

Let the child choose what the art becomes, even if it is one mark or a direction for you to draw. There is no required picture or color order.

If they are unsure, ask “Would you like help?” and give the needed explanation or demonstration. Younger preschoolers may need substantial help. Repeated step-by-step directions for an older child are useful practice, but not evidence that only occasional reminders were needed.

If a tool is hard to move, the child can direct a helper using their usual reliable communication. Record their choice and your movement separately. If you choose and perform everything while the child watches, do not count that as the child's routine-following.

If the child chooses an unexpected but safe and usable storage place, consider agreeing to it. If it blocks access or risks damage, explain the reason and offer another place. The learning goal is not remembering an arbitrary color or pleasing an adult.

4. Close at a real stopping point

When the child is ready, or before an actual scheduled transition, give a gentle preview and processing time. Offer to save unfinished work. Ask before moving or discarding their artwork. Returning tools does not mean giving up the picture.

Notice how the child leaves the tools ready for next time, with appropriate help. Say what their action accomplishes: “You put it where you can find it again.” Avoid public scores, speed contests or praise for being “good.” If the child is distressed, stop observing and attend to comfort and safety.

What did you actually see?

A short private note is enough: the date and real setting; which action the child did or directed; exactly how you helped; and what to try next. No photograph or recording is needed. Do not put personal child records in this lesson library.

A meaningful assisted step can be valuable evidence, especially for younger preschoolers. One step is not proof of the whole routine. An older child following a real classroom routine with occasional reminders is different from an adult directing every step. There is no fixed allowed number of reminders.

A tidy table, finished drawing, spoken rule or adult cleanup alone does not prove the child's action. A familiar routine can still show learning; it does not have to be brand new. If the opportunity was missed, declined or inaccessible, write “not observed,” not “failed.”

Stop and return naturally

Do not require a second art activity to finish this lesson. If a useful new opportunity occurs, another material or clearly explained storage place can show how the child applies the idea. Do not hide rules or model the exact choice immediately before calling it an uncued response. Otherwise leave transfer unobserved.

On another actual day, offer a real art occasion and notice the child's actions and help again. A picture still on display is not evidence that the child remembered the routine. Simplify to one meaningful step, keep practicing or add a useful challenge according to what you actually observe. Keep necessary access supports throughout.

This is a written teaching design, not proof of classroom effectiveness or any child's mastery. The Texas preschool guidelines permit adult assistance for younger preschoolers and occasional reminders for older preschoolers; they are not a diagnosis or readiness test.

Teach a class: A useful art-center routine

Children choose what to make while practicing how to start an art activity and leave its tools ready for the next use. One teacher can teach this within ordinary supervised art time. Do not turn the center into a cleanup test, require identical pictures or count a tidy classroom as evidence for every child.

Younger preschoolers can participate meaningfully with adult assistance. Older preschoolers may follow classroom routines with occasional reminders, not necessarily no reminders. These are flexible developmental expectations, not a behavior grade or a kindergarten-readiness test.

Prepare for the group you can actually supervise

For each occupied art place, set out two sheets of usable paper and one intact age-safe broad drawing tool. Extra colors are optional. Provide a shallow reachable tool tray for the table and enough separate dry space to save each child's finished or unfinished work. If one shared tray would cause reaching or congestion, add another nearby tray or use fewer places. No printed mats, scissors, special kit, device or additional adult is required.

Arrange stable accessible places in your normal line of sight. Check broken pieces, mouthing, reach, traffic and sensory load. Introduce the actual storage places with familiar words, signs, real objects or child-understood tactile cues; reading labels is not a prerequisite. Have each child's usual communication and motor supports ready.

Use your existing safe center arrangement. Children not receiving a focal observation should keep making their own art or participate in established safe play that you can still supervise. Do not create an unsupervised waiting group. If the room cannot support that, use a shared table in view or teach the routine without an individual check until a safer opportunity. Limit participation to the capacity you actually have rather than assuming a particular class size.

Plan around ordinary center time, roughly 10–20 minutes if useful, not a timed test. You need not observe every child's complete routine in one session.

Start with a meaningful invitation

Tell children: “You can choose what to make. Our tools live here so we can find them again. Your picture can go in the save place when you are ready.” Show the real locations. Children can choose materials, position, marks or assistance. A quiet parallel maker is participating; no partner role or public speaking is required.

If useful, demonstrate with your own book, choosing it and returning it so it is findable. You can instead demonstrate your own art tool directly when that is what children need. The demonstration is teaching, not proof of a child's independent performance. A chorus repeating the rule is not individual evidence.

Teach while children make

Children's artwork is their own. One child may make a single mark, another may explore a tool, and another may direct an adult's movement with their normal communication. Do not require the same picture or judge routine skill from artistic quality.

Choose one focal child briefly during a real start or ending while maintaining supervision of everyone. Notice their actual contribution, not merely proximity to a clean tray. Rotate attention over later opportunities, including children who communicate quietly or need more support. Mark children not individually observed as “not observed”; don't fill missing records from the class result.

Respond to what matters: - If a child is unsure, ask whether help would be useful and show or name the needed step. Younger children's assistance is legitimate. With an older child, repeated next-step directions remain supported practice rather than the occasional-reminder pattern. - If movement or communication is difficult, change the setup or use the child's established way to direct help. Note what the child chose and what the adult physically did. - If the child uses another safe workable storage place, consider it. If the place blocks access, explain the real reason for a change instead of demanding arbitrary uniformity. - If several children need help at once, stop recording and support them. Do not ask peers to police rules or leave others out of sight to finish an assessment. - If a child is overwhelmed, declines or needs a break, attend to safety and comfort. Do not force hands, gaze, speech, instant movement or giving up necessary aids.

End without taking over children's work

At an actual transition, give a gentle preview and processing time. Let children finish where possible or help save unfinished work. Ask before moving, displaying or discarding a child's picture. Returning tools does not require surrendering artwork. Keep saved work separate from shared tool storage.

Observe a focal child's real contribution if it is feasible: selecting where to put a tool, returning it, or meaningfully directing a helper. If you choose and execute everything while the child watches, record teaching exposure, not child control. Descriptive feedback such as “Now you can find it again” connects the routine to its purpose.

Keep individual evidence modest and useful

After supervision permits, make a short private note of date/context, the child's actual action or directed choice, exact assistance, steps you did not see, and the next helpful support. Ordinary confidential program records are sufficient; no public behavior chart, photo or recording is required. Do not upload personal child records into the lesson library.

A meaningful assisted action can be relevant evidence for a younger preschooler. An older child following a classroom routine with occasional reminders differs from continuous adult direction. There is no fixed reminder quota. One step is evidence of that step, not the entire routine, and one routine does not prove broad mastery. A clean table, completed group display or repeated rule is not a substitute for observed action. Missed, inaccessible or declined opportunities are “not observed,” not a diagnosis.

Use later opportunities, not an assessment queue

Familiar taught routines are legitimate. A separate transfer observation may use another material or an explained different storage place if that arises naturally. Do not hide rules, surprise children or demonstrate the exact choice immediately before claiming it was uncued. If no suitable opportunity arises, leave transfer unobserved.

On another actual day, revisit a real art routine and record what happens then, with necessary support retained. Choose practice, simplification or a useful challenge from the child's actual actions. Rotate focal observations fairly rather than requiring a same-day finish for everyone. Stored artwork alone does not show retention.

This prospective teaching design has not established observed child outcomes, field-tested staffing, physical-print accessibility or classroom effectiveness.

Method 01 — Make something, save it, and leave the art place ready

Purpose, source and claim boundary

The child practices a simple, meaningful art routine: get a usable material, make something they choose, then put tools where they can be found again and choose a safe place for their own work. The purpose is usable space and child agency, not obedient speed, a tidy-looking class, or a prescribed artwork.

Prepare a workable invitation

For one child, provide two sheets of usable paper, one intact age-appropriate broad drawing tool (a second color is optional), a shallow reachable tool tray, and a clear dry space for finished or unfinished work. Use a stable accessible surface and suitable positioning. A plain household tray or open box works; no purchased kit, printout or screen is necessary. Provide the child's usual communication and motor supports before inviting a response. Check mouthing risks, broken crayon pieces, sharp edges, congestion and reach; adult handles unsafe fragments. No scissors, small cutouts, paint, water or food are required.

A real book and its reachable resting place are optional for the adult's different example. If no book is available, model putting your own drawing tool back, label it teaching, and do not later call a copied action an uncued decision. The teacher-only original routine mats and support receipts are optional planning/record aids, not a child worksheet or a requirement to read. Never use a stock picture as proof of real behavior. A simple object cue at the actual tray or a child-understood tactile marker may help; let the child explore it before expecting use.

Ask privately what makes this routine comfortable, using ordinary family/program communication if helpful; do not require family disclosure or a form. Keep artwork private unless child and ordinary program permissions allow sharing. Ask before moving, discarding or displaying a child's work.

Planning window, not a timer: allow roughly 10–20 minutes within normal art/center time, with shorter participation or continuation later. Do not interrupt absorbed making simply to collect a cleanup score. At a real closing boundary, preview the end calmly, provide processing time, and offer to save unfinished work. If supervision or access cannot be maintained, pause the observation and help everyone safely; no assessment deadline overrides care.

ENTRY — notice a real beginning

Invite: “Would you like to make something here? You can choose what to make. The tools live in this tray so we can find them again. Your picture can go in the save place when you are ready.” Show where the supplies and save space are; this initial explanation is not a secret test or an independence violation.

Let the child choose a paper, tool, mark, position or directed assistance. Notice the action they actually control. Record any adult guidance plainly. If they do not begin, check understanding, interest, comfort and reachable materials rather than interpreting refusal as a skill deficit. They may watch, choose a different quiet activity, ask for help or return later. Never manufacture spilled materials or distress to test recovery.

MODEL — make the purpose visible

When a model would help, use your own book: “I chose this book. I am finished now, and I put it here so I can find it again.” Then ask the child what they want to do with their own supplies. If the child needs direct demonstration of the actual tray routine, demonstrate it; that is good teaching. Identify what you supplied and invite a meaningful child contribution rather than withholding instruction to preserve a test. Do not make a second adult, a peer, a story or a different room necessary.

DURING — make, notice, respond

Let art remain genuinely child-owned: no required shape, color sequence or finished product. A child may make one mark, tell an adult where to mark, explore a safe tool, or make a detailed picture. The routine supports this purpose; artistic accuracy is not the assessed construct.

  • Child starts and returns tools meaningfully: describe the effect: “You put it where you can find it next time.” Do not praise obedience or compare children.
  • Child continues making: offer time if available, or give a calm real transition preview and help save the work. Do not demand immediate stopping as evidence.
  • Child seems unsure of the next step: ask “Would you like help?” Then show or name the next step as needed. For PK3 such assistance can be source-consistent. For PK4, repeated step-by-step directions mean supported learning today, not the occasional-reminder pattern.
  • Child cannot reach/hold/select: change the setup or use their established accessible choice method. A directed helper can move a tool to the place the child chooses. Record that the child directed the action and adult executed the movement. Adult-selected destination with a passive child is not child-directed following.
  • Child puts a tool somewhere unexpected: check whether that place is actually safe and workable. An equally usable agreed place is valid; if it blocks access or risks damage, explain the real reason and show another option. Do not insist on an arbitrary mat/color as the only correct answer.
  • Distress, refusal, ambiguous communication or unsafe action: stop scoring, ensure safety, co-regulate or pause. Reoffer only when appropriate and willing. Do not diagnose, force hands, withhold access equipment or essential needs, or stage peer conflict.

The child can choose to keep, save or appropriately discard their own work with adult safety help. Returning tools does not require surrendering the artwork.

EXIT — a short observation, not a trick

At an actual end of making, observe what happens as the child leaves the place ready. Explain any unfamiliar storage arrangement before expecting its use; concealed rules test guessing, not routines. Keep usual supports. Note exactly which steps the child contributes and the help supplied.

A familiar taught routine may show meaningful routine-following. Do not erase it just because it was modeled. An adult's demonstration alone, a puppet action, verbal recitation, a class chorus or an already tidy table is not evidence of this child's action. A child directing a helper's real tool return can be meaningful control; a passive child watching adult cleanup is not.

If a fresh transfer check is useful and a real opportunity arises, use another familiar accessible art material or clearly introduced storage place later in the session or another day. Do not model that exact decision immediately before claiming it was uncued. Record reminders honestly; PK3 assistance and PK4 occasional reminders can remain legitimate. Do not add an unnecessary second task to a child who is done. Without a new opportunity, transfer is NOT_OBSERVED, not failed.

Private evidence and next teaching choice

A short private note is sufficient: date and routine/context; child identifier needed by the program; actual child action or directed choice; exact adult/peer help; which steps remain unobserved; next useful support. No photo, audio, public behavior chart or child diagnosis is required. Store under ordinary confidential program practice, not in the public lesson library.

Technical provenance, if used: U = observed child-controlled action without a supplied next-step answer; H = instructional hint/direct step assistance; A = access help under the child's meaningful direction; X = origin/meaning unclear. These are descriptions, not grades. H does not automatically disqualify source evidence. For PK3, meaningful assisted participation can match the source; for PK4 decide whether the actual pattern was occasional reminders rather than continuous direction. U is not whole-objective mastery; X is uncertain, not a negative diagnosis.

Decision anchors: (1) no opportunity, declined or inaccessible: not observed; (2) adult chose and performed everything while the child only watched: teaching exposure, child contribution not observed; (3) child meaningfully performed/directed one part with help: record that part and support; (4) observed child participation across start/use/return with PK3 help or PK4 occasional reminders: source-action candidate in the recorded context, not mastery; (5) continuous adult next-step instruction for PK4: supported practice, retain access and try a manageable routine another time. No fixed number of reminders or mandatory zero-help performance.

Developmental choices and continuity

PK3 R3-1: Begin with one meaningful step, such as choosing and placing paper, with adult assistance. Increase to a short start/use/return routine only when comfortable. PK3 R3-2–R3-4: Connect the end cue to a real save/return action, participate in a safe shared-space expectation, and later try a changed routine with assistance. These are possible teaching choices, not a required four-stage test.

PK4 R4-1: Orient to a known routine. PK4 R4-2–R4-5: Invite self-directed familiar transitions, a different safe setting, recovery after an occasional reminder and broader classroom routines over time. Do not invent a minimum number of contexts, insist on spontaneous error or treat stage labels as measured progress. See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for wider family possibilities, not prerequisites to this lesson.

REVISIT: on another actual calendar date, offer a real art routine when useful. Record then what the child does, including assistance, familiar versus changed context and how it differs from the earlier observation. A picture still on display does not prove retained routine learning. Later-date evidence is NOT_OBSERVED until genuinely observed. The child's interests, fatigue, sensory needs and local routine determine whether to practice, simplify, deepen or defer.

Two human routes and future-mode limits

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD / CO-PLAY: a teacher, parent or caregiver offers the above complete routine. No peer or second adult required. Use the actual setting in the record; household practice is not automatically school evidence. The companion facilitator guide provides a compact launch and observation summary.

TEACHER-LED CLASSROOM: choose an existing art area within the teacher's normal line of sight. Set out one safe tool and paper for each participating place, plus shared reachable storage and save space. Limit occupied places to the teacher's actual supervision capacity. Other children continue established safe activities visible to the same teacher; if that is not feasible, teach at a supervised whole-group table or defer individual observation. A short group introduction may demonstrate the teacher's book, but does not count as each child's evidence. Select one focal learner for a brief real start or closing observation while others make their own work. Rotate focal attention fairly over later opportunities, recording unobserved children honestly. Do not isolate a child out of view, depend on an unassigned adult, ask peers to enforce rules, or demand all children finish simultaneously. If several need help, give care first and stop recording. The separate Teach a class guide makes this route explicit.

SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: future narrated simulations could rehearse routines, but neither autonomous software nor learning parity is demonstrated. Images, badges or virtual cleanup cannot prove real classroom action. Existing assistive communication tools may support the human routes without turning them into a delivered SOLO game.

False credit: a tidy table, attractive picture, sequence-card answer or adult-only cleanup cannot establish child following; neither can a repeated commanded performance establish PK4 occasional-reminder independence. False rejection: meaningful child-directed accessible help, ordinary modeling, home-language communication, PK3 assisted action or PK4 occasional reminders are not automatically failures.

Facilitator companion — A useful art routine

Use with the full Lesson 01 plan. This is one independently selectable lesson, not a requirement to finish other family lessons first. The child learns to begin making, use accessible supplies, and leave tools findable while retaining choices about their own art.

Source and fit

PK3.I.B.1.a: “Child follows simple rules and routines when assisted by adults.” PK4.I.B.1.a: “Child follows classroom rules and routines with occasional reminders from adults.” These are from the 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, printed page 11. Assistance is legitimate; do not invent a no-help or timed-cleanup standard. One routine does not establish broad mastery or kindergarten readiness. Record home practice as home practice; it cannot establish PK4 classroom performance. A younger child's assisted simple home routine can be relevant to the PK3 action without demonstrating school transfer.

Set up before inviting the child

Per occupied place: two usable sheets and one intact age-safe broad drawing tool. Provide a shallow reachable tool tray and separate dry place where a child can save a finished or unfinished picture. One extra color is optional. No printing, cutting, device, peer or second adult is required. Check broken pieces/mouthing, stable positioning, clear movement routes and reachable materials. Use familiar language/signs and the child's normal access supports. An object or tactile cue at the tray can replace a printed label; introduce its meaning first. Keep records private; do not require photographs or publish child work without the relevant permission.

Allow roughly 10–20 minutes in ordinary art time, flexible to the child's engagement and real schedule. It can be shorter or continue later. Do not wait for a perfect picture or rush a transition to obtain a score.

ENTRY: invite and notice

“Would you like to make something here? You choose what to make. The tools go here so we can find them again. Your picture can go in the save place.” Explain the routine before observing; it is not a hidden test. Let the child choose a tool, paper, position or help. If they decline, watch quietly or need access changes, respond respectfully and record no unobserved skill.

MODEL: teach when useful

With your own book, show choosing, using and returning it so it is findable. If a book is unavailable or the child needs the art action demonstrated, show your own tool return directly. Label this teaching, not the child's independent answer. Invite their next meaningful contribution. No second adult is needed.

DURING: protect making and useful support

Let the child decide what to make; art quality is not the routine target. If they need a step named or demonstrated, help. If they cannot physically move a tool, offer the normal accessible way to direct a helper. Record who chose its destination. Do not call adult-chosen cleanup child control.

If the child seems uncertain, ask if help would be useful; if overwhelmed, pause and meet the need. If they choose another safe usable storage place, agree it rather than demand a color-coded “right” answer. If a place is unsafe or blocks access, give the real reason and offer another. If it is time to transition while they are still engaged, preview gently and offer saving unfinished work. Never force hands, withhold needed aids, require gaze or speech, or label a help request noncompliance.

EXIT: notice a real ending

At a genuine end, observe actual tool return and the child's choice for their work. Familiar taught routines can yield meaningful evidence. Record the actual help; PK3 assisted participation can match the source, while PK4 continuous step directions remain supported practice rather than the occasional-reminder pattern. Observing one step does not prove the complete routine. Ordinary assistance and modeling are not themselves defects.

A later new material or clearly introduced storage place can provide a fresh transfer check if useful. Do not model that exact choice immediately before claiming an uncued response. Do not hide the destination or create a surprise. If no natural extra opportunity occurs, transfer is not observed; do not force another activity.

What to record and do next

Use a brief private note: actual date/context; child action or directed choice; adult/peer help; observed steps versus missing ones; next support. Plain words are enough. If using technical codes, U means child-controlled action without a supplied next-step answer, H means instructional help, A means child-directed access help, X means uncertain. H is not automatic failure. Unknown, inaccessible and declined are not observed, not low ability.

  • Adult chooses and performs all steps while the child only watches: teaching exposure only; offer a meaningful child contribution later.
  • Child directs a helper or contributes one step: name that step and help, not whole mastery.
  • PK3 follows the simple routine with help: a source-action candidate in that context.
  • PK4 follows a classroom routine with occasional reminders: a source-action candidate, not full mastery.
  • PK4 needs every step directed: preserve support; simplify and practice rather than punish or remove access.

Teach a class with one educator

Use existing safe activities within your normal line of sight; restrict the art area to the number of children you can actually support. Supply the per-place materials above. Others continue meaningful supervised making or established safe play, not a queue for assessment. Observe one focal child briefly during a real beginning or ending. Rotate attention over later opportunities; a class chorus or clean table earns no individual credit. If supervision cannot support simultaneous activities, use a shared table in view or defer the check. When several children need support, stop recording and provide it. No added staff, forced peer role or universal same-session completion is assumed.

REVISIT and limits

On another actual date, use another meaningful art occasion, familiar or changed as appropriate. Observe then and compare actual help and child contribution. Until then REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED. A stored picture is not retention evidence.
