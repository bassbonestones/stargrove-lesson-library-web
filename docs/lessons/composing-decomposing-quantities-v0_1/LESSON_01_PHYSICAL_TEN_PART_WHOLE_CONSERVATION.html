Two trays, one whole

Teach 1-on-1

Help a child discover that the same blocks can be shared between two places and brought back together. This is playful introductory work, not a test of every number to ten.

Get ready

Use two broad shallow trays and a nonslip mat enclosing them. Put TWO large blunt, washable, nontoxic blocks on the mat; keep up to ten in a closed adult box. For your demonstration, prepare a separate three-block set with two trays and another mat. No small counters, food, screen, printer or written equations are needed. Check swallowing risk and reach. Two clearly marked flat areas can replace trays.

Start with two unless the child comfortably chooses another small amount. Let them name the places: shelves, garages or simply “here” and “there.” No pretend story is required. A child may pause or decline.

Use tactile boundaries, quiet materials, signing, home language or a communication device as needed. If a child cannot move objects, move exactly the piece to the place they direct. Keep those supports throughout; choosing the grouping for them is teaching, not physical access.

Five steps to teach

1. Let the child try first

“These are all our blocks. Choose where they go in these two places. What happens when we bring them together?” Wait without a countdown. It is fine for one tray to be empty. Notice what the child does before helping. A child who watches or declines has not given evidence yet; that is not a wrong answer.

2. Show why the whole stays the same

Set the child's blocks aside where they can see them. With your three, put one in one tray and two in the other. Bring them together: “Still three.” Then put none in one tray and all three in the other: “The parts changed. I didn't bring in another block.” Invite looking, touching or listening. Your demonstration is not the child's performance. If only one set is available, explicitly switch to your teaching turn and back; later use of those objects is familiar practice, not automatically new-context transfer.

3. Give the choices back

Return the child's blocks. “How would you arrange yours? How can you check they are all still here?” Allow moving, joining and trying again. Ask “Would you like another way?” rather than naming the parts. A few meaningful turns are enough; roughly five to ten minutes is a planning estimate, not a deadline.

If they count just one tray, gather both parts together and show the enclosing whole. If they add a block accidentally, notice it openly, then restore the original whole or recount the new one. Never secretly add a block to trick them. If they swap the trays, say “That is another arrangement.” It is correct even though the sizes repeat. When ready to explore different sizes, use your three to show one-and-two versus none-and-three. If they count a tray as a block, distinguish container and contents with an empty tray. If reasoning is unclear, ask “How could you check?” Accept rejoining, matching or counting across both parts. Help as needed, then record that help honestly.

4. See what the child chooses without an answer

Offer “Choose a way for these blocks to be in our two places. How can you show how many there are together?” Keep access supports but stop giving part sizes. Familiar objects and numbers are fine; note what was modeled. Exact immediate copying is practiced evidence, not broad transfer.

Record the actual whole, parts, how the child checked, and whether they or someone else chose the arrangement. One meaningful split is local progress. Two different size pairs can show variety when possible, not mastery. Zero has only none-and-none; one has one-and-none or its reversal. Never ask for an impossible second size pair for zero or one. Different-looking sides are still valid, even when sizes repeat.

5. Stop, clean up and return later

Stop for fatigue, refusal or safety concerns. Bring all blocks back together and store them safely. Plan another short turn on a later date. Familiar materials can reveal return to the idea; a changed setting can sample transfer. An immediate retry is practice, not a later revisit. Do not record future learning as already observed.

What to try next

For a beginning learner, stay with one to three. When useful, explore two to five in different ways, empty groups and zero, then six to ten with familiar groupings. Let the child help choose. No one must finish the whole range in one lesson. A child comfortable with five may choose seven; another may benefit from many calm turns with two.

Keep the evidence fair

A spoken “five” copied from you, a grouping you selected or a partner's action is not independent child reasoning. A directed motor helper, home language or communication device can preserve child ownership. If you cannot tell who chose, write “uncertain,” not “failed.” If nothing was observed, write “not observed.” Keep only necessary local instructional notes under your setting's policy; no photos, names or uploads are required.

This lesson supports an introductory preschool mathematics guideline, not a readiness cutoff, diagnosis or proof of every number. Younger children may enjoy ungraded play. No real child learning or classroom effectiveness was tested in this written package.

Two trays, one whole

Teach a class

Children explore how the same collection can be in two places without gaining or losing objects. This route assumes ONE educator and a supervised group, not an extra adult or private testing station.

Materials for a real group

For twelve children in six pairs: twelve broad shallow trays, six nonslip enclosing mats and thirty large safe blocks (five reserved per pair). Add your model set of two trays, one mat and three blocks: FOURTEEN trays, SEVEN mats and THIRTY-THREE blocks total. Initially put just TWO blocks on each child mat; keep the rest in your controlled box.

For other class sizes, prepare one set per pair rounded up. An odd child has their own set. If sharing is not appropriate, provide individual sets and recalculate quantities. Use the existing safe table or circle so all children stay within ordinary supervision. Do not create unattended centers. No printer, device, worksheet, small counters, coins or food is required. Check swallowing risk, reach and material safety.

Two marked flat spaces can replace trays. Provide tactile boundaries, high contrast, quiet pieces, home language, signing or communication devices. A helper may move precisely as a child directs but must not choose their grouping. Keep necessary access even when observing independent choices.

Introduce the idea together

Before showing an answer, invite children to arrange their two blocks: “These are all your blocks. You choose where they go in the two places. What happens when you bring them together?” One empty part is valid. You cannot observe everyone at once; treat this as an opportunity, not a class score.

Model with your separate THREE blocks where the group can see/hear or tactually access it: one here, two there, then reunite. “Still three.” Next none here, three there. “The parts changed; I didn't bring any more.” Describe the moving objects rather than relying on color alone. If the group cannot access the model together, repeat it within supervision rather than treating inaudible or unseen teaching as delivered.

Give each child a real turn

In a pair, one child chooses placements and the other watches/checks; then switch after a short turn. Both may give their own names to the places, or use no story. A child may work individually instead of sharing. No public ranking, speed race, compulsory speech or forced role exchange.

Ask “How would you arrange yours? How could you check they are all still here?” Allow joining, matching, counting across both parts and repetition. Start with two; offer a manageable familiar amount up to five when useful. Six to ten is a later option, not a whole-class requirement. Roughly ten to fifteen minutes is a planning estimate; stop sooner for fatigue or unsafe handling.

Stay with the whole group. If objects scatter or supervision becomes difficult, pause all activity and gather materials. Never leave children unsupervised to conduct a private exit test. If accessible materials or safe supervision cannot be provided today, postpone the affected activity and record no observation, not failure.

Respond to what you see

  • A child counts only one tray: invite gathering both parts within the enclosing mat. If you show how, label the turn supported.
  • A block arrives from another pair: identify it openly, return it or recount the changed whole. Do not quietly accept it as the old whole.
  • A child swaps sides: acknowledge a valid arrangement. It repeats the same sizes. To teach size variety, show one-and-two versus none-and-three on your separate set.
  • A child counts the tray itself: distinguish the container from the objects with an empty adult tray.
  • A child is unsure or frustrated: reduce the visible whole, offer a pause or model again. No diagnosis or compliance score.
  • A child is ready for more: offer a larger familiar whole or another child-chosen context, not every possible partition.
  • One partner gives the answer: thank them for helping during practice, then preserve a later individual choice. Their help cannot establish the other child's independence.

Observe individual learning fairly

Choose two or three focus learners during ordinary group activity ONLY if supervision permits. Rotate focus on later days. Ask the focus child to choose a fresh grouping and show the whole while the partner refrains from answers. Familiar blocks and familiar amounts are valid for local evidence. You do not need a different object set or new number for every child.

Note the actual whole, chosen parts, how the child checked and whether the choice was theirs. A class chorus, repeated adult answer or partner-built arrangement is not individual evidence. If influence cannot be separated, write “supported” or “uncertain.” Mark other children “not observed,” not failed. Write one or two minimal notes after cleanup, not at the cost of sightlines. No photographs, names or uploads are required by this lesson.

One meaningful partition can be local progress. Two different unordered size pairs add evidence of variety where possible, not a universal pass threshold. Zero only has none-and-none; one only has one-and-none as a size pair, though sides may reverse. Never reject these valid cases or demand a second impossible pair. Actual changed-context evidence can sample transfer; familiar practice alone does not prove it. No single activity proves every number to ten.

Close and plan the return

Invite pairs to bring their parts together, put all blocks away and check the floor and trays. Children may show or describe a discovery, but public sharing is optional and not a substitute for individual observation. Plan another brief experience on a later real date. Return to familiar materials or introduce a changed context when helpful. Immediate retries are practice; only an actual later encounter can be recorded as a revisit. Do not prefill future outcomes.

Today's local goal: child chooses or directs a way to share a manageable fixed whole between two places, joins those parts and notices the same quantity. Over repeated experiences, invite varied partitions of familiar totals, eventually sampling the 0–10 range. “Begins to understand” is not mastery of every number, every partition, written equations or explaining in English. A child who shows 2+3 and 3+2 has made valid ordered arrangements of five. These have the same unordered part sizes; they do not alone establish variety of sizes. Materials, safety and setup

For one child: two broad shallow trays, one nonslip mat large enough to contain both, and ten large, blunt, washable, nontoxic blocks reserved in a closed adult box. Start with TWO blocks on the mat; for a child already comfortable with two, agree on three to five. Keep unused blocks off the mat so the whole is unambiguous. No beads, coins, seeds, edible rewards, tiny counters, sharp cutouts or tall unstable containers. Adult checks age suitability, swallowing risk and reach before use. No physical touch or eye contact requirement; a child may decline or pause.

For the adult model, use three additional safe blocks and two separate trays on a separate mat. Different color is helpful but never the only cue; tactile boundaries and clearly separated locations suffice. If only one set is available, explicitly end the child's trial, model as teaching, then restore the child's turn and label its history. Do not call immediate copying independent transfer. No purchase, printer, screen, reading or numeral writing is necessary. Two flat marked areas may replace trays; preserve the enclosing whole. A large tactile boundary and child-directed proxy are valid access.

ENTRY: find a meaningful starting point

Place two blocks in the whole mat before showing a solution. “These are all our blocks. You can choose where they go in these two places. What do you notice when we bring them together?” Do not require some in each tray: an empty part is valid. Wait without a countdown. Child may move, point, sign, use a home language or direct one object at a time through a motor proxy. Record what happened before helping, including the selected parts and whole. Merely watching is NOT_OBSERVED, not a wrong answer.

If the child chooses four familiar objects spontaneously, use four; two is a safe starting proposal, not a placement cut score. If counting or membership is uncertain, begin with one or two and reunite visibly. This is teaching, not diagnosis. If the child wants a pretend library or garage, name the trays accordingly without requiring cultural background, family disclosures or ownership language.

MODEL: show why, not just what

Set the learner's objects aside visibly. On the adult's three-piece set say, “These three are our whole. I can put one here and two here. I will bring them back: still three. Now none here and three here. The parts changed; I didn't bring in another block.” Move slowly and let the learner inspect, touch or hear a description. The model includes 1+2 and 0+3, not a misleading reversal claimed as new sizes. Spoken equations are optional. This adult action is never scored as the child's.

If contrasting addition, announce a separate demonstration: “Now I am bringing a NEW block from the box. This changes the whole.” Remove that demonstration before the child's turn. Never secretly add/remove an item as a test, accuse the child, or stage surprise to manufacture an error.

DURING: child owns the mathematical choices

Return the child's small whole. “How would you arrange yours? How could you check they are all still here?” Allow repeated moving, joining, checking and self-correction. When ready ask, “Would you like to try another way?” Do not tell the child the part sizes in an independent observation. Two or three meaningful turns are an opportunity, not a quota. A brief episode of roughly 5–10 minutes is a planning estimate, not a timed performance standard. Stop earlier for fatigue, refusal or unsafe materials.

Respond to the actual idea: - Counts one tray as the whole: gesture around the enclosing mat, ask to gather all the blocks, then model on the adult set if needed. Record conceptual help; after teaching offer another open turn. - Adds a block accidentally: calmly identify the extra, restore or recount the new whole transparently. Record that trial's changed total; do not call it successful conservation of the old whole or global mathematical failure. - Reverses sides only: “That is another arrangement of the same blocks.” If exploring size variety, ask whether the two amounts are the same sizes; use 1+2 versus 0+3 on the adult set. Do not reject the valid reversal. - Counts the container: touch/describe the container separately from its contents, demonstrate empty containers, then let the learner choose. - Correct arrangement but unclear reasoning: ask an open “How could you check?” Rejoining, matching each object, counting across both parts, or intentional reconstruction can communicate meaning. A rehearsed number alone is not enough to infer understanding. - Needs physical help: move exactly the child-selected object to the indicated tray, confirm ambiguous direction neutrally, and keep that access for later turns. If adult selects the grouping, it is supported practice. - Already finds several ways: let child choose a larger familiar whole up to ten or explain a pair to an interested partner. Do not demand all combinations.

EXIT: distinguish local evidence, help and transfer

When the child is comfortable, remove answer-bearing prompts and offer a fresh decision, not necessarily new materials or a larger number: “Choose a way for these blocks to be in our two places. How can you show how many there are together?” Invite another arrangement if meaningful and desired. Preserve essential access. Familiar objects and numbers can give valid local evidence; record prior modeling rather than discarding all familiar performance. An exact copy of a just-modeled arrangement remains practiced evidence unless the child's own choice/check makes more visible.

Record actual total, ordered part pairs, whether all original objects remain, the child's check/action, and who chose each part. Mark U for child-origin without conceptual help, A for access-only child-directed assistance, H for answer-bearing conceptual help, X when provenance is uncertain. X means independence NOT_ASSESSABLE, not learner failure. H is worthwhile practice, not unprompted evidence. No observed response is NOT_OBSERVED. Adult-supplied parts do not earn child-origin evidence.

For totals 2–10, two different unordered pairs can strengthen evidence of size variety, but are not a universal pass threshold. For 0 or 1, record the valid available pair and explain why a second size pair is impossible. Physical repartition and rejoining support this local goal; they do not establish all 0–10, retention or broad transfer. Only label a changed-context attempt “transfer sampled” when it actually uses a meaningfully changed context without an answer supplied. Do not withhold access or introduce unfamiliar objects solely to force novelty.

The optional synthetic evidence.py helper records narrow source-relevant patterns; it is not a child test, diagnosis, norm or validated score. It cannot decide whether an adult's observation accurately captures understanding. A local valid partition, varied-size sample, unknown provenance and supported practice remain different results.

REVISIT and progressive choices

Plan another short opportunity on a genuinely later date; do not prefill it as performed. An immediate retry is practice, not later retention. Use familiar materials to check return to an idea, or change a context when ready to sample transfer; document which. No actual children or later revisit were observed here: REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED.

The shared stages are options, not age locks or required daily completion: - C4-1: one to three, see parts and reconstruct the same whole. - C4-2: two to five, choose different arrangements; compare size pairs when possible. - C4-3: empty whole 0+0, one as 1+0/0+1, and an empty part in familiar larger wholes. - C4-4: 6–10 with optional five-group anchors, only when meaningful. - C4-5: sample a different context and a later return; each claim stays bounded to observed cases. Seven access decisions

  1. Attention/executive: offer a small visible whole, one invitation, unhurried turns and a break, not mandatory persistence.
  2. Language/communication: accept child-authored home-language, sign or augmentative and alternative communication; adult translation must not choose the math.
  3. Literacy: no reading, digit writing or equation recital required.
  4. Numeracy: reduce the whole for meaningful exploration; preserve actual membership. Zero/one do not have impossible second size pairs.
  5. Sensory/autism: quiet large objects, tactile/visual contrast and predictable transparent moves; no surprise “trick,” forced touch or eye contact.
  6. Intellectual/developmental: model slowly and repeat as useful; label prompted practice honestly rather than denying its teaching value.
  7. Motor/hearing/vision: use stable boundaries, meaningful description and a proxy moving exactly as directed. Adult-chosen parts are conceptual help, not access.

False-positive and false-rejection checks

False-positive: adult chooses both pairs, peer arranges while focal child repeats “five,” or a sixth item arrives while observer marks “same five.” Correction: describe assistance/membership, teach openly, and offer a new child decision without pretending the earlier trial passed.
