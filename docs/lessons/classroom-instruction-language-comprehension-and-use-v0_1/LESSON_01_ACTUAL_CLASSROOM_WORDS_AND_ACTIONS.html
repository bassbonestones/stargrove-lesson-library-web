Teach 1-on-1: words that help us make things together

What your child is learning

“Choose” helps us decide what we want. “Show” helps someone else know. Explore these useful classroom words while your child makes a little display or picks something to enjoy. Younger preschool learners can show understanding without saying the words. The older preschool goal also includes intentionally using classroom language, through speech, sign or a communication aid. Start where this child is, not with a demand to finish every goal today.

This lesson focuses on a few useful action words, not all classroom language. A child can also use a taught word meaningfully to describe their activity, such as “I chose this.” Directing a helper is one option, not the only valid expressive response.

Use these words in a real instructional activity, such as an actual classroom book choice, individual tutoring opportunity or genuine home-learning session. A similar household game can be helpful practice, but not every household request automatically demonstrates classroom instructional language. Record the actual context; no pretend classroom or artificial new task is needed.

Get ready

Gather two different large, safe familiar objects and a stable reachable workspace. Mats or paper sheets are optional. Take turns with the same pair for your demonstration and the child’s activity; a separate model pair or extra objects for an optional contrast may be convenient, but are not required. Board books, large cards or chunky age-appropriate blocks work. No special kit, printer, worksheet or purchased device is needed.

Check that the child knows the object names and can perceive and indicate both choices. Let them explore unfamiliar textures first. Avoid choking-size pieces, sharp edges and loose fibers. Sit comfortably beside the materials. Keep the child's familiar communication aid and usual access supports available. Use a language the child understands; if a word's translation is uncertain, check it rather than scoring a guess.

Make a simple private note: date, target word/language, exactly what you asked, the child's response, and any help. Keep understanding and expressive use in separate notes. No photos, diagnosis or family details are needed.

1. Invite a useful choice

“We could make a little display, or choose a book to enjoy. Would you like to try?” Follow the child's interest. “Choose one for your display.” Either object is a valid choice; do not point out a preferred answer. Later, “Show me the one you chose.” The child may point, name, sign, use their usual communication aid or direct your hand as an access helper. They need not give the item away.

A single grab might show preference rather than understanding the word. Note it without a mastery claim. A child who says “I know you mean pick one, but I want both” has communicated meaning, even while declining the requested action. A child who does not respond may need access, time, comfort or a different activity. Do not label refusal a vocabulary failure.

2. Show how the words do different jobs

Take your own demonstration turn with the same pair, or use a separate pair if available. “I choose this book. Choosing means I decide which one I want.” Then show your choice: “I show you so you know what I picked.” You can choose quietly; showing communicates. Your child does not have to make eye contact. Make the demonstration visible, audible or tactile in their accessible way.

Teach the word you want the child to learn. You are not spoiling a later observation by teaching its meaning. Familiar words, materials and classroom routines can provide genuine current evidence. Change a feature only if it helps clarify a specific ambiguity, or if you want to explore optional transfer.

3. Let the child try, and respond to what happens

Continue their project. If choose and show are mixed, say, “You picked one. That was choosing. Showing helps me know your choice.” Demonstrate with your own item, then offer another useful turn. Record that explanation as help. Do not move their hand to make a correct answer.

If the child is comfortable, vary the materials or positions. If ready for more, try “place” and “check”: put your card on your mat, then examine whether it is where you intended. Explain the intention before asking someone to check. Add one useful contrast, not a long word list.

Gradually stop answer hints while retaining language, sensory, positioning and communication support. Familiar symbols should stay in their usual places. The child can ask for clarification, take a break or continue free play. No useful observation today is a reason to return later, not to force a test.

4. Notice actual meaning, without requiring another test

If the child already showed genuine meaning during the activity, record that observation. No new materials, different purpose, extra test or later date is required before it counts as current evidence. If a further observation would help and the child is willing, use familiar materials and say the familiar/taught word without pointing out the semantic answer. Later ask the child to show something already selected. Do not always make the same movement correct for both words. If a response fits both meanings and you cannot tell, write “uncertain” and look for a natural contrast another time.

For an older preschool learner, look for both understanding and intentional expressive use. They can occur in the same real episode if each is genuinely evidenced, or in different natural opportunities. If an expressive opportunity is missing, offer, “How can I help?” in their project. Follow a safe, intentional instruction the child selects, such as “show me,” “put it here,” a sign or a meaningful communication-symbol message. A familiar phrase can count when independently chosen for this purpose. Repeating your suggested answer immediately is helped practice, not independent expressive evidence. Do not choose the answer on the device for them.

A meaningful description such as “I checked it” can also be expressive use; a direction to a helper is not required. For each dimension, note whether the child selected the meaning, received an answer hint, used only access support, or left you uncertain. Record the two dimensions separately, not necessarily in two separate events. A younger child is not required to speak. An older child's independent understanding does not automatically prove independent expressive use. One example is limited evidence for that word and context, not mastery of all classroom language.

5. Stop comfortably and return naturally

Enjoy what the child made or the book they selected. Stop when they are tired, uninterested or uncomfortable, even if no check was completed. If a later observation is useful, on a genuinely later day notice the same word in the same or a different routine. Record the real date and response only when it happens; a planned revisit is not evidence. If meaning is unclear, teach the useful contrast again. If it is secure in varied contexts, invite one new useful word or the child's own question.

This is a written teaching design, not a claim of observed learning or field-tested effectiveness. No reading, exact English wording, fast response, forced sharing or novel sentence is required.

Teach a class: words that help our projects

Goal and developmental fit

Children explore how useful classroom words change what we do and communicate. Begin with “choose” and “show”; add “place” or “check” when meaningful. Younger preschool learners demonstrate understanding without a speaking requirement. Older preschool learners also intentionally use instructional language; understanding and expression may be evidenced in the same real episode. These are learning goals, not labels of obedience or fixed age-based starting levels.

This lesson focuses on useful action words, not the full range of classroom language. A child may also describe their activity using a taught word, such as “I chose this”; an instruction to a helper is not the only valid expressive response.

Prepare an activity you can supervise

Use an established tabletop or floor activity where all children remain within your normal safe supervision. Offer displays, book choices or constructions using familiar materials. Provide two safe familiar objects per participating child, or plentiful shared materials with voluntary turns. A separate adult model pair or extra objects for an optional contrast are conveniences, not prerequisites; take turns with familiar shared pieces if needed. Mats or sheets are optional when useful for the actual activity. No special worksheet, printer, second adult or new device is required.

Choose flat, stable, large age-appropriate materials without choking parts, sharp edges or loose fibers. Confirm object names, perceivable options and communication access. Keep familiar signs, home-language equivalents and usual communication aids available, with their normal vocabulary layout. A child may observe, choose a different safe activity, or take a break. Do not create scarcity or make children surrender personal objects for a language test.

Use your setting's actual staffing and supervision requirements. This page does not establish a safe adult-to-child ratio. If supervising the activity already takes all your attention, teach without individual recording and observe during another suitable routine.

Launch with a useful purpose

“We can choose things for our projects, and show someone what we chose.” Invite children to select their own materials. On your own pair, explain, “I choose this book because I want to use it. Now I show you so you know my choice.” Demonstrate in a sensory-accessible way without demanding eye contact. Group gestures, repeated words and peer examples are helpful practice, not individual proof.

Children who already know these words can plan a different project or use a meaningful next word. Those who need a simpler contrast can stay with choose/show. Nobody has to wait through a public one-by-one test.

Keep children creating while you notice one learner

Children continue their own projects within sight. Briefly join a focal child in that same supervised space: “Choose one for your display.” Later, “Show me what you chose.” Both objects can be valid selections. Do not point to a preferred answer. Notice actual meaning, not merely a correct-looking grab.

Familiar objects, purposes and routines can provide real meaning evidence. For an independent claim, observe the child's semantic choice rather than merely crediting an immediate copied peer answer. Seeing a peer does not automatically erase later meaningful use. When copying versus understanding is genuinely unclear, describe the uncertainty and seek a natural contrast without shaming or isolation. Keep teaching in the group and seek another natural opportunity later.

A private rotation list helps quieter children get later opportunities. Record only children you actually observed. There is no requirement to finish every child's observation today. Children you did not see remain “not yet observed,” not unsuccessful. Stop recording whenever supervision, care or conflict needs your attention.

Respond without taking over the meaning

If choose and show seem mixed, explain the difference on your own materials: “Choosing is deciding which you want. Showing helps someone else know.” Record this as teaching help. Offer another useful opportunity when comfortable. Avoid moving the child's hand or providing the answer on a communication device.

If nouns, language access or sensory comfort are uncertain, fix those before judging word meaning. Use verified home-language/sign/communication equivalents. Fade answer-bearing hints, not essential access. A child can refuse and still show understanding, for example by explaining that they understand the request but prefer both books. If the meaning is unknown, record uncertainty rather than a behavior judgment.

For ready learners, teach place/check through a genuine purpose: place a card where intended, then check whether it is there. State the intention so checking is not guessing your private rule. Do not introduce a never-taught word and call failure lack of transfer.

Gather honest individual evidence over time

A genuine meaningful observation already made can count; no second task, changed materials or purpose is required. If another observation is useful and the child is willing, use a natural brief opportunity with familiar materials and the taught word without supplying the semantic answer. Vary a feature only where needed to rule out a misleading habit/cue, or to explore optional transfer. Look for a meaningful response that is not simply the familiar tray route. If the same action could fit several words, keep the exact response and collect a clearer natural contrast later; one movement is not conclusive.

For an older preschool learner, identify both receptive understanding and intentional expressive use. They may occur in one real episode or different natural opportunities; distinguish the evidence and help, not a compulsory event count. If an expressive opportunity is needed, offer to help with the project: “How can I help?” Follow a safe, intentional instructional message the child selects through speech, sign or their usual communication aid. Independently selecting “put it here” can count even if the phrase is familiar. Immediate repetition after an adult says “Say put it here” is helped practice. The teacher may be the willing listener; no peer or second adult is required. A meaningful description such as “I checked it” can also supply expressive evidence, without directing another person.

Privately note date and learner code, target term/language, exact prompt/context, actual response, and specific help. Record receptive understanding and expressive use separately. Distinguish child-selected meaning, adult/peer answer support, access-only help and uncertainty. A group product, chorus, attendance or unobserved turn is not individual mastery. No photograph or personal/family information is needed.

Close, revisit and extend

Let children enjoy or show their projects voluntarily; do not rank vocabulary performances. Return to a normal routine before attention or safety breaks down. If a later observation is useful, on a genuinely later calendar date notice the taught word in the same or another activity and record what actually happens. Until then, retention is not observed; a changed context may additionally explore transfer, but is optional. Meaning still unclear? Return to the useful contrast. Meaning secure across varied contexts? Add one helpful word, invite children to explain what they want a willing helper to do, or ask how a different word would change the action.

This plan supports child choice and supervised play. It does not claim classroom-tested results, guaranteed observation quotas or implemented child-only software.

This real-material exploration teaches the meanings of useful classroom words through their different purposes. Start with choose (select an option) and show (make something known to a listener). Later add place (put somewhere) and check (examine against an intention). These are author-selected examples, not a state-mandated word list. No reading, adjective test, counting quota, exact English pronunciation, complete sentence, or speed requirement. PK3 means the younger prekindergarten source group; PK4 adds intentional expressive use, not simply more compliant behavior. Developmental starting points depend on this child's knowledge and access, not age alone.

Current evidence and optional transfer: Teach the target word and its meaning. Genuine understanding or expressive use in a familiar real classroom activity can count immediately; new objects, a changed room/purpose, an unheard word, a second test and a later date are not source prerequisites. If a response could merely follow a memorized motor route, gaze cue or immediate answer model, record uncertainty/help and vary only what is needed to distinguish meaning in a comfortable natural opportunity. Meaningful child-selected reuse of familiar wording, signs or communication symbols is valid. A separately claimed independent check leaves the semantic choice to the child. A changed context can supply stronger transfer evidence but is optional, not a gate for current source action.

Actual instructional context: This target concerns language used meaningfully in classroom learning. The one-child route can be a real classroom tutoring/individual teaching opportunity, including an actual home-learning instructional setting. A similar household game may be useful language practice, but do not automatically label any household request an observation of classroom instructional-language use. State the real context and intended learning purpose; no fake school scene is required. Content-area and descriptive terms are also instructional language; the four action-word examples are a focused selection, not exhaustive coverage.

Two dimensions, not two compulsory events: For PK4, identify both receptive understanding and intentional expressive use. They may be observed in the same authentic episode if each is actually evidenced, or in different natural opportunities. One dimension alone is partial evidence; neither a chorus nor compliance alone establishes expression. No extra manufactured task is required after a sufficient genuine observation.

Materials and adult preparation

For one adult and one child, gather two different large familiar safe objects and a stable workspace; one or two flat mats or plain sheets are optional if useful for the chosen activity. The adult may use the same objects in turn, explain whose turn it is and then return them to the child. A separate model pair or extra pair for an optional transfer contrast may be convenient, but six objects and a reserved check set are not required. Examples are familiar board books, large flat cards, or chunky age-appropriate blocks; use available safe items rather than buying special props. The child must recognize the object names and be able to perceive and indicate both options. Offer free looking/touching before the first prompt so unfamiliar texture is not the hidden test. Do not use choking-size pieces, sharp edges, loose fibers, food, or props that invite unsafe climbing. Flat stable materials reduce falls and grabbing.

Keep the child's established communication system available, with usual vocabulary in its familiar positions. Do not remove familiar symbols to make the test “independent.” Provide hearing/visual/tactile access, a quiet enough setting, stable positioning and child-directed movement assistance. Check a home-language equivalent with a knowledgeable adult when needed; if meaning is uncertain, teach in a verified language and postpone that inference. Follow individual support plans without making diagnoses.

Choose an ordinary purpose the child cares about: picking a book to enjoy, designing a little card display, or arranging blocks. The child can change the purpose, explore quietly, decline, or stop. Prepare an adult note with date, private learner code, target term and language, exact prompt, actual response, and help for each observation. Record receptive and expressive evidence separately. Optional adult-only aids are word mats and observation receipts; no printer or child worksheet is needed.

ENTRY: notice the child's meaning before today's teaching

Sit beside the materials, not across a test desk. Say, “We can make a little display, or pick something to enjoy. Would you like to try?” After the child agrees, offer two familiar objects and say, “Choose one for your display.” Do not point at a preferred option. Any deliberate selection is valid, but a single grab could be preference rather than word understanding. After acknowledging the choice, say, “Show me the one you chose.” A point, intentional gaze established as communication, sign, spoken label, or child-directed proxy can show it; handing over the item is not required.

If the child repeats the same movement for both words, do not diagnose a vocabulary deficit. Observe what it means in context; then teach the contrast. If nouns, language access, sensory comfort or willingness are uncertain, mark this part not yet observed and repair access first. Never treat refusal as poor comprehension. If a child communicates “I know you mean pick one, but I want both,” record that meaningful receptive evidence even though the requested action was declined.

For PK4, also offer a real reason to communicate: “I can help with your display. What would you like me to do?” Wait without offering the target phrase. Follow a meaningful child-selected instruction if safe. A gesture may demonstrate receptive meaning; PK4 expressive evidence needs an intentional meaningful communicative word/sign/symbol/message, not the adult guessing from a reach. Do not require every child to produce expressive evidence before receiving teaching.

MODEL: make the useful difference visible

Use your own demonstration turn, with a separate pair if available or the same pair before returning it to the child. Say, “I choose this book. Choose means I decide which one I want.” Then, “I show you my choice so you know what I picked.” Indicate it without transferring it. Explain: “I can choose quietly for myself. Showing helps another person know.” The child need not look at your eyes; present the actions through their accessible sensory route. One adult is sufficient: narrate your own choice and show it to the child, without demanding a scored response.

For a child ready for place/check, use a card and mat: “I place the card on my mat. I check whether it is on the mat.” Inspect the relation rather than automatically moving it again. No deliberate deception, startling error or manufactured conflict is needed. Explain the intention first so “check” is not an unexplained guess about the adult's private criterion.

DURING: play, feedback and fading

Return to the child's own project. Let the child choose what to make or enjoy. Offer a short invitation using the selected term. If choose and show seem mixed, say, “You picked one. That was choosing. Showing lets me know your choice. You could point to it, tell me, or use your usual way.” Demonstrate on your own object, not by moving the child's hand. Invite another useful turn; do not force repetitions.

If a child already distinguishes the words, acknowledge the observed meaning. Offer another useful term or optional varied context if wanted, rather than require a further check or add every word at once. If place/check are too demanding, return to choose/show. If a child wants free play, follow a natural moment when a word would help; if no moment occurs, stop without manufacturing an assessment. A child can ask “Which one?” or request clarification. Clarification that only names an unclear reference may preserve the target; explaining the word's meaning is teaching and must be recorded as help.

Fade answer-bearing gestures, supplied words and completed sentences when comfortable. Keep neutral language translation, established communication vocabulary, positioning and child-directed motor access. Do not fade disability access. Log actual support, not a label alone: U = child selected the meaning independently; H = adult or peer supplied the answer/meaning; A = access support without selecting meaning; X = uncertain origin. These are technical observation codes, not child scores. Supported practice remains valuable learning and is not retroactively relabeled independent.

EXIT: notice current meaning; clarify only when needed

A genuine meaningful observation already made during ENTRY or play can be current source evidence. Do not disregard it because the materials, wording or setting are familiar or because no separate EXIT occurred. If another observation is useful and the child is willing, use the same familiar objects or a naturally different arrangement. Ask with the familiar/taught term without pointing out the semantic answer. For example, an established classroom project can offer a reason to choose a book or show a selection. Do not always make one motor route correct regardless of the word. If the response fits several interpretations, record uncertainty rather than manufacture failure, and seek a natural contrast later.

PK3 requires receptive understanding, not speech. PK4 additionally needs intentional meaningful expressive instructional language: a child-selected “show me,” “put it here,” “I checked it,” a meaningful sign or communication-symbol message can serve when appropriate to the actual classroom learning purpose. A teacher may offer “How can I help?” if a communicative opportunity is missing, but directing a helper is not the only expressive form. Receptive and expressive evidence can come from one real episode if both dimensions are genuinely demonstrated; record their basis and help separately. Otherwise keep partial evidence honestly. Familiar phrasing is valid when selected meaningfully; an immediate adult-cued “say check” echo is supported practice, not independent expressive use.

Keep communication/access supports. Do not preselect the correct device symbol, supply the meaning with gaze/pointing or credit a peer’s answer to the child. If the adult supplies semantic help, describe supported learning rather than independent evidence. Variation is necessary only when the particular inference would otherwise be misleading; a genuinely changed context is optional transfer. One observed term/context remains limited evidence, not mastery of the whole classroom-language standard. Stop for fatigue, refusal, unclear access or safety; no mandatory final test.

REVISIT and conditional progression

If a later observation is wanted, on a genuinely later calendar date notice whether the same useful word works during the same or another real routine: choosing a book, showing a construction, placing art, or checking whether a container is closed. Keep access and note the real date and context. Until it happens, REVISIT is NOT_OBSERVED, not scheduled evidence. No mandated interval or number of successful trials is inferred from the standard. If evidence is unclear, return to the contrast that matters; if it is secure across varied opportunities, add one useful word, invite child questions or explore how different words change what a willing helper does. Do not make unrelated developmental domains prerequisites.

Grade-sensitive progression, not a one-session checklist

  • PK3 Q3-1: choose/show with familiar perceivable options; understand the word's purpose, not a motor routine.
  • PK3 Q3-2: add place/beside/under only when familiar nouns and accessible spatial relationships support meaning; use correct reference objects, not color shortcuts.
  • PK3 Q3-3: explore check/wait/turn in a real voluntary routine; understanding a request does not oblige surrendering a personal object.
  • PK3 Q3-4: optionally observe taught meanings in other actual routines/days. Current familiar-context understanding remains valid; do not introduce an unheard term as a mandatory test.
  • PK4 Q4-1: establish receptive foundation without pretending it earns expressive credit.
  • PK4 Q4-2: intentionally select a useful familiar instruction for a willing adult using speech, sign or communication aid.
  • PK4 Q4-3: add meaningful relation/comparison language as the child's actual project calls for it, without an extra math test.
  • PK4 Q4-4: communicate in voluntary collaboration; the adult can be the willing listener, so no second adult or peer is required.
  • PK4 Q4-5: identify both receptive and expressive evidence, in one or more actual opportunities as available. Changed contexts can explore transfer, not a prerequisite. Independence is semantic choice, not novelty of phrasing.

CLASSROOM: one teacher, multiple learners

Plan within the setting's existing safe staffing and supervision, not an invented class-size ratio. In an established supervised tabletop or floor activity, provide two safe familiar objects per participating child, or shared plentiful supplies with voluntary turns. A separate model pair or extra observation pair is optional; safe shared materials can be used in turn. Introduce the useful word briefly through your own project. Whole-group imitation and chorus are practice only.

Children continue their own displays, book choices or constructions within sight while you briefly notice one focal child's meaning in the same supervised area. Do not isolate the focal child behind a screen or turn away from the class. Familiar objects and purposes are valid. For an independent claim, observe the child's actual semantic choice rather than crediting an answer copied from a peer. Seeing a peer does not automatically invalidate later meaningful use; if copying versus meaning is genuinely unclear, record uncertainty and seek a natural contrast without isolating the child. Observe only as many children as you can safely attend to. Keep a private rotation note so quieter children have later opportunities; unobserved children remain not yet observed rather than failed. Never suspend safety supervision to finish the list.

If the whole class needs support, pause individual recording, simplify the activity, or return to the usual supervised routine. A later natural observation is preferable to forced one-by-one waiting. Children not being observed remain engaged in their chosen projects; no long queue, mandatory public performance, or ranking. Mixed developmental levels use the same shared materials but different receptive/expressive goals.

False-positive and false-rejection controls

A correct-looking grab, memorized tray route, adult pointer, peer imitation or immediate echo is insufficient. Conversely, independently reused vocabulary, translated/signed communication, established communication symbols, a child's intentional refusal that demonstrates meaning, and child-directed access assistance must not be rejected. Record the actual prompt, response and support for receptive and expressive parts separately. Keep notes private; no photograph, diagnosis or personal-family disclosure is required.

Four written routes and limits

CO-PLAY: caregiver follows child interests with the real materials and distinct receptive/expressive evidence above. ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: tutor intentionally teaches one contrast, fades meaning prompts and observes actual meaning in a familiar context or an optional changed-context check. CLASSROOM: teacher preserves supervision and individual evidence across real opportunities. These are written designs, not observed outcomes. SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: future digital exploration would require independently usable narration, communication access and honest limits; an animated helper cannot prove real human reciprocal communication. No child outcome, physical-print acceptance, external educator endorsement or four-mode parity is claimed.
