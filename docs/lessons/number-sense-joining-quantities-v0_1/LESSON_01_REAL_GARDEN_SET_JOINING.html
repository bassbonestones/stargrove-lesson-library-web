Why this particular experience

A group is preparing a pretend community garden display. On a garden tray are two large pretend vegetables (original cards or safe toy food). Three more arrive from a second garden basket. The group wants to put every piece on one table before a friendly pretend gardening event. Children decide what to do with the new objects and investigate what changes. There is no canon character, reward economy, timed correctness gate, competitive rank or child data. The garden is a short original context, not an assertion that this is educationally better than another story.

Big idea: A new group joins an existing group → the combined collection contains more objects (when the joining group is nonempty); how many altogether can be found by combining and counting, matching, recognizing or another valid strategy.

Explicit lesson boundaries: This is joining, not guaranteed fluency in numbers, symbolism, subtracting, or mathematics-as-memorized-facts. To avoid conflating reasoning and enumeration, children can meaningfully show getting more before they know the new exact number. Comparing quantities and showing evidence with objects is the point. Writing a numeral is optional and separately observed.

Primary educational outcomes (research opportunities): - Concept — learner shows/indicates what changes when additional objects are placed with existing objects, and distinguishes adding from moving the same items without adding any. - Representation — learner can enact a small new-group-joining scenario with physical pieces, pictures or accessible communication (PK4-relevant). - Quantity — when able/interested, counts/matches all objects in the joined collection, or otherwise indicates the total; counting difficulty does not void emerging understanding of joining.

Who/time: One adult + one child, small group of 2–5, or group-class teacher leading with a document camera/projector and physical materials. Core 10–15 min, optional start/transfer up to ~20 min; shorter, longer, or skipped per learner engagement. Not a rigid timer or screen-time target.

Materials and rights: For the optional later 2+3 garden example, print the actual ORIGINAL_GARDEN_KIT.html at 100% (two original garden baskets, the central collecting tray, large round produce tokens and three original story/prompt cards); or use five or more LARGE, age-appropriate, non-breakable toy food pieces or blocks. If printing: keep full sheets/mats whole or attach big counters to sturdy cardboard before cutting, adult prepares safely. Prevent mouth/choking hazard: no beans, seeds, beads, coins, button batteries, tiny counters, or scissors in child reach. High-contrast tactile blocks can replace pictures. No branded media, copyrighted publisher script, photos of children, personal names, identifiable class work or online account. Wipe surfaces as needed. Child may handle objects, point, gaze, sign, use home language, AAC, direct an adult proxy or observe/decline.

Actual first-party garden story: three short connected scenes

Read naturally; don't insist children memorize text. The adult controls pace and lets the child make choices.

Scene 1 — Where should these go? (0–2 min)

“Today we're getting a pretend garden stand ready. Here is our wide display tray. I have two big vegetables ready to show. Where would you put them so visitors can see them?”

Place exactly two objects on the tray only after the child has looked and chosen a location. Invite the child to handle or direct, not to obey a chore. Accept any reasonable placement and unexpected pretend-game suggestions. Ask if the two pieces are all that we have right now, before adding anything.

Scene 2 — Something arrives (2–5 min)

“Someone brings another basket. Look: three more vegetables. We want all our garden vegetables together on the display. What could we do?”

Lay exactly three pieces nearby in a separate basket. First opportunity for child-originated action: let them predict or demonstrate the change; don't state the result. If they want to play rather than count, ask, “Will our display have more, fewer or the same number when these join?” Accept gesture, movement, home language, eye-gaze, or no answer.

Ask them to join the new objects to the original display in their own way. Say, “We had some, and more arrived. What changed?” Do not make an assessment out of an uncertain response. Observe if the child brings/indicates new objects versus moving the same two around.

Scene 3 — Let's find out (5–10 min)

Now ask, “We began with two and added three. How can we find how many altogether?” Wait; child may count all, use fingers, make groups, select an accessible count representation, or choose not to determine an exact total. If child demonstrates that there are more but cannot count to five, celebrate the reasoning, then provide a one-to-one counting scaffold without pretending exact-count understanding was independent.

Invite one check: “Can we show the same collection in a different arrangement without bringing in or taking any away?” Move existing pieces apart; ask if the number changed, and why. This is a useful transfer/extension only and not independently credited as the full joining source outcome.

Close (1 min)

“Today two vegetables were on the tray, and three more arrived. We put the two groups together. You showed how the collection changed.” Child may retell, draw two-and-three dots, move objects to clean up, or simply finish. Do not require repeating 'five' as a closing ritual. A child who says something surprising may lead the final reflection.

Contingent teaching moves: what to say and do in real moments

Says “more” and moves the new 3 onto the tray, but does not know the new total “Yes, these are additional pieces. Let's look at everything on our table. Teacher discipline: Ask an authentic question; allow response/strategy before intervening; model only as needed; keep mathematical verbs (“we had,” “more arrived,” “joined,” “all together”) clear; never require motor speed, speaking, eye contact, reading, a device, or perfect counting as a precondition for engaging in the joining idea. Avoid turning every child action into a test.

Intentional alternatives, not duplicates of the same question

  1. Simplification/entry (1 + 1 or 1 + 2): child can make the new pieces physically join, recognize more, or indicate size with a gesture. Use the same story context but truly reset the pieces and don't feed the result.
  2. Primary learning opportunity (2 + 3): exactly five, clearly two initial + three incoming. Ask what changes before asking how many; support counting all if appropriate.
  3. New-context transfer (1 + 3): a different display, e.g., “one flower on the picnic sign, and three more flowers are brought to decorate it.” Adult can use large blocks as proxies instead of real flowers. Ask learner to show a joining event, then optionally number. This is a distinct opportunity from mimicking the teacher's garden tokens.
  4. Child-authored variation: learner selects how many of the five available large counters initially appear and how many new counters are delivered, as long as the adult can make the quantities visible. If learner invents taking away, acknowledge and name separating as another interesting idea, not claim addition evidence.
  5. PK4 representation probe: “Can you draw or arrange two groups to show someone else what happened, even without the toy basket?” Story picture, block arrangements or signed/AAC story are alternatives; adult scripting their solution is supported, not child-authored.

Real classroom/homeschool adaptation (not yet a product feature)

  • Whole class: Large original picture cards / object camera plus five physical objects; teacher invites multiple strategies, voluntary participation, pairs or small groups, anonymous group reasoning; doesn't publicly score individual children or force public cold calls.
  • Homeschool one-to-one: Parent may replace garden with an interest the child chooses (pretend garage, toy bakery, animal shelter) while preserving source-specific joining logic. Use non-edible safe props; no claim one theme is superior.

November 1 learning-differences remediation

PK3: access and child-owned source relation

Differentiate instruction: Garden delivery needs concrete genuine NEW toy placement: start one existing vegetable and one incoming different item, with calm nonfood accessible substitutes and child-owned direction of an adult hand when needed.

Fresh independent evidence: Exit moves out of garden to an unknown household set; observe the child direct actual new object arrival and indicate that more items now exist.

False-credit trap: The adult dropping toys in and saying five before child responds, or child shifting a preexisting toy, is not independently demonstrated increase.

False-credit trap: Reciting the gardeners total from a model or adult-authored story is not independent PK4 representation.
