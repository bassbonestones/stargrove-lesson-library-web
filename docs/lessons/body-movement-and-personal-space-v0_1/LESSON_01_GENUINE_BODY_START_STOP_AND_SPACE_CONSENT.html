Teach My Start, My Stop, My Comfortable Space

One adult and one child. This is a written teaching guide, not a physical-ability diagnosis or evidence that a child has demonstrated the skills.

What the child is learning

For a 3-year-old, the goal is gradually developing control of their own real, comfortable body movements. Starting, pausing or adjusting a personally feasible movement are meaningful ways to learn; the child does not have to achieve every step in one lesson. An authentic small movement can be useful early evidence; not every stage must be completed today. The child does not have to stand, walk, reach a prescribed distance or perform a perfect motion.

For a 4-year-old, two things matter together: being aware of where their own body is in real space and respecting other people's actual personal-space preferences. There isn't a fixed “arm's length” rule. A child may respond by maintaining a distance that is already comfortable, moving a little, asking first or respecting “no.”

Respect does not require someone to announce a complaint. A child who knows where their own body is and independently avoids intruding on another real person's visibly occupied personal workspace may show meaningful boundary respect without hearing “move back.” Watch what the child actually does in the genuine shared setting, not what you assume the other person privately wants. A fictional example or a general class “hands to yourself” chant is useful practice, not individual real-person evidence. If no real relevant human spatial encounter happens, record that component not yet observed, not failed.

Prepare a comfortable choice

Set out two broad, soft, stable mats or large washable paper shapes on an accessible table, floor or lap-level surface. Remove slipping and collision hazards. Ask what movements and communication methods are comfortable for this child, including seated or assistive movement, head/hand/arm motions and a child-directed motor aid. Never push the child's limb, move their chair on their behalf and call it the child's own movement, or insist on uncomfortable touch.

Only one adult is needed. The same familiar adult can be the real willing person whose actual workspace preference the child respects. Two adults are never required to demonstrate this lesson. If you describe a hypothetical “please move back” exchange without another person, label it practice, not a real human response.

Five friendly moves

1. Invite the child's own first choice

For a younger child, say: “Which comfortable little movement would you like to try? Can you choose when it begins and ends?”

Observe real child-started hand, head, arm or seated-body motion and any voluntary adjustment. The adult may protect stability and provide neutral physical access without supplying all the movement.

For an older child, say: “Where is your hand or body compared with this big soft shape? Is it beside it, between the shapes, or farther away?” Accept child-authored pointing, sign, speech or communication-device choice about their real present position.

If the adult actually needs room for their own drawing or work, they may naturally say, “I need this space for my hand. Could you leave room here?” Observe how the child respects the true preference. Don't fake a complaint just to collect an answer.

2. Model a different adult movement

On your own separate soft shape, gently choose when to move and stop your own wrist or hand.

Say: “I chose my move. I can change it. I can stop.” For personal space, calmly explain that people may want different comfortable distances. If you act out an imagined response, call it an example; no child-source evidence exists from a fictional second person.

3. Offer meaningful guided exploration

Let the child choose a small safe movement near a soft edge; they may pause, turn, reverse or stop without any timed task. Ask: “What would you like to change?”

An older child can choose how to do a shared, freely agreed drawing or building activity. If a real boundary is expressed, support an appropriate response without dictating the exact message or physically repositioning the child. Respect the child's own “stop” too.

4. Notice a new individual opportunity

Give a different soft shape or starting orientation. A younger child may show their personally controlled start, adjustment and stop again. An older child may identify their actual body position and then, if a real willing human expresses a genuine preference OR the child independently protects that real person’s visibly occupied workspace without any request, show meaningful respect. Notice the child’s actual choice, not an inferred private feeling. You can observe these older-child components on separate suitable occasions; don't manufacture an intrusion or “no.”

Note what the child actually did, what was only a rehearsed answer and which adult actions were neutral access versus supplied instruction. No actual human preference and no real clearly occupied other-person space to respect? Record that aspect not yet observed, not failed.

5. Return on a genuinely different day

Use another safe familiar or changed arrangement if the child wishes. A planned revisit is not completed evidence.

Teach My Start, My Stop, My Comfortable Space with a Class

Classroom guide for an educator responsible for multiple children. No second adult is assumed. This is a prospective teaching design, not a tested class staffing plan or proof of learner outcomes.

What children are learning

Younger preschoolers develop personally controlled, comfortable real body movements. The first useful action may be opening a hand, turning the head or shifting an arm from a stable seat; they need not walk, stand or race. A teacher moving a child's body for them isn't child-origin motor control.

Older preschoolers develop both awareness of where their own body is in actual space and meaningful respect for another real human's personal-space preference. An actual willing adult's genuine need for room can be valid; a fictional puppet's “please move” is a teaching illustration only. No one must stage annoyance, an invasion of space, or a peer refusal.

Respect does not require someone to announce a complaint. A child who knows where their own body is and independently avoids intruding on another real person's visibly occupied personal workspace may show meaningful boundary respect without hearing “move back.” Watch what the child actually does in the genuine shared setting, not what you assume the other person privately wants. A fictional example or a general class “hands to yourself” chant is useful practice, not individual real-person evidence. If no real relevant human spatial encounter happens, record that component not yet observed, not failed.

Prepare the classroom

At existing accessible tables or supervised seated/stationary areas, offer each child a large stable soft shape, a second harmless soft reference and a calm alternative. Check choking risk, secure surfaces, reachable arrangements, sensory preferences and any individual mobility/supervision plan. Avoid mandatory walking, touch, closed-eye exploration or a crowded movement circuit. Count the staff actually present, not a hypothetical assistant.

Single-teacher option: Keep movements at calm existing workspaces under ordinary whole-room supervision; use a brief shared introduction and then circulate among a manageable number of focal children. If a child's physical support consumes the teacher's attention such that other children cannot be kept safe, defer individual assessment until real coverage is available. All children may meaningfully practice even if not individually assessed today.

Introduce the idea together

Invite private first ideas: For younger children: “What small comfortable movement would you like to start or stop?” For older children: “Where is your own body compared with your soft shape?”

Allow nonspoken choices without asking all children to move in synchrony. Demonstrate on different adult-only soft material how the teacher starts, changes and stops their own hand. Explain that classmates can like different amounts of space. A hypothetical narrated permission example is teaching, not evidence that anyone's real boundary was respected.

Give children meaningful practice

Learners explore self-selected safe stationary motions and optional pauses. Older learners may notice their own body-to-mat relation during calm voluntary shared art, choosing how to keep workspaces comfortable. A willing teacher's actual preference for their own working area can be expressed naturally, but no child is required to encroach or hear a scripted “no.” Respect children's own STOP requests immediately.

Organize a small number of real observation turns while the others continue safe, supported, independently chosen quiet work. Pairs can talk about how different people prefer space, but an assigned partner or observed class discussion is not evidence of a child's own responsive real human behavior.

Observe individual learning fairly

For younger focal learners, note a personally initiated or ended real bodily movement at the child's emerging stage, with adjustment as optional progression, and the difference between stabilizing access and an adult supplying the entire motor act. A new comfortable shape/position can provide a stronger independent check; the printed objective doesn't require an arbitrary distance.

For older focal learners, record separately accurate awareness of own present real body location and actual respect for a willing real person's comfort preference or observable occupied personal workspace. Someone already at an agreed safe distance may simply maintain it; don't require moving away just to tick a box. If a human boundary opportunity never arises, that part is not yet observed, not failure. Do not infer individual evidence from a class chorus, cartoon or adult model.

Include, respond, stop and revisit

Accept home language, sign, accessible choices, AAC, trusted seating/mobility support and a voluntary quiet route. If a teacher moved the child, describe that honestly and invite a child-owned comfortable motion another time. No motion/partner observed -> not yet observed, never a deficit label. Later different-day observations count only after they actually happen.
