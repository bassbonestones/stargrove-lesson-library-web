A Note About Something I Notice

Help a child try punctuation while making a note about something that interests them. A few approximate dots or other marks can be a real beginning. This is for the PK4 writing goal, not a grammar test. Younger children may join without being assessed against an invented PK3 punctuation goal.

Gather and settle

You need two blank sheets, a broad washable marker or a familiar accessible writing tool, one sheet for your example and a private scrap for notes. Start near a safe familiar object, picture or window view. Do not look directly at the sun, touch unknown plants or go outside for the activity. The child may choose a toy or something remembered instead. Plain paper is fine; printing is optional.

Use comfortable light and reach. A large object or spoken description can replace a picture. Offer the child's established access supports and home language, sign or AAC; they need not speak or write conventional letters. Ask before physical help.

1. Find something worth a note

“What have you noticed? Would you like to make a note so we remember it?” Let the child choose the subject and whether to join. They can begin with drawing, scribbles, letters or shared writing. If they ask you to show them first, do so. No pretest is required.

2. Show one possibility

Write a short note on your own sheet: “I noticed a long leaf.” Say, “I can try a dot to show where my thought stops,” and add it. The example may stay visible. Tell the child it is all right to try marks in different places. You can also write words together and let the child add marks to that shared note.

3. Explore together

“Would you like to try a mark in our writing?” Follow their interest. You may suggest trying a dot; later record that help. Accept approximate marks and unusual placements. Do not require a new topic, perfect punctuation, independent composition or a spoken explanation.

If the child only draws, enjoy the drawing and offer a note beside it. If dots may be decoration, ask what they are doing without suggesting the answer; leave meaning uncertain when necessary. If no mark is attempted, continue writing, model again later or stop. Refusal is not failure. For access help, follow the child's choices rather than choosing the mark for them, and note who physically made it.

4. Finish and notice the contribution

Allow around 5–10 minutes, flexibly, and stop sooner when the child is tired or done. Ask whether they want to keep or revisit their note. Privately record the context, their actual mark, your prompt or help, access support and any uncertainty. “Added three dots to our note after I suggested trying a dot” describes real prompted experimentation, not independent mastery. Watching you add all the punctuation does not show their own action. A dot alone does not prove its purpose.

Return to the same note or another interest on a later day if helpful. Record that revisit only when it happens. No new-context test or quota of punctuation types is needed. Keep child records private under your setting's rules; do not upload them to the lesson repository or share without permission.

A Class of Noticers and Note Makers

Children explore punctuation in personal or shared writing. One teacher can offer the experience during ordinary supervised writing time without promising to assess every child at once. The PK4 goal is beginning experimentation, including teacher-prompted interactive writing, not perfect conventions. Younger children may explore without a fabricated PK3 punctuation target.

Prepare for the group you actually supervise

For N participating learners, provide at least N writing surfaces and N usable broad washable markers or established accessible writing tools, a few spare sheets, one teacher model sheet and private notes. Printing is optional. Use familiar safe pictures, an indoor view, remembered observations or large safe objects already checked by the teacher. Avoid sun-gazing, unknown plants, small choking objects and outdoor travel. Arrange all children within your ordinary supervision; do not create a remote unattended center.

Plan for accessible reach, lighting and quieter nearby participation while remaining in view. Use descriptions or safe tactile objects for children who cannot use pictures; support home language, sign, gestures and AAC. No additional adult is assumed. If a child's support requires staffing you do not have, defer that particular activity rather than compromising safety.

Invite and model briefly

“Let's make notes about things we notice.” Children choose their subjects, personal or shared notes, language and whether to participate. Drawing or observing can remain meaningful participation. Show your own short note and say, “I can try a dot to show where my thought stops.” Add it. This can be a short group invitation, or a model beside children already writing; a compulsory circle is unnecessary.

Let the writing develop

Children make pictures, scribbles, letters or words; you may write a shared note with them. Invite willing children to add marks: “Would you like to try a mark in the writing?” A direct dot prompt is allowed. Accept experimentation after words, at the end, or in sporadic places without correcting every mark. The model may remain visible. A child need not create all the words independently to contribute punctuation.

Attend to a few willing focal children, perhaps two or three, while keeping the whole group supervised. This is a planning example, not a tested class-size guarantee. Other children continue their personal/shared notes or another familiar supervised choice, not waiting in an assessment queue. Rotate your close attention on later occasions. If a spill, conflict or support need arises, put documentation aside and supervise first. Mark missed observations as not observed.

Respond without taking over

For drawing only, enjoy the idea and offer writing beside it. For apparently decorative marks, ask neutrally what the child is doing; do not supply an interpretation or demand speech. For no punctuation, model later if welcome. For frustration, offer established access help or a break. Ask before touching a child's hand. A peer may collaborate, but peers are not safety supervisors or substitute assessors. Children may stop or opt out without public comparison.

Capture individual contributions, not class success

Privately note date, secure learner reference, writing context, who contributed words, the child's actual punctuation action, exact prompts/access help and uncertainty. Shared writing can provide evidence when you actually saw the child's contribution. Group attendance, chorus, a peer's mark or a completed display cannot establish each learner's action. Teacher-added or automatic punctuation is not the child's contribution. A child adding dots after your suggestion shows supported experimentation, not independent mastery.

End flexibly when interest or supervision calls for it. Return to familiar or new notes later if useful; only record a later revisit after it occurs. Do not impose a new-topic test, perfect placement or all punctuation types. Keep records local under school privacy rules, never in public displays or the shared curriculum repository. This is a written teaching plan, not a claim of observed classroom effectiveness.

Lesson 01 — A Note About Something I Notice

Purpose and exact source

PK4.IV.C.5: “Child begins to experiment with punctuation when writing.” This experience invites a child to leave a written trace of something worth noticing, then explore marks that may help a reader. It is beginning experimentation, not correct grammar, perfect placement, original composition, an oral explanation test or independent mastery. There is no published PK3.IV.C.5 outcome; younger children may participate voluntarily without a fabricated PK3 assessment target.

Direct comparison with the archived original 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, printed p47, confirms that children may put punctuation after an entire piece, after each word, in sporadic places, or add missing punctuation to interactive and independent writing with teacher prompting. Prepare a worthwhile invitation

One adult and one child need two blank sheets (another if requested), a broad washable marker or established accessible writing tool, one adult modeling sheet, and a private scrap for observations. Use a familiar safe view through a window, an available large picture, a child-selected harmless object checked by the adult, or a remembered observation. Nothing must be newly made or unfamiliar. Do not look directly at the sun, touch unknown plants, bring small choking objects or go outdoors for this activity. A nature subject is an invitation, not a gate: a child can notice a toy, household object or imagined feature instead.

The optional first-party writing packet provides three genuinely empty response spaces. Plain paper is equally suitable. Keep teacher instructions off the response area. For low vision offer large high-contrast marks, suitable lighting and the child's established tactile or digital writing setup; an adult description or safely explored large object can replace a picture. Ask before physical help. The child may use home language, sign, AAC, eye gaze or gestures to direct choices; speech and conventional spelling are not prerequisites. A technical writer/proxy may follow the child's exact direction, but record whose action produced each mark and do not infer independent handwriting.

ENTRY: begin with the child's interest

Allow roughly 1–2 minutes, or join an already underway writing activity. Say, “What have you noticed? Would you like to make a note so we can remember it?” The child may choose their subject, surface, language and whether to participate. Notice existing writing or punctuation if naturally available; do not require an unprompted baseline before teaching. A child who wants an example first can have one. Picture-making, noticing without writing and declining remain worthwhile participation, not failure.

MODEL: make the idea visible without taking over

On your own sheet, write a short message about your own observation: “I noticed a long leaf.” Say, “These words tell what I noticed. I can try a dot to show where my thought stops.” Add the dot and read the message. It need not be a different topic from the child's. Explain that children can try marks without getting every shape or place right. A familiar model may remain visible during supported learning. Do not describe the teacher's dot as a child contribution. If the child prefers interactive writing, compose a shared note and leave room for the child to add marks; teacher-written words do not disqualify the child's actual punctuation contribution.

DURING: write, explore and respond

Offer about 3–6 minutes, flexibly. The child may make scribbles, mock letters, letters, words, or contribute to shared writing. Invite, “Would you like to try a mark in our writing?” A direct teaching prompt such as “Let's try a dot” is permitted; note it rather than disguising it as independence. Accept approximate shapes, sporadic placements and marks after several words. Do not insist that the child justify a grammatical function verbally. Watch their action in the writing context; if its purpose remains unclear, describe it without inventing meaning.

If the child has only drawn a picture, enjoy it and offer to make a note beside it. Model on your own sheet or offer shared writing. Do not score decorative dots as punctuation merely because they look like periods. If no punctuation is attempted, continue meaningful writing or stop warmly; model again on a later occasion. If a child repeats the model, acknowledge supported experimentation. Offer less help when useful, not a compulsory new-page test. If a tool or communication barrier prevents participation, change the setup or defer; do not diagnose a literacy deficit.

EXIT: notice what actually happened

Invite the child to show or revisit their note, or end when interest fades. A familiar piece can provide evidence. Record: date and secure local learner reference; writing context and who supplied words; exact child punctuation action; adult/peer prompts; access help; uncertainty; and a feasible next invitation. For example: “On our shared leaf note, after I said 'try a dot', child added three dots beside the words.” That supports prompted experimentation, not independent punctuation convention mastery. An unprompted contribution can be recorded as such if actually observed. Do not require novel materials, a different topic, perfect placement or a spoken explanation to retain valid evidence.

False credit: Adult writes and punctuates the entire note while a child watches, or an app inserts the mark automatically. Neither demonstrates the child's writing action. Conversely, a child adding a dot to interactive writing after a teacher prompt is not automatically false credit. A multiple-choice symbol quiz without writing is not equivalent to this activity. A child-controlled accessible insertion into a real writing product must be judged by actual action and provenance, not rejected solely because the child uses technology.

REVISIT and optional development

On a later available day, return to the same meaningful note or a new interest. Ask whether the child wants to add or change anything. A new context can explore transfer; it is not a prerequisite for beginning experimentation. Record a later revisit only if it occurs. DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md describes optional teaching directions rather than compulsory stages or a developmental ranking. One dot does not establish retention or mastery of all punctuation.

CLASSROOM: one teacher, several learners

For N participating learners prepare at least N usable writing surfaces and N writing tools, a few spare sheets, one teacher model and a private observation record. Use existing safely supervised writing time with all learners in view. Begin with a brief invitation/model, then offer personal notes, collaborative writing or drawing-and-dictating as meaningful participation. No compulsory circle, chorus or separate unsupervised center is necessary. Choose two or three willing focal learners to observe closely while maintaining whole-class supervision; these are planning examples, not tested capacity guarantees. Rotate attention at later opportunities. If helping one learner prevents safe supervision, stop recording and assist/supervise first; mark others not observed. Do not promise a complete individual assessment in one session.

A shared note can contain genuine contributions from several children; privately record which child added which mark and what help they received. Group attendance, a finished display, a peer's punctuation or a choral answer cannot substitute for attributable evidence. Offer a quiet nearby place within supervision, accessible tools and a familiar language partner where available, never require another adult or make peers responsible for safety. Keep notes local under setting privacy rules, not public displays or this repository. A child can stop, observe or return without losing access to the group.

Other delivery routes and limits

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: the sequence above, contingent on one child's interest, accessible authorship and actual responses; see companion facilitator guide. CO-PLAY: the adult and child may compose together, with each contribution honestly attributed. Facilitator guide — A Note About Something I Notice

ADULT-LED preparation and ENTRY invitation

One adult with one child: two blank sheets, one washable broad marker or the child's established accessible writing tool, one adult sheet and a private observation scrap. Choose a safe familiar object, available picture or view; no new artwork, outdoor trip or unfamiliar item is necessary. Never look at the sun or handle unknown plants. An accessible description, large object or remembered observation is equally useful.

Ask “What have you noticed? Shall we make a note?” Let the child choose the subject, paper, language and whether to join. If they want a model first, give one. Observe existing writing naturally, not as an obligatory pretest. Allow roughly 5–10 minutes overall, less or more according to interest and support needs.

MODEL and DURING: demonstrate, then follow the child

On your paper write about your observation and say, “I can try a dot to show where my thought stops.” Add it. Invite a mark in their own or shared writing. A child can add punctuation to words an adult wrote with them; describe the prompt honestly. Keep the model available if useful. There is no compulsory independent exit on a new subject.

If the child draws only, respond to the picture and offer a note beside it or shared writing. If marks seem decorative, ask what the child is doing without putting an answer in their mouth; record uncertainty if needed. If the child adds dots after many words or in unusual places, accept the beginning experiment without requiring conventional accuracy. If the child declines, stop without penalty. If the tool blocks participation, offer established access help, ask before touching and separate the child's direction from the adult's physical action.

EXIT: what observations mean

Record writing context, learner contribution, exact help, access support, uncertainty and next step. “Added dots after I suggested trying them in our leaf note” is prompted experimentation. It is not independent mastery, but it is not disqualified for teacher-written words. “Watched me add every dot” does not show the child's punctuation action. Do not infer intent solely from a dot's shape or require an oral grammar explanation. Home language, gesture, sign and AAC may convey intention.

REVISIT: finish kindly and return when useful

End when the child is finished, tired or disengaged. Return to the same note or a new interest another day if useful. A future revisit remains unobserved until it actually occurs. No standard requires every punctuation type, five stages, new contexts or a completed worksheet.

CLASSROOM: one teacher with multiple children

Prepare one writing surface/tool per participating learner, spares, one model and private observation notes. Work in an existing supervised writing area, all learners in view; personal notes, collaboration and drawing remain meaningful choices. Briefly model, then circulate or offer help within sight of the group. Observe a few focal learners while supervision permits, rotate attention later, and record unobserved learners honestly. Stop documentation if safety or support needs take priority. A shared product does not show each child's contribution unless actually observed and attributed. Peers may collaborate but are not substitute supervisors.

CO-PLAY: shared writing with honest attribution

A caregiver and child may compose together using the same safe materials and voluntary invitation. The adult may write shared words while the child experiments with punctuation. Record who added each mark and which prompts or access help were used; neither shared words nor a teaching prompt invalidates the child's actual contribution. Do not credit adult-made punctuation to the child.

Source and boundaries
