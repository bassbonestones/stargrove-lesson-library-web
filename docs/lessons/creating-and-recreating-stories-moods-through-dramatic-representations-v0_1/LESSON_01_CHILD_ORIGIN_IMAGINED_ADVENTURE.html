Pretend a little adventure together

Help your child show a pretend moment in their own way. They might talk as a character, move a hand as a boat, use a communication device to give a character a line, or direct you to move a toy. A familiar scene counts too. This activity is aligned with a Texas prekindergarten-four dramatic-play goal; it is not a readiness test.

Before you start

Allow about 8–15 minutes, but a shorter enjoyable moment is fine. Clear a stable seated or floor space where you can both move safely. You need no purchased kit, screen, costume or printout. Hands and imagination are enough. If wanted, offer two or three large lightweight familiar objects, such as a soft toy and a broad paper map. Leave out ties, small loose pieces, face coverings and anything that invites climbing or unsafe wrapping.

Keep the child's usual communication supports within reach. Offer a comfortable position and quiet option. Agree how they can say or signal stop. The optional linked family printables are adult aids: page 1 fits this activity; pages 2–3 are other activities, not extra requirements. You can simply use these directions without printing.

Invite an idea

“Would you like to pretend something is happening? You can use your hands, your voice, a toy, or tell me how to move it.” Give time. The child can choose a familiar shop, a tiny journey, something from a story, or something entirely different. They do not need to invent a character before you help. If they are already pretending, join gently rather than interrupting for every step below.

If choosing is hard, ask whether they want to see your idea. Briefly open an imaginary umbrella: “I'm opening my umbrella because it's raining in my pretend story. My action shows an umbrella even though it isn't really here.” Use a safe small movement and describe it accessibly. Then give the child room to choose. They can borrow your idea; meaningful re-creation is welcome.

Follow the play

Let the child show a moment. Respond to what they mean: “Your boat stopped. What happens now?” Do not decide the plot for them. One action can be enough; if they want more, invite another moment. A child who changes the bird into a bus is changing their story, not getting it wrong.

A quiet gesture, signed dialogue, role voice or purposeful direction of a toy can show the idea. If they want you to move a prop, ask how and follow their direction without adding the answer. You do not need a spoken explanation when their meaning is already clear. When unsure, ask gently or leave your interpretation uncertain.

If they only tell you about a scene, invite them to show a little part or direct a toy. Speaking as the character may already be dramatic play; don't demand extra body movement. A drawing can become scenery, but the drawing alone doesn't show dramatic enactment. If they watch, decline or get tired, stop or offer a familiar quiet pretend routine another time. Never require a public show or a real personal emotional story.

Notice what your child chooses

When they are comfortable, say, “Show me a bit you want me to see,” and stop giving plot or movement instructions. They can continue the same scene or choose another. Keep communication and physical supports.

Notice what they portrayed, how they showed it, and what help you gave. If they direct a toy under a pretend shelter and speak as it, that is meaningful dramatic action. If you tell every line and move every prop while they watch, you have modeled, not observed their own portrayal. Repeating a familiar line in a meaningful role isn't automatically wrong. A single moment is not proof of mastery or lasting learning.

Finish and come back

Let the child choose to stop. You can ask what they enjoyed without demanding an answer. Later, on another real day, offer this or another pretend invitation. Don't count that return before it happens. If you keep a note, make it factual and private: “directed toy under shelter; adult steadied toy,” rather than a score. No home assignment, recording or sharing is required.

Pretend little adventures with a class

Children create or recreate a pretend moment using role voice, gesture, movement, signed or communication-device dialogue, or purposeful prop direction. Familiar scenes and borrowed ideas are welcome. This is a Texas prekindergarten-four dramatic-play opportunity, not an originality, acting-skill or school-readiness test.

Prepare for one teacher and several children

Plan a brief invitation and about 10–15 minutes of flexible play within your usual supervised routine. Do not add a separate unsupervised drama corner. Use in-place seated/floor play or established visible play spaces you can genuinely supervise. No costumes, printouts, stage, screen or purchased supplies are needed. Hands, voices and imaginary props work. If using objects, offer at most two or three large lightweight familiar safe props per play space; if sharing would cause waiting or conflict, use no-prop versions instead of manufacturing scarcity.

Clear collision paths. Remove choking-size pieces, neck ties, face coverings and climbing invitations. Keep each child's normal communication/access supports available. Choose quiet, seated, nonspoken and private-within-supervision options, and explain how to stop. If a child's access needs require more direct support than you can safely provide with the group, arrange another supported opportunity; don't invent a second adult or mark the child unsuccessful.

The optional linked family printables are adult planning/record aids. Page 1 belongs to this activity; pages 2–3 are different routes, not extra assessment requirements. Use paper notes if easier, store real child records privately, and never upload them to the lesson site.

Invite without requiring a performance

“Today you can pretend a little moment. You might use your hands, your voice, a toy, or tell me how to move something. You can choose your own idea, watch, or take a break.” Children can play individually or with willing partners; no assigned partner or public stage is necessary. Agree that others' bodies and props are not touched without permission. Respect different home languages and ordinary familiar pretend roles.

If useful, give one short model: open an imaginary umbrella and say, “My action shows an umbrella in my pretend rain.” Describe it accessibly and keep the movement small. Do not require children to produce ideas before seeing teaching. They may meaningfully recreate that scene or choose something else; don't police novelty. Skip the model if it would interrupt already meaningful child-led play.

Keep everyone meaningfully occupied

Invite children to begin small scenes where they are safely visible. Others can play their own quiet scenes, build optional scenery for their later play, watch voluntarily or use an established quiet activity. Scenery-making and watching are worthwhile participation, but do not alone prove dramatic representation. Partners can negotiate a shared scene with consent; one assertive child must not decide everyone else's role.

For a manageable observation round, choose two or three focal children whose safe opportunities you can actually see. This is a planning example, not a required quota or class-size claim. Keep scanning the whole group; pause note-taking and help when safety or participation needs attention. If voices, movement or prop use overwhelm supervision, simplify to seated/no-prop play, shorten the session or defer individual observation. Never keep an unsafe arrangement just to finish a record.

Respond to their meaning

Ask a focal child, “What happens next?” only if it helps their play. Accept a small gesture, role voice, signed/AAC line, or a child-directed adult movement of a prop. A child may change the plot or use a familiar role. If meaning is unclear, ask gently or record uncertainty, not an invented story or an oral-language failure.

For narration without enacted meaning, offer, “Would you like to show a little part or tell me how to move the toy?” Speaking in role can already be dramatic representation. For copying, look for purposeful re-creation rather than rejecting it automatically. If the adult has supplied all actions, describe that as adult-led rehearsal and offer a later child-choice moment. Never require a stereotyped face, loud voice, public explanation or real distress disclosure.

Observe individuals without testing the whole group at once

At a natural moment, invite a focal child to show a bit they choose. Remove answer-leading plot/movement cues but keep their access supports. The scene can remain familiar. Note the represented meaning, actual action, child decisions and adult/peer help. Child-directed prop movement can be relevant even when the adult supplies the physical movement.

A class chorus, audience applause, attendance or a complete group story does not show what each child did. If another child supplied the role and actions, record that context; supported participation is not automatically independent invention. If you did not see an individual opportunity, write “not observed,” not “failed.” Rotate focal attention across later real sessions so quieter children are not perpetually invisible; avoid a public tracking board or comparison ranking.

Close and return

Give a calm stopping cue and help put optional props away. Children may share if they choose; no compulsory finale. Use actual observations to plan another day: perhaps fewer props, a familiar routine, a different communication route or an optional second story moment. Later retention or transfer remains unobserved until a real later opportunity occurs. No extra home practice, caregiver report or recording is required.

01 — Pretend a little adventure

Learning target, source and scope

The child creates or recreates a meaningful moment through dramatic representation: pretending with role voice, gesture, movement, sign, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), or a purposefully directed prop/partner. This lesson invites an imagined adventure, but a familiar role or re-created episode is a valid response. It is not an originality contest or a test of speaking, athletic movement, facial expression, memorization or public confidence.

Exact 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines source, PK4.VIII.C.1: “Child creates or recreates stories, moods, or experiences through dramatic representations.” Official PDF printed pages 73–74 show no PK3 outcome for this domain and examples of acting out a read-aloud, creating a story with classmates, using props and re-enacting real-life events. The recorded source-cell SHA-256 is 65c74d87275652b9e9df4a721b1a8b83d73c1a69769a6118b605b13326a265dc (see source concordance for verified full value). The printed guide's developmental, non-checklist limitation applies: one opportunity is not a readiness verdict or a diagnosis. This is a PK4-aligned written design; younger children can join accessible pretend play without inventing a PK3 standard.

The source uses creates OR recreates and stories OR moods OR experiences. One meaningful dramatic portrayal can provide relevant evidence; the child need not demonstrate all six combinations. This lesson focuses on playful story/experience enactment. An ordinary safe event such as opening a pretend shop can replace fantasy. Do not require family history or real emotional disclosures. This is one component of a balanced curriculum, not full Fine Arts coverage.

Materials and PREPARATION

For one adult and child, allow roughly 8–15 flexible minutes, or stop after a satisfying short moment. Prepare a clear, stable floor or seated space where both can move arms without colliding. No special purchase or printout is required. Hands, role voice and an imaginary object are enough. Optionally offer two or three familiar, large, lightweight objects, for example a soft toy and a broad piece of paper as a pretend map. Avoid neck ties, face coverings, small loose pieces, trip hazards and furniture climbing. If a cloth would invite unsafe wrapping, omit it. Follow the setting's safety and accessibility plans rather than assuming one prop suits everyone.

Check that the child's usual communication system is present and within reach, not locked away as a test accommodation. Offer a comfortable position, quiet area within adult sight, and a clear stop signal. The child may watch, change the story, or decline. Choose adult model examples that do not require unfamiliar visual knowledge or a culturally prescribed expression. Explain/descriptively demonstrate the action in the child's accessible mode.

Optional family printables are adult planning/recording aids, not worksheets. For this lesson use the first page only of the scene invitations or observation form. Pages 2–3 concern different routes and do not expand this lesson's target. All pages are optional; paper notes stored privately are sufficient. Do not place real child records in Git or on the website. See the two reader paths for practical organization. No supplied story text is required: the child authors or re-creates their chosen meaning.

ENTRY — invite, notice, then decide whether a model helps

Say, “Would you like to pretend something is happening? You can use your hands, your voice, a toy, or tell me how to move it.” Wait without inventing a role for the child. An everyday familiar event is welcome. If the child starts playing, follow their meaning rather than interrupting to deliver all phases. A child pretending to open a heavy door may push with a hand, make a sound or direct a prop. Notice actual enacted meaning rather than silently interpreting an arbitrary movement as a story.

If choosing is hard, do not insist on a character before teaching. Offer to show a brief example or invite the child to choose between two broad options and an “anything else” option. Label any adult-supplied idea honestly. A choice from options can still lead to the child's own meaningful portrayal; it is not automatically invalid. Watching alone is participation and preparation, not evidence of the child's dramatic representation.

MODEL — make the pretend meaning visible, without prescribing a correct answer

Ask, “Would you like to see my idea?” Briefly pretend to open an imaginary umbrella as gentle rain begins: a small hand movement and “I'm opening my umbrella.” Explain, “There isn't an umbrella here; my action shows one in my pretend story.” Use a safe seated movement and describe it if the child cannot see it. The example is optional, short and unrelated to an already chosen child plot. It is not a compulsory sequence or a required novel gesture.

The child may re-create the model meaningfully. That shows a supported/re-created portrayal if the actual evidence warrants it, not proof of wholly independent invention. Do not automatically reject an echoed line or familiar movement: look for purposeful pretend use, not surface novelty. If the adult directs every movement and meaning, record adult-led rehearsal rather than independently authored dramatic decisions.

DURING — follow a child-owned scene and respond contingently

Invite, “What happens in your adventure?” Then allow a moment of play. A child may make a tiny boat with a hand, speak as a shopkeeper, direct an adult to move a toy through a pretend doorway, or use AAC to give a character a line. The child can change the role midscene. One meaningful action is a useful entry level; two connected moments are an optional extension, not a prerequisite.

Respond to the meaning: “Your boat stopped. What might happen now?” Avoid supplying a rescue plot or demanding a named emotion. If the child says the motion represents a bird rather than your guessed boat, accept the correction. Speech is not required to explain intent: familiar context, the child's repeated purposeful action, directed prop choices, sign or AAC can make meaning clear. If meaning remains uncertain, ask gently or record uncertainty; do not force a verbal explanation to count accessible enactment.

If the child describes a story but has not dramatized it, say, “Would you like to show a little part, or have me move the toy how you choose?” Offer a demonstration if wanted. A voice speaking as a character may itself be drama; do not require extra body movement. Static drawing can prepare scenery but is not alone evidence of dramatic representation. Selecting an AAC phrase or switch that the child intentionally uses as a character's act can be relevant; pressing an adult-prescribed animation without meaningful choice is not enough by itself.

If the child copies your umbrella, ask what is happening in their version and follow their response. Re-creation is source-valid. Only call it independent invention if observed decisions justify that narrower claim. If the child disengages, return to a familiar pretend routine, shorten the invitation, offer quiet witnessing or stop. Do not infer inability from refusal, fatigue, language difference or inaccessible materials.

EXIT — a fresh observation opportunity, not a compulsory new story

When interest and time allow, say, “Show me a bit you want me to see.” Let the child continue the same adventure, re-create a familiar moment or choose another scene. Observe without supplying the next meaning/action. Keep normal communication and motor supports. The check is separate from the adult's immediate answer-leading coaching; it need not be a new setting or a never-seen story.

Record a brief factual note: what the child represented, how it was enacted, what the child decided, and what the adult or peers supplied. Example of relevant evidence: the child directs a toy under a pretend rain shelter, then speaks as the toy, “I'm dry.” Example of False credit: the teacher places every prop and dictates every movement, then credits independent child story creation merely because the child follows along. Class laughter, applause or a complete shared scene is not individual evidence.

Use descriptive states: observed child-directed portrayal; supported portrayal with specific assistance; adult-directed rehearsal; uncertain meaning; or not observed. These are not ranks or mastery labels. A single source-relevant portrayal is not comprehensive standard mastery, retention, generalization or proof of teaching effectiveness. Familiarity is a description, not an automatic failure. A later changed-context opportunity may explore transfer when useful, but it is not a source eligibility gate.

REVISIT — return when appropriate

End with the child's choice to pause or share one thing they enjoyed, without a required summary. On an actually later day, offer the scene again or a different meaningful pretend invitation. Record that later event only after it happens; currently REVISIT is NOT_OBSERVED. No exact calendar interval or daily performance quota is required. Use actual interests and prior support notes to choose the next challenge.

Developmental possibilities, not a compulsory staircase

See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for family context. PK4 P4-1: one meaningful pretend action. P4-2: child links two moments if useful. P4-3: re-creates a familiar story event. P4-4: voluntarily portrays a fictional mood or safe experience, with no facial stereotype. P4-5: chooses and sustains meaning in a changed context with support documented. These are author-designed teaching possibilities, not five official levels every child must pass in order. A child may start with a familiar role-play scene, move between possibilities, or use a smaller movement without lower conceptual meaning.

Four written modes and two present human routes

SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED. A future app might support pretend play; no functioning independent software or observed digital parity is claimed. CO-PLAY: a caregiver follows the child's plot and can act as a neutral physical partner at the child's direction. ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: use the invitation, optional model, responsive play and honest observation above, with the plain-language one-on-one guide as the practical entry. CLASSROOM: use one visible whole-group invitation and safe in-place or familiar supervised play. One teacher observes a few focal children while maintaining sight and responsibility for everyone. Do not promise all children can be assessed during one short session; others continue meaningful own play and receive focal attention over later opportunities. If supervision or individual access cannot be maintained, reduce activity complexity or defer focal observation, not safety. The classroom guide gives quantities and rotation details.

Safeguards and family/community connection

No costumes, audience, partner, emotional display or real-life disclosure is compulsory. Use culturally flexible pretend choices, honor home language and familiar role scripts, and avoid correcting a family's ordinary expression to match the teacher's drama style. Spontaneous familiar characters are not a failure; educators must use appropriately permitted material if distributing published scripts/images. An optional ordinary family conversation about what the child enjoys pretending can help planning, but no home assignment, device, caregiver performance or disclosure is required for participation.

Accessible drama retains expressive ownership, not a uniform motor form. The adult can steady or move a prop neutrally at the child's direction; document that physical assistance separately from who chose the meaning. Keep sensory supports, positioning, communication access and extra time while reducing answer-leading prompts. Apply actual child plans and professional advice where needed; these written alternatives are not validated equivalents for every learner.

Facilitator guide — Pretend a little adventure

Use the paired technical lesson and the two website teaching paths. The target is meaningful DRAMATIC representation, not originality or public performance. PK4.VIII.C.1 permits creates OR recreates and story OR mood OR experience. No official PK3 outcome is invented.

Prepare and invite

One adult and child need a clear stable seated/floor space and their ordinary communication supports. No objects or printing required. Optionally provide two or three large lightweight familiar safe props. Remove trip/choking/wrapping hazards. Plan 8–15 flexible minutes, not a compulsory duration. For classroom delivery, use in-place or familiar visible play and observe a few focal children, not an unsupervised performance corner. Check actual supervision and access needs first.

ENTRY

Ask what the child would like to pretend. Notice spontaneous purposeful enactment. The child may choose an everyday familiar scene, watch or decline. Do not demand a role before providing teaching. If they cannot choose, offer an optional model or two broad possibilities plus their own option. Record adult-supplied ideas without discarding all subsequent child meaning.

MODEL

If helpful, briefly open an imaginary umbrella and explain that the action represents a pretend object. Use a small safe movement, accessible description and ordinary supports. Avoid overwriting an existing child plot. The child may meaningfully re-create the model; this is not automatically failure. Separate supported re-creation from an unearned claim of independently invented story.

DURING

Follow the child's drama. Ask what happens next only when it supports rather than interrupts play. Accept role voice, signed/AAC dialogue, purposeful puppet direction, small movement or a quiet scene. If the child only describes a story, offer to show a moment or move a prop at their direction; voice in a character role may already be dramatization. If intent is unclear, ask or record uncertainty, not an invented interpretation. Do not require spoken explanation, a novel plot, a face displaying a stereotyped feeling or the teacher's gesture.

EXIT

Invite a moment the child wants to show, in the same or another scene. Withdraw answer-leading cues, not access supports. Record actual child meaning/action and adult/peer help. Familiar play and re-created episodes can be source-relevant. Distinguish observed child-directed portrayal, supported portrayal, adult-directed rehearsal, uncertainty and not observed. No all-category test, novelty gate or pass/fail readiness verdict.

REVISIT

Stop when the child is done, fatigued or no longer comfortable; a short meaningful moment can suffice. Return on a later actual day if appropriate, using prior interests and support notes. Until then REVISIT remains NOT_OBSERVED. Changed context is an optional transfer exploration, not proof that all same-context enactment was invalid.

Practical materials and records

Optional three-page family invitations/observation forms are adult aids. Use page 1 for this lesson; pages 2–3 belong to other routes. They do not add required tasks. No printing is necessary. Store real observations privately under the setting's practice, never in public repository files. Record support and uncertainty rather than a score. Shared family guidance is subordinate to this repaired lesson's source-faithful inference boundary when historical sibling prose differs.

Responsive decision examples

Child acts meaningfully: acknowledge meaning without ranking theatrical skill. Child recreates your rain scene: follow their interpretation; record teaching context, not plagiarism. Child directs an adult prop: credit only decisions actually chosen by the child; motor assistance is not conceptual takeover. Child repeats a familiar line: look for purposeful dramatic role, not vocabulary novelty. Adult dictates everything: record adult-led rehearsal, invite later child choice. Child watches or declines: respect it, record not observed, offer another time; no diagnosis.

CLASSROOM and other routes

CLASSROOM: one teacher introduces an optional short model, then children pretend safely where supervision remains possible. No compulsory audience, partnered role or isolated corner. Focal observations rotate fairly over actual sessions; unobserved children remain not observed, not failed. Reduce props/movement or stop the activity if safety conflicts with note-taking.
