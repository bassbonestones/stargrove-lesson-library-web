Real CAT–HAT–BAT sound bridges

Put two sounds together: cat, hat and bat

What your child is learning

Play with the first sound and the rest of a spoken English word. In preschool 3, the goal is joining sound-blending play with support. In preschool 4, the goal is identifying a familiar one-syllable English word from its two chunks. Naming letters or saying every sound separately is a different task. These are teaching goals, not a test of school readiness.

Before you begin

You need one adult, one child and your voice. Choose a quiet-enough place and plan about 5–8 minutes, less if your child prefers. No printer, phone, puppet, second adult or purchased materials is required. Cat, hat and bat work if their meanings are already familiar. Otherwise teach meanings warmly or choose another known word; not knowing English vocabulary is not a diagnosis. Bat might mean an animal or a sporting object, so agree which one you mean.

Optional supports: two hand positions for the sounds; a whole-page neutral sound mat; two or three equally clear familiar pictures or safe large objects; the child's usual communication system. The included neutral mat is not a finished set of picture answers. You may draw simple options and check their meanings in ordinary talk, or use familiar objects/sign/AAC instead. Keep small loose parts away. Pictures may be visible throughout, provided you do not single out the answer. Never remove needed AAC or hearing support to make a task seem independent.

Invite, show and play

“Want to join my sound mystery? You can listen, join me, point, tell me, or take a break.” Model first if that helps: “/m/ ... ap ... map!” Say the first sound, then the entire ending. Put your hands together only if useful. No child response is owed before your model.

Try the brief sound /k/ from cat, then say “at” as one chunk. Do not say the letter name C, add “uh,” or split it into k-a-t. Ask, “What word do those sounds make?” and wait comfortably. The child may name it, sign it, use AAC, say a meaningful home-language label, or choose from balanced known pictures. For a younger learner, joining your “c-at, cat” sound play or adding an agreed playful gesture is enough participation; do not require a correct independent answer.

After a response, say the word together and enjoy its meaning. Change the beginning: /h/ ... at, hat; /b/ ... at, bat. Ask which familiar word or theme the child wants to try. Repeating known words is welcome. Stop after a few enjoyable turns rather than chasing a correct-answer total.

If the response surprises you

  • “Hat” after /k/ ... at: first check audibility and word meaning. “I heard hat. Listen: /k/ ... at. Together, cat.” Co-blend, then invite another comfortable turn. The immediate corrected echo is practice, not independent identification.
  • The child taps without attending to the sounds: enjoy the movement but do not infer word identification. Reconnect the gesture to the sound play or offer another route.
  • The word is unknown: teach its meaning, do not score inability.
  • The child needs a repeat: replay both chunks without pointing to the answer. Neutral replay is access, not necessarily an answer hint.
  • Noise, fatigue or uncertainty: quiet the setting, model together, let the child listen or stop. No forced speech, accent correction or public performance.

A useful closing turn

Offer one voluntary familiar word from its chunks. A word taught before can be valid evidence of current identification. If you just said the whole answer or showed only the correct item, note supported practice instead of independent identification. A balanced picture/AAC array is a legitimate response route, not cheating. A plausible guess or ambiguous gaze remains uncertain; do not label mastery from one lucky choice. PK3 participation may be supported, including copying your model.

If useful, privately note the actual chunks, child's response, choices available and what help you gave. Keep access support, answer-bearing help and uncertainty separate. No observation means “not observed,” not “cannot.” Do not upload child names or responses to the lesson repository.

Another day, or another willing moment

Return for pleasure or practice when useful. An additional same-session turn is practice, not proof of lasting retention. To explore transfer, use a different known word or rime without feeding the answer; this is optional, not a condition for today's learning. Try a child's silly invented word as play, but do not call it familiar real-word identification.

Access and source notes

English sound chunks are the task; the response need not be spoken English. Use quiet, established sign/AAC/gaze, safe large familiar choices and the child's usual hearing/communication supports. If the relevant sound contrast cannot be accessed, use an appropriate individualized support plan or teach a related accessible language goal, without claiming this sound task was demonstrated. Other-language sound games are valued under their own goals.

Source: Texas Education Agency, 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, pp.34–35, PK3.III.B.8 and PK4.III.B.8. Page 35 explicitly allows choosing from several pictures; p.8 warns that the guidelines are not an assessment checklist. Teach a class: sound bridges without a chorus test

Goal and group arrangement

PK3 learners join English onset–rime sound play with support. PK4 learners identify a familiar one-syllable word from its beginning and intact ending. You are one teacher responsible for the whole group. Use a brief seated or otherwise safely visible shared game; do not create unsupervised centers merely to collect scores. Individual word evidence comes from an attributable response, not the class chorus.

Prepare

Your voice and familiar English words suffice. Cat, hat and bat share the ending “at”; choose other known words if needed. No second adult, device, purchased kit or printer required. Optional: your adult-only word script, one whole-page neutral sound mat, a private rotation note and balanced familiar picture/object or established AAC options for the focal learner. Large safe objects or adult-drawn recognizable choices can substitute; neutral blank spaces are not a supplied picture vocabulary. Keep answer text out of reading children's view and small loose items away. Check sound level, audibility and usual hearing/communication supports before relying on a response.

A planning example: 2 minutes of modeled/shared sound play, 3–5 minutes for a few focal turns while the group stays engaged, then a brief closing turn. Adjust to attention, needs and supervision; this is not a tested class-size guarantee. Some children can listen or use a quiet gesture. No child must answer publicly or wait through every other child's test.

Launch with everyone

“Let's join two sounds. You can listen, join, show me, or take a break.” Say /m/ ... ap, then map, joining your hands if welcome. You can model the whole sequence yourself; no second adult or puppet is needed. The group may co-blend immediately. Say /k/ ... at, then invite a playful response. Keep “at” together; don't spell C-A-T or say three separate phonemes. Use a brief /k/, not “kuh.” Shift to /h/ ... at and /b/ ... at to hear the beginning change.

Younger children may join the rime, imitate your blend or coordinate an agreed action with the sound game. Do not demand PK4 independent accuracy from them. A silent observer is welcome, but attendance alone is not evidence of participated blending. Older children can identify the word by voice, sign, AAC, meaningful home-language label or choosing among familiar pictures. Picture arrays may be visible before the question; do not highlight or name just the target.

Make focal turns fair and feasible

Choose a few willing focal learners, keeping all children in view. Invite the others to listen for the sound change or quietly practice their own bridge rather than shout the answer. Do not demand silence from the whole class as a prerequisite for playful learning. If others supply the focal answer, record shared practice and defer that individual identification inference. Do not assume the first loud child represents everyone.

Keep a private list of children offered usable opportunities so quiet learners and those needing longer communication time are not missed. Return in later sessions or an existing safely supervised routine. If noise, waiting or divided attention makes the focal turn unworkable, return to shared play or end. Do not promise to assess every child in one sitting or leave the rest of the class unsupervised. Children who decline can listen and rejoin without losing rewards or status.

Respond to what you see

If a child says hat after /k/ ... at, first check whether the chunks and meaning were accessible. “I heard hat. Listen: /k/ ... at, cat.” Model, practice together and invite another uncued response if appropriate. The corrected echo is supported learning, not independent identification. If a word is unfamiliar, explore meaning rather than label a blending deficit. Retain replay, sign, gaze/AAC and hearing support; removing access is not fading a conceptual hint. If children race or become uncomfortable, slow down or stop rather than count speed.

A child choosing cat from several known options after the chunks can provide identification evidence. A sole visible cat, teacher-pointed option or adult-selected AAC answer cannot establish independence. A familiar taught word may still yield a genuine current response; an unheard word is not mandatory. For PK3, modeled participation can be valid. When evidence is uncertain, describe uncertainty rather than mark success or failure.

Close, record and return

Offer one final willing shared or individual sound turn. End while participation remains worthwhile. A short private note can record focal child's exact response, actual onset and rime, available choices, neutral access and any answer-bearing adult/peer help. No public ranking, recordings, uploaded child records or readiness scores. Unobserved children remain not observed, not failed.

Return for practice when useful. A same-session repeat is not proof of durable retention. A genuinely changed word/rime can explore transfer if it remains familiar and accessible; keep that stronger claim separate from basic current participation/identification. Children may choose themes or silly sound inventions as optional enjoyment; nonsense words are not PK4 familiar-word evidence.

Access and boundaries

English phonology is the task, not English expressive speech or accent conformity. Honor home-language response, sign, gaze, AAC, quiet, large familiar options and individualized hearing/communication plans. If sound contrasts cannot be accessed, teach a related accessible language activity and defer this exact inference. No forced eye contact, public speaking or specialist diagnosis. This focused activity leaves room for conversation, stories, movement and other play; it is not an entire literacy program.

Real CAT–HAT–BAT sound bridges

Target and original source

PK3.III.B.8: “Child participates in onset-rime blending activities (in English only).” PK4.III.B.8: “Child identifies a familiar one-syllable word that is segmented by onset and rime (in English only).”

Source: Texas Education Agency, 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, original comprehensive guide, printed pp.34–35; archived at education/planning/source_documents/TEA_2022_PK3_PK4_COMPREHENSIVE_GUIDE_ORIGINAL.pdf. Page 35 explicitly permits selecting an appropriate picture from several pictures after segmented input. Page 8 says the guidelines are not a curriculum, assessment tool or checklist. Use these targets to plan responsive teaching, not diagnose, rank children or decide school readiness. Research-cell hashes remain f1254f9bbbebb775c1c9a51667f28d57c4744575eb4c0f1754bd7f3da4cd2ce3 (PK3) and 0597b22ca850b9dfa4a57f2ad438408ae97f14775017e1b9cb08e636078f246f (PK4). Publisher text examined directly during DOT21 R01; this does not qualify the repaired lesson.

The mechanism is hearing the beginning sound and the intact rest of a word, joining them, then changing the beginning while retaining the rest: c-at, h-at, b-at. This is not a three-phoneme c-a-t task, letter naming, spelling, rhyming judgment or syllable counting. Spoken English supplies the task; a meaningful signed, home-language, AAC, pointing or gaze response may identify the English referent without demanding English speech. Other-language sound games are valuable separately, not mislabeled English phonology.

PK3 participates in supported play, including imitation, joining the rime, coordinating an optional gesture with the sound game or requesting another sound turn. A model does not disqualify that participation. Passive presence alone is insufficient to infer engagement: describe what was actually noticed without diagnosing quiet listening. PK4 identifies a familiar one-syllable word from the onset and rime. The child need not invent a word, select the teacher's target, pronounce perfectly, segment the word or encounter a never-before-taught item. A learned familiar word can support a current identification claim. Broader transfer and delayed retention require their own evidence.

Materials, setup and safety

One adult, one child, a quiet-enough space, and the adult's voice suffice. No device, printer, puppet, cutout or second adult required. Optional: one adult script (ORIGINAL_ONSET_RIME_ADULT_AUDIO_MATS.html page 1), one whole-page two-spot gesture mat (page 2), and a private paper note or observation form. Do not cut small pieces. Optional balanced response choices: two or three large familiar pictures/objects or the child's established AAC vocabulary, equally available without a highlighted answer. Use safe large objects already in the setting, not small toys. This package supplies neutral sound spaces, not a finished picture vocabulary set. Adult can draw recognizable simple alternatives and check their meaning in ordinary talk before using them; if pictures are unclear, use words/sign/AAC or familiar safe objects instead.

Know which English words the child understands from ordinary conversation or a separate vocabulary-teaching moment. Do not demand a vocabulary test before allowing play. If cat, hat or bat is unfamiliar, teach its meaning warmly or use a familiar substitute with a real onset and intact rime. “Bat” can mean the animal or a sporting implement; establish the intended meaning without assuming family experiences. Avoid confusing vocabulary knowledge with blending. Printed symbols below are adult pronunciation reminders, not child reading demands. Say the short /k/ in cat without adding “uh,” then say “at” as one continuous chunk. For hat, use /h/ then “at”; for bat, /b/ then “at.” Use natural local English pronunciation and a brief audible gap, not distorted stretching. The rest of sun is “un,” not separate vowel and n. If uncertain about producing a sound contrast accessibly, teach with a trusted fluent speaker or appropriate specialist support rather than certify an unheard contrast.

Keep the adult answer script out of a reading child's response view. A balanced array may be visible before and during a question; do not point to, enlarge, name only or otherwise reveal the answer. Do not hide a child's communication system to remove cues. Hearing/vision/communication supports stay available. An access barrier leads to a changed opportunity, not a deficit label. No recording, public scores or uploading child names to the repository.

ENTRY — invite a sound mystery, not an examination

Allow roughly 5–8 minutes, shorter if interest or comfort changes. “Want to help me put two sounds together? You can listen first, join me, point, tell me, or take a break.” The child may choose voice or a small hand movement. If already interested and comfortable, try /k/ ... /at/ and notice a response. This initial unmodeled turn is optional; model first when helpful. Neither withheld readiness nor refusing a test blocks the lesson. Let the child choose a familiar theme or object; the adult remains responsible for supplying valid English sound chunks.

MODEL — one adult makes the joining audible

“I say /m/ ... /ap/. When I put them together, map!” Speak both chunks, bring hands together if useful, and say the whole word. No second adult or independent puppet actor is needed. A child can join immediately. This is teaching, not evidence that the child independently identified map. Using a different model from the next probe can reduce immediate copying; it is not an absolute requirement never to reuse taught words. If map is unfamiliar, choose a known word instead. Do not insist the child copy a gesture.

DURING — real playful turns and responsive feedback

Offer /k/ ... /at/. PK3 may join the adult's blending, add the rime, request a repeat or use an agreed meaningful action in the sound play. PK4 may name/sign the referent, use familiar AAC, or select it from the balanced array. Wait comfortably rather than time the child. Replay the same two chunks if requested; replay is not automatically answer-bearing support. After the response, say the whole word together and explore its meaning if desired. Offer /h/ ... /at/ or /b/ ... /at/ to change the first part while keeping “at.” Invite “Which sound shall we try?” but do not score target selection as the learning goal. Let familiar word play, a child's discovered rhyme, or a preferred theme shape turns without requiring a fixed quota.

If the child says hat after /k/ ... /at/, do not label a disability or lack of effort. Check whether the prompt was audible and pictures understood. “I heard hat. Listen: /k/ ... /at/. Together: cat.” Model that distinction, permit shared practice, then offer another willing uncued opportunity or pause. That corrected immediate echo is supported learning, not independent PK4 identification. If child says /k/ /a/ /t/, the adult should repair their own task to two chunks, not demand phoneme segmentation. If unfamiliar vocabulary, build meaning before checking identification. If hesitant, offer listening or co-blending instead of repeated interrogation. If easy and enjoyable, try a known different rime such as /s/ ... /un/; this is optional additional challenge. Never require novelty for a basic target record.

EXIT — a proportionate individual check

End with one voluntary sound turn, on a familiar taught or untaught word. Record what happened, the exact chunks, available options, the child's response and what the adult supplied. A familiar repeated word with a genuine choice can demonstrate current identification; an immediate adult answer echo, sole-answer card or automatic selected item cannot establish independent PK4 identification. Balanced picture options and established AAC can support genuine identification; repeated positional guesses remain uncertain, so change placement or obtain another low-pressure opportunity rather than declare mastery. PK3 supported participation is legitimate even with the adult's whole-word model; do not impose PK4 accuracy or independence.

Use plain-language notes: observed participation; child identification from chunks; supported identification after answer cue; uncertain response; or no usable opportunity/not observed. An incorrect response is a factual response on this occasion, not a readiness judgment. Access support is not a conceptual hint. Record uncertainty separately from adult supplied answers. No one-turn mastery cut score, no calendar gate and no expected total of correct words. Stop while the experience remains welcome.

REVISIT — return for a real purpose

Return later when useful to enjoy the game, reinforce familiar words or introduce known words with other rimes. Record the actual interval and support if observing; another same-session turn is practice, not proof of lasting retention. A later observation can inform continuity, but does not retroactively validate an earlier record. New-context transfer is only a claim when the observed change genuinely challenges sound joining rather than merely changing decoration. All child outcomes and later observations remain NOT_OBSERVED in this authored plan.

ADULT-LED one child and CO-PLAY

Use the flow above with one adult responding closely to one child. The same language can support a caregiver-child game, with the child taking playful initiative if desired. The adult can model first, keep known words, retain communication access and allow a stop. A caregiver need not understand phonetic notation; the short sound instructions and familiar word examples are sufficient. See LESSON_01_FACILITATOR_GUIDE.md for concise planning and interpretation anchors.

CLASSROOM — one teacher, multiple children

Use a brief seated, visible-group sound game; do not send unobserved children into unsupervised stations. The teacher models one bridge and invites voluntary co-blending, listening and small in-place gestures. No child is obliged to perform publicly. Shared sound play is worthwhile practice, not individual PK4 proof. Choose a few focal learners for optional private-to-teacher responses while everyone remains within supervision. Other children can quietly rehearse the chunks or listen for the change; peers must not shout the focal answer first. If chorus or peer answers obscure ownership, record group practice and defer individual inference.

A workable planning example is 2 minutes shared modeling and play, then 3–5 minutes of a few focal turns while the group stays engaged, followed by a short closing turn. This is an adjustable design, not a field-tested class-size claim. If group noise or divided attention prevents audibility or adequate supervision, remain in shared play and schedule observations during an existing safely supervised routine, not a fictitious second-adult rotation. Keep a private rotation list so quiet children and AAC users receive opportunities across later sessions. Do not promise all individual assessments in one sitting. Children waiting to respond have a learning role; if waiting stops being useful, return to a shared round or end.

Access, belonging and sensible scope

Offer quiet and child-controlled replay, optional neutral gesture, nonvisual oral responses, large familiar picture options or established switch/gaze/AAC. Check the child can distinguish the actual sound contrast with their usual supports; a picture alone is not evidence of hearing/processing the English chunks. Use appropriate individualized hearing/communication plans, without inventing universal alternate sensory equivalence. Do not require eye contact, stillness, fast naming, English accent conformity, drawing skill or removing needed support. Accept a recognizable concept-correct home-language label after English chunks. Participation in other-language sound play remains meaningful, but is reported under its own goal.

Optional extension: the child chooses a silly character or invents a sound message. Enjoy nonsense words as play without recording them as PK4 familiar real-word identification. This focused activity is not a complete literacy curriculum or a substitute for stories, conversation, movement and other play. Family input about familiar meanings can be welcomed privately, never made compulsory homework.

Developmental continuity and false credit

See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for flexible opportunities, not prerequisite gates. PK3 O3-1: join a simple bridge; O3-2: enjoy a changed onset; O3-3: try a puppet message; O3-4: optionally extend to other words. PK4 O4-1: identify a familiar word; O4-2: notice changed onsets; O4-3: explore another rime; O4-4: use an accessible uncued opportunity; O4-5: optionally explore transfer. Children can revisit or enter any useful opportunity; no rigid ladder is earned by paperwork.

False credit: teacher chants cat and child echoes, yet observer records independent PK4 identification; teacher-selected AAC answer is credited to child; chorus substitutes for a private response; a decorative two-dot tap is called demonstrated blending without any sound-play relation. False rejection: modeled PK3 co-blending, previously taught CAT, a balanced picture array, home-language identification, or repeated access support is discarded merely for not being novel spoken English production. Describe the real action and the actual help instead.

Facilitator companion: CAT–HAT–BAT sound bridges

Read the complete plan. This companion is not a separate lesson.

Prepare and choose

One adult and voice suffice for one child or a visible group. Pick familiar English words; cat, hat and bat share the whole “at” ending. Confirm meanings through ordinary talk; if unfamiliar, teach vocabulary instead of scoring failure. No printer, second adult, letters or device required. Optional adult-only script, whole-page neutral gesture mat, balanced familiar picture/AAC choices and a private note. Avoid loose small items and uncomfortable sound levels.

Do

Invite a voluntary sound mystery. Model first if useful: “/m/ ... /ap/ ... map!” Use a small joining gesture only if welcome. Then /k/ ... /at/: two groups, not c-a-t letters or three phonemes, and no added “uh” after the consonant. Let the child join, ask for replay or identify the word. Change /k/ to /h/ while preserving “at.” No requirement to finish a set. End after a welcome turn; return later for practice or optional transfer.

Grade distinctions and source

Texas 2022 Prekindergarten Guidelines pp.34–35: PK3.III.B.8 participation in English onset-rime blending; PK4.III.B.8 identification of a familiar one-syllable English word segmented by onset and rime. P35 explicitly allows choosing from several pictures. P8 prohibits treating these guidelines as a curriculum, assessment tool or checklist. No child diagnosis, ranking or readiness cut score. A home-language label, sign or familiar AAC may identify the English referent. This task does not require English expressive pronunciation or independent segmentation.

Responses that change teaching

  • PK3 joins modeled sound play: record participation, not failure because adult helped.
  • PK4 chooses cat from a balanced array after /k/ ... /at/: current identification evidence; array may already be visible. Do not hide communication support.
  • Child echoes cat immediately after adult gives the whole answer: supported practice; not independent identification.
  • Child points to the only visible answer: cue is too strong for an independence inference; offer genuinely competing known choices or another response mode.
  • Child says hat: check audibility and meanings; model “/k/ ... /at/, cat,” practice kindly, then invite another uncued response if comfortable. No diagnosis from one mistake.
  • Child declines, cannot access the sounds, or peer answers first: no usable individual opportunity. Respect listening and defer; do not record inability.
  • Child identifies taught CAT again without a whole-answer cue: legitimate current response. Novel words are needed only for a stronger transfer claim, not basic evidence.

Record plainly

Exact spoken chunks; familiar word; options; child's own response; access support; answer-bearing adult/peer help; uncertainty; next sensible move. Use words rather than ambiguous H/X codes. Private teacher notes only, not child records in this repository. All actual child outcomes are NOT_OBSERVED at authoring. A same-session return is practice; a later return is not automatic proof of retention.

One teacher with a class

Brief visible-group game, a few focal turns, and meaningful co-blending/listening for other learners. Do not promise individual evidence for every child at once. If noise, peer answers or supervision prevent fair observation, continue group learning and return during a safe existing routine. Keep a private fair rotation list. No unsupervised stations, public scoreboards or compulsory performance. Adjust pacing and stop if waiting or sound becomes uncomfortable.

Access and limits

Retain replay, quiet, large familiar pictures, established AAC, gaze, sign, and needed hearing support. An adult selecting the conceptual answer is not neutral motor help. If the actual English sound contrast is inaccessible, consult the relevant plan/support and teach a related accessible language goal without claiming this sound task was observed. Not a full curriculum, field trial, implemented SOLO app or measured teaching-mode equivalence. Both human teaching paths require independent complete review before publication.
