Feeling Words and Meaningful Communication · REVIEWED LESSON 01

Feeling words in gentle stories and everyday communication

Preschool instructional-design materials · PK3 & PK4

PK3.I.B.2.bPK4.I.B.2.b
Written design reviewedNo classroom outcomes implied

Teach 1-on-1: Feeling words, gently explored

Help a child understand words people use for feelings. For younger preschool learners, notice familiarity with word meanings. For older preschool learners, also offer chances to communicate feelings in comfortable verbal or nonverbal ways. Start where the child is; this is not a test of maturity, personality or willingness to share.

Have ready

One adult, one child, these short stories, and an ordinary safe activity the child enjoys: a book, drawing with suitable crayons/paper, or familiar play. Keep the child's usual sign, home-language, AAC or other communication supports available. No printing, reading, screen or second adult is required. Optional word mats provide examples; optional observation pages are private adult notes. Do not show an answer mat and call its copied answer an independent check.

Make the setting comfortable and use established language/access preferences. Read aloud, sign or describe the stories; allow speech, sign, AAC, pointing, eye-gaze or child-directed motor help. Do not assume an unfamiliar symbol system is already understood. Let the child listen, choose a story, keep playing, pause or pass. Food, toilet, water and comfort are never rewards for answering.

1. Invite a little meaning-making

“We can explore words people use for feelings. Would you like a story, or would you rather keep playing?”

If welcome, read: “Ari finishes a paper garden and says, ‘I feel happy about my garden.’” Ask, “What could happy mean here?” Wait. A home-language synonym, meaningful example or an accessible choice between neutrally explained meanings may show understanding. A smile by itself does not reveal a feeling; repeating happy alone may leave meaning unclear. Already familiar understanding is valid.

2. Explain, then let the child explore

“In my story I say, ‘I feel glad my picture is ready.’ Glad can mean happy or pleased. People can feel differently about the same thing. We listen to what they communicate rather than guess from a face.” Use a separate adult story and an optional explained gesture. You need not pretend to feel something or ask the child to copy your expression.

Let the child choose another gentle example or return to activity: - “Bo says, ‘I feel sad that our story is finished. I enjoyed it.’” Sad can mean unhappy about something. Bo may ask for another story. - “Dee says, ‘I feel mad because my idea was not heard. I want someone to listen.’” Mad can mean angry. Feelings are okay; hurting others is not required or excused. - “Ren says, ‘I feel scared of that unfamiliar shadow. Please stay with me.’ The trusted adult explains the shadow and stays.” Scared can mean afraid. Skip this story if unwelcome. Do not make shadows, sounds or frightening surprises to test it.

One word may be plenty. These examples are not a four-word quiz. Ask what the word helped us understand. Accept a plausible synonym or another cultural expression; ask what it means before correcting. If the child echoes, explore meaning with them and call that teaching. If translation or intent is unclear, keep it uncertain, not wrong. Stop for discomfort and give care rather than asking for a feeling performance.

3. Listen during real chosen activity

If appropriate, ask once: “Anything you want me to know about how you feel, or would you rather keep playing?” A child can communicate in their preferred way or decline. A familiar phrase, a prompted but owned word or an intentional gesture can be meaningful. Do not demand a word plus a facial expression. “Help” deserves help, but it does not prove a particular feeling. If needed: “I'm not sure what you mean. Would you like to show me, or leave it?” Respect leaving it.

4. Notice what this actually shows

Use a familiar meaningful story or, if useful, another: “Sam says, ‘I am happy to hear my favorite song again.’ What does happy tell us?” Do not supply the answer while calling it independent. A different context may explore transfer, but is not necessary for valid familiar understanding.

Privately note the actual meaning, prompt, access help and uncertainty. For word familiarity, describe which meaning was shown, not a score or fixed quota. For feeling communication, note the mode and whether it concerned a character or the child's own feeling. A character's feeling is not the child's private state, but meaningful character communication can still contribute to learning. A single event or channel is not full outcome mastery. Broad verbal and nonverbal learning is considered over opportunities, not forced into one response.

A neutral invitation is different from “say happy.” Adult-dictated content or a guessed facial feeling is not child-owned evidence. If the child declines or no opportunity occurs, note “not observed this time,” preserving earlier evidence and any access/safety concerns separately.

Finish kindly and return when useful

Stop after a useful exploration, at a child's request, or for fatigue, care or loss of interest. Return later to a familiar story or optional different context. Record the actual later date and response; don't prefill retention or transfer. Keep notes minimal and private under your setting's rules, not on a class display. This is a written teaching plan, not proof of classroom effectiveness or a diagnostic assessment.

The source is the 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, page 13, PK3.I.B.2.b and PK4.I.B.2.b. It includes prompted feeling labels and story-character feelings. It does not require novelty, spontaneous initiation, audible speech, two simultaneous modes or personal disclosure.

Teach a class: Words for feelings without a public mood test

One teacher can introduce gentle feeling-word meanings and observe individual communication during ordinary supervised activities. Younger preschool learners explore what basic words mean; older preschool learners also explore ways to communicate feelings. Grade labels guide teaching, not a rigid starting point or a diagnosis.

Set up for real supervision

Use the original stories below and the class's usual safe drawing/book/play choices. Provide enough ordinary materials or existing rotations so children not receiving your attention have meaningful activity. Keep every area within your normal safe sight/hearing supervision. Do not create a private booth or second room to interview a child. No extra adult, projection, cutting, reading worksheet or device is required.

Use established home-language/sign/AAC support, processing time and child-directed response access. Describe stories rather than relying on faces, print or unfamiliar icons. Optional word mats are teaching resources, not answers for an independent check. Optional observation pages are private adult records. Check individual access/safety plans relevant to this activity; do not invent a new specialist-permission gate.

A short, optional group invitation

“People use words and other ways to tell us about feelings. You may listen, share a story idea, or pass. Nobody has to tell the group their own feelings.”

Read: “Ari finishes a paper garden and says, ‘I feel happy about my garden.’” Invite possible meanings without calling on everyone. A group response is shared practice, not evidence for each child.

Explain on a separate adult story: “I say, ‘I feel glad my picture is ready.’ Glad can mean happy or pleased. Two people might feel differently about the same thing. A face alone doesn't tell us everything.” You can narrate and explain your own optional gesture; no second actor is needed.

Choose at most as much further story as attention supports: - “Bo says, ‘I feel sad that our story is finished. I enjoyed it.’” Sad can mean unhappy about something; Bo can ask for another story. - “Dee says, ‘I feel mad because my idea was not heard. I want someone to listen.’” Mad can mean angry. All feelings are allowed; harm is not required or excused. - “Ren says, ‘I feel scared of that unfamiliar shadow. Please stay with me.’ The trusted adult explains the shadow and stays.” Scared can mean afraid. Skip it if unwelcome; never generate frightening conditions.

No fixed number of words or personal disclosures is required. End the shared story when children need activity, care or a break.

Ordinary activity, focused listening

Children choose accessible familiar drawing, books or play within the supervised area. While attending to the whole class, listen to a feasible small set of focal children. Others continue meaningful choices and retain access to you for help. Rotate your focus over later opportunities rather than racing through everyone today. Defer observation if supervision or communication support is insufficient; never leave a group unattended or withhold care to gather evidence.

For word meaning, ask a focal child about Ari's familiar story, or offer “Sam says, ‘I am happy to hear my favorite song again.’ What does happy tell us?” Familiar meaningful understanding counts; a new context is optional. A child can answer through established accessible communication, offer another example, listen or pass. Naturally occurring peer conversation can count when this child's contribution is attributable; artificial isolation is unnecessary.

For optional feeling communication during real activity, ask only if appropriate: “Anything you want me to know about how you feel, or would you rather keep playing?” Respect their choice and privacy. Do not demand a public mood, a word plus gesture, or a particular facial expression. Respond to “help” immediately without claiming it proves frustration or sadness. If meaning is unclear, ask once whether the child wants to clarify, then accept no further disclosure.

Respond without shutting learning down

If a child echoes, explore meaning with another explanation rather than score comprehension from the echo. If a synonym or cultural expression differs, ask what it means. If access or translation is uncertain, record uncertainty, not inability. When a child is distressed, stop the assessment and support care; no induced fear, shaming or emotion performance. Keep AAC/access available while fading answer clues.

Record fairly, after care and supervision

Make brief private notes of actual meaning, context, prompting/access and uncertainty when safe to do so. For PK3, describe bounded word familiarity. For PK4, record the communication channel and whether the feeling concerns self or a character. Character communication is not a child's private state but may contribute to source learning. A meaningful prompted or familiar response is not automatically imitation; an adult-selected answer is not child ownership. One event/channel does not demonstrate all verbal/nonverbal breadth or mastery.

No chorus, peer's answer, attendance or completed group display establishes every child's learning. For an unobserved or declined opportunity, write “not observed this time”; preserve earlier partial evidence and known safety/access concerns separately. No moral judgment, readiness ranking or diagnosis. Keep only necessary instructional notes under setting privacy/retention rules, not intimate stories or public emotion labels.

Later opportunities

Return when welcome, possibly on another day. Familiar reuse can inform retention; optional different contexts can inform transfer. Record the actual event, not scheduled success. No completed revisit or learner outcome is claimed in this design.

Source: 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, page 13, PK3.I.B.2.b and PK4.I.B.2.b. Shared examples allow prompted labels and story-character feelings. Neither novelty, self-initiation, speech, paired channels per event nor disclosure of private emotions is an official quota. This is a written teaching route, not field-validated classroom effectiveness.

Method 01 — Feeling words in gentle stories and everyday communication

Purpose, source and scope

Learn what basic feeling words mean, then use a comfortable communication route to convey a feeling if willing. No child owes a personal disclosure. This is a focused written teaching design, not a clinical tool or proof of learner outcomes.

Published 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, Social and Emotional Development, printed/PDF page 13, I.B.2.b: - PK3.I.B.2.b: “Child is familiar with basic feeling words (e.g., happy, sad, mad, scared).” Research-cell SHA 507615c89e9a31e23919d29d202c1881eaa8e711e247c5e4a5273c9fa3f6775b. - PK4.I.B.2.b: “Child uses verbal and nonverbal communication to communicate basic emotions and feelings.” Research-cell SHA e2d48c85498c3756424d394575565580e2b9037d4266a47ef7f0b4f9f184147a.

The archived original publisher PDF places these two outcomes above shared child-behavior examples: familiarity with varied feeling words; labeling own feelings when prompted; identifying characters’ feelings in stories/movies. A neutral invitation does not invalidate child-owned meaning. Character communication may contribute to the broader source; never relabel it as the child's own emotional state. This lesson offers voluntary everyday communication, not an additional official self-disclosure requirement. Familiar contexts and understood conventional phrases are valid; novelty is an optional generalization probe, not a pass gate. Teaching spans verbal and nonverbal communication, while a single-mode event is recorded as that contribution, not whole-outcome mastery. No required word count, simultaneous two-channel display, audible speech, or prompt-free initiation quota is published.

Prepare for one adult and one child

Use this page's original short stories, one ordinary safe activity the child already enjoys (drawing with age-appropriate crayons and paper, looking at a familiar book, or quiet play), and the child's existing communication supports. Print is optional. Optional ORIGINAL_BASIC_FEELING_WORD_MATS.html provides teaching examples; ORIGINAL_EMOTION_MESSAGE_OBSERVATION.html supports private adult notes. Do not put answer-bearing mats in a supposed independent word-meaning check. No reading, writing, cutting, screen, second adult or special purchase is necessary. Adapt the ordinary activity to individual safety/access needs; do not introduce small loose objects or inaccessible materials merely for this lesson.

Ask or use established family guidance about meaningful home-language terms, communication gestures and sensory comfort. Describe stories aloud, sign as appropriate, enlarge text or provide the learner's established accessible presentation. Read option meanings neutrally and let the child point, sign, speak, use AAC, eye-gaze or direct a motor proxy. Keep the communication system available throughout; remove answer hints, not access. Do not invent a new tactile/AAC code and presume immediate comprehension.

Choose a calm, trusted setting. Explain: “We can explore words people use for feelings. You can listen, help tell a story, tell me something, or pass.” Begin with one comfortable word and one short story. A few responsive minutes may be enough; these are flexible planning choices, not tested durations or required completion. Pause for care, fatigue, discomfort or declining interest. Comfort, food, water, toilet and access are never earned by answering.

ENTRY — notice existing meaning

Read: “Ari finishes a paper garden and says, ‘I feel happy about my garden.’” Ask, “What could happy mean here?” Accept an explanation, a related example, a meaningful choice between neutrally described possibilities, or a home-language/sign equivalent. A child may instead listen or choose an ordinary activity. Do not infer meaning from a smile or from repeating the last word alone. If unclear, record uncertainty rather than a deficit. Already familiar understanding counts; do not make the context strange to earn credit.

MODEL — make meaning understandable

On the adult's own example say, “In my story I say, ‘I feel glad my picture is ready.’ Glad can mean happy or pleased. People can feel differently about the same thing. I listen to what they communicate; I cannot know just from a face.” Use your own words/sign and an optional deliberate gesture while explaining its meaning, without requiring the child to imitate your voice or face. One adult suffices. A hypothetical example is identified as a story, not a claim about the adult's real feelings.

DURING — explore and respond

Let the child choose another gentle story or return to play: - “Bo says, ‘I feel sad that our story is finished. I enjoyed it.’” Explain sad as feeling unhappy about something; Bo may ask for another story. Do not make the child sad. - “Dee says, ‘I feel mad because my idea was not heard. I want someone to listen.’” Mad can mean angry. All feelings are permitted; hurting someone is not required or excused. Never engineer rejection. - “Ren says, ‘I feel scared of that unfamiliar shadow. Please stay with me.’ The trusted adult explains the shadow and stays.” Scared can mean afraid. Skip this example if unwelcome; do not produce scary sounds or darkness.

These are original optional teaching stories, not a compulsory four-word test. Ask “What did that word help us understand?” or offer two neutrally explained meanings. Explain before expecting a new unfamiliar term. For a mere echo, say “That is the word I used. Let's explore what it means,” and supply a meaning with another example. For a plausible different interpretation, ask what the child means instead of treating a synonym or culturally different expression as wrong. For a language mismatch, use established translation/communication support and mark meaning unresolved until understood. For distress, stop the assessment and respond to care; never mine the moment for a feeling performance.

During ordinary chosen activity, an optional invitation is “Anything you want me to know about how you feel, or would you rather keep playing?” Accept an intentional word, sign, selected symbol, gesture or familiar phrase whose meaning the child owns. “Help” alone is a valid need, but does not prove a particular feeling. Give help regardless. Clarify only when useful and nonpressuring: “I'm not sure what you mean; would you like to show me, or leave it?” A prompted but owned “happy” is not automatically adult-generated. Dictating “say happy” and hearing an echo is not evidence of the child's feeling or semantic understanding.

EXIT — bounded observation, not a test of compliance

Return to a familiar story or use another harmless one if useful: “Sam says, ‘I am happy to hear my favorite song again.’ What does happy tell us?” Record actual meaning, context, exact help and uncertainty. Optional variation can probe generalization; it is not necessary to credit meaningful familiar-context response. Do not call an answer independent if the adult just supplied its meaning. Preserve supported learning as supported learning rather than erase it.

For PK3, describe the particular word familiarity shown. For PK4, record the feeling communication and its actual mode/context; character-feeling communication is labeled character-related, not own-state evidence. One mode or one event does not establish the full breadth of the outcome. Neither a neutral prompt nor child-chosen communication aid automatically removes ownership. Unknown intent remains uncertain. If no opportunity or child declines, record NOT_OBSERVED for that opportunity while preserving earlier observations and any safety/access concerns separately. No diagnosis, mastery ranking or numeric cutoff follows.

REVISIT — a later real opportunity

Return when welcome during a genuinely later session/day. Reuse a familiar meaningful story or explore another context if the child is ready. Record the actual date, prior support, present meaning and any change. Later retention and transfer are distinct inferences: same-context later use can inform retention; different-context use can inform transfer. Neither exists merely because scheduled. All learner EXIT and REVISIT outcomes here remain NOT_OBSERVED.

Teach a class: one educator, individually attributable evidence

Keep everyone within the teacher's existing safe sight/hearing supervision. Briefly tell one gentle story to the group; invite listening, optional responses or passing without a public mood round. Continue with ordinary accessible drawing/book/play choices already safe for this class. Do not create a separate hidden interview station. While supervising the shared area, listen to a feasible small number of focal children and record brief private notes after attending to care. Others continue meaningful chosen activity and can request help; peers are not assigned to diagnose or extract feelings. If supervision or communication support is insufficient, shorten or defer individual observations, not care. Rotate focal attention fairly across later opportunities. A chorus, attendance or another child's answer is not each child's evidence. Nobody must publicly disclose, leave the group, or wait without an activity to be assessed.

Progression and related routes

See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md. Local responsive options, not mandatory stages: PK3 F3-1 explore one familiar word; F3-2 contrast comfortable meanings; F3-3 connect a word to an understood context; F3-4 explore varied meanings when useful. PK4 F4-1 offer a meaningful language-bearing message; F4-2 offer a nonverbal route; F4-3 notice varied channels over time without pairing quota; F4-4 clarify only when needed; F4-5 generalize if naturally useful. Older children may revisit prerequisites; grade labels are not exclusion rules. Recognition/expression I.B.2.a and regulation I.B.2.c are related but not automatically credited.

False credit: An adult says “you look sad,” selects sad on AAC, then scores a child tap as self-expression. Repair the interpretation, restore child choice and honor uncertainty. False rejection: A child answers a neutral “How do you feel?” with a familiar meaningful sign in the usual play area. Count the bounded owned communication with its support/context; do not demand a strange activity, spontaneous initiation or additional facial display.

Privacy, access and limits

Keep minimal private notes of instructional meaning and assistance, not unnecessary intimate stories; follow the setting's established record-access/retention rules. No public names, face analysis, diagnostic labels, compulsory eye contact or emotional ranking. Attention: one brief story at a time. Language: familiar language/sign/AAC. Literacy: no text-decoding demand. Numeracy: no feeling score. Sensory/autism: predictable invitations and privacy. Developmental support: meaningful guided practice without withdrawing permanent access. Motor/hearing/vision: established accessible presentation and child-directed response. Consult relevant family/individual plans without treating them as mandatory new paperwork for this simple activity. Actual child outcomes, physical printing/tactile usability, external educator trials and implemented digital modes remain unverified.

Facilitator guide — Feeling words in gentle contexts

Use the complete lesson for ready-to-read original stories and both human delivery routes. This is the lesson-specific guide, not another counted lesson.

Goal and preparation

PK3.I.B.2.b concerns familiarity with basic feeling words. PK4.I.B.2.b concerns verbal and nonverbal communication of basic feelings; record the channel actually used without demanding two channels in every event. The original 2022 publisher page 13 also permits prompted labels and story-character feelings. Distinguish story content from a child's own feeling, and meaningful supported contribution from independent evidence. No novelty, voice, self-initiation or personal-disclosure quota.

One adult needs only the lesson's short original stories, an ordinary safe child-chosen book/drawing/play activity and the child's established access tools. Optional first-party mats supply examples; the observation pages are private adult notes, not compulsory worksheets. Read aloud/sign/describe meaning; no reading prerequisite. Prepare age-appropriate supplies already safe in the setting. Check meaningful language, processing time, sensory comfort and child-controlled response access before interpretation. Keep all communication aids; don't confuse withholding aids with fading answers.

ENTRY: invite, don't diagnose

“We can explore words people use for feelings. Listen, tell a story, play or pass.” Read Ari's happy-garden story and ask what happy could mean. Notice exact meaning through speech, sign, AAC, a related example or a meaningful neutrally offered choice. An echo alone is inconclusive; a familiar context is legitimate. Give time and allow a break or decline without penalty.

MODEL and DURING: make meaning explicit

Use the adult's separate hypothetical picture story to explain glad/happy and why faces don't tell the whole meaning. One adult models; no second person needed. Let the child choose another gentle story or continue play. The complete lesson supplies sad, mad and scared examples with benign support, never induced feelings. Skip unwelcome content. Accept plausible synonyms; explain unclear meaning and try again only when welcome. Keep teaching answers labeled as teaching, not independent proof.

If a child repeats the word, explore its meaning together rather than announce failure. If a gesture means something culturally different, seek respectful clarification. If language access is uncertain, do not score inability. If distress or need emerges, respond to care first, without demanding an emotional label. A child's “help” gets help; do not invent the feeling it denotes.

EXIT: observe a bounded contribution

Use a familiar meaningful example or optional different context. Ask without supplying the desired meaning. Record what the child actually conveyed and the prompt/access help. Neutral invitations are not dictated answers. A child who communicates a story character's feeling has character-related evidence, not evidence of their own private emotion. A single meaningful language-bearing or nonverbal event contributes to the source; whole verbal/nonverbal breadth is not established by that one event. Direct imitation alone, staff-selected AAC answers and a face interpreted without child meaning cannot substantiate the intended claim.

Preserve partial/supported learning with its limits. If no opportunity or a decline occurs, NOT_OBSERVED applies to that opportunity, not to everything previously observed. Record known safety/access concerns separately even if no response. Do not infer diagnosis, moral character, mastery or readiness from this record.

REVISIT and stopping

Stop when the child has had a useful exploration, wishes to return to play, needs care, is tired or loses interest. Later return may reuse a familiar story or vary context when useful. Actual later date and actual meaning support retention evidence; optional context change concerns transfer. Both remain NOT_OBSERVED until they happen. No scheduled future success.

One teacher with a class

Share one brief story without a compulsory mood circle. Keep ordinary safe drawing/book/play activities in the supervised shared area. Listen to a feasible subset of focal children while others continue meaningful choices; do not isolate a child behind a screen or leave peers unsupervised. Take brief private notes once care is attended to, and rotate focus across later days. Defer observation if safe supervision or communication access cannot be maintained. No chorus/group-product inference for an individual. Peer conversation can be legitimate evidence when the child's contribution is attributable, not a reason to demand artificial isolation.

Access and limits

Home language/sign/AAC, nonvisual description, large text, neutral child-directed motor proxy and processing time preserve participation. There is no forced eye contact, voice, public self-disclosure, acted fear or stereotyped facial matching. Use relevant established family and individual guidance; no invented specialist clearance gate. Keep minimal records under setting privacy rules. No empirical classroom results, physical print/tactile validation or later revisit is claimed. SOLO is NOT IMPLEMENTED; CO-PLAY, ADULT-LED and CLASSROOM are written human routes, not proven cross-mode parity.
