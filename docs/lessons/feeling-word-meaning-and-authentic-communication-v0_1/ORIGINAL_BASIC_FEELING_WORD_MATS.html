01 · Feeling words can have meaningful use

These are context examples, NOT a required list of exactly four words or a request that the learner feel any one emotion.

Happy / pleased

A fictional learner says “I feel happy my paper garden is ready.” A different learner might feel proud or quiet.

Sad

A character reports “I feel sad that our story ended.” The adult can offer a new story, without ever making a child sad.

Mad / angry

A fictional character says, “I feel mad that my idea wasn't heard.” Feeling mad never makes hurting anyone an acceptable requirement.

Scared / afraid

A fictional gentle story character says “I'm afraid of an unfamiliar shadow” and asks a trusted adult for help. No frightening real test.

PK3 meaning: these printed words are examples, not a compulsory fixed four-word quota or forced personal emotion. Offer established home-language/sign/AAC supports and meaningful familiar or optionally varied contexts.

PK4 own communication

When voluntarily communicating a genuine basic feeling or need, use a chosen language-bearing or nonverbal route (home-language speech, sign, AAC, intentional gesture or child-directed accessible action). Teach across routes over time; no paired-channel requirement per event. Honor needs without inferring a feeling from a request alone. Do not require vocal speech, eye contact or disclosure.

02 · Word meaning is not a facial guess

Story A — two people who look similar

Sera says, “I feel happy about my art.” Lio smiles politely, then says, “I feel unsure where this book belongs.” They might look similar yet communicate different things. The words they choose help us understand them.

Story B — the same quiet activity can mean different things

Mika quietly reads because she says she feels content. Tavi quietly rests because he reports being tired. Quiet alone is not proof of sadness, contentment or a diagnosis.

What is known about each person's chosen word?
Could a person mean something else? How could we ask respectfully?
Fictional stories can teach basic meaning. A child communicating a character's feeling may contribute to PK4 learning; it is not evidence of the child's OWN private emotion. Never infer actual peer emotion from face or posture.

03 · Child-controlled message, real optional recipient

Optional communication choices (not a scripted answer)

Own words / home language

Child chooses what, whether and how to communicate. Care requests are honored but do not prove a particular feeling.

Sign / child-selected AAC

Meaningful language-bearing supported expression, no forced audible speech.

Child-directed gesture

A point, nod, gesture, safe action or chosen motor-access support can convey intentional meaning.

Private option / no message

A child can take space or decline self-disclosure. Always support care anyway.

Adult listener response

Listen to actual child meaning. If unclear, ask neutrally, “Can you show me what you mean?” Never dictate which emotion a child must say. Respect private response, cultural meaning and nonvocal communication.
