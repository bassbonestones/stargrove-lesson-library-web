Getting help when a disagreement happens: teach 1-on-1

What your child is learning

Your child learns that a trusted adult will respond when help is needed, and practices a comfortable way to get that help. For preschoolers working toward the three-year-old guideline, the real-life action is seeking adult help during a conflict with another child. For the four-year-old guideline, it is starting a safe problem-solving strategy during a conflict with others and seeking adult support when needed. Asking for necessary help can itself be a sensible first strategy. No child must try and fail alone first.

This calm activity works with just you and one child. It cannot manufacture evidence of handling a real disagreement. If no conflict happens, the teaching still matters: record real-conflict behavior as not observed, not failed. Use the child's communication and developmental starting point, not age as a rigid entry test.

Before you start

Choose a comfortable moment, not the middle of distress. Sit or move where the child chooses, with a familiar book, drawing materials or another safe ordinary activity if desired. No special equipment, printout, second adult or second child is required. Keep the child's usual communication device, signs or other access supports available. Ask the family about a familiar help signal or language when you do not already know it; do not demand personal conflict stories.

Identify an actually reachable, responsive trusted adult. You might be that adult, but the child may prefer someone else. Agree where that adult can be found and how they will respond. An unreachable adult is not a workable help plan. Never promise secrecy about safety concerns; follow safeguarding procedures if the child says an adult feels unsafe.

The optional original choice mat and observation record are linked in the lesson's materials. They are adult-supported aids, not reading tests. You may speak choices, use familiar tactile objects or the child's existing communication system instead. Never remove a communication aid to test independence.

1. Let the child choose a help signal

Ask, “If you wanted my help, how would you let me know?” Accept an approach, gesture, sign, word in any language, eye-pointing at an established help symbol, or a child-directed communication device. Do not require eye contact, a polite sentence or spoken words. If the child wants to show you, respond immediately: “I noticed your signal. I'm here.” If the signal is unclear, check its meaning gently rather than assuming.

If a signal is not yet established, offer one accessible way the child can try while calm. Rehearse with a harmless everyday need, such as asking you to open a book. This rehearses a reliable communication route, not conflict mastery. If the child declines, keep adult support available and return another comfortable time.

2. Tell one tiny pretend story

Say, “This is a pretend story. Two children both want a wide piece of paper. An adult is nearby. One child asks for help. The adult listens and helps them find a safe way.” Do not take the child's paper or ask another child to act upset.

Think aloud: “Getting help is a useful choice. A child could also ask for space or choose a different available activity. Nobody has to give up a personal belonging, touch someone or say sorry to earn care.” Ask, “What could help the pretend child?” The child may suggest a different safe response, draw one, use a sign, or just listen. This is exploration, not a single-answer quiz.

3. Respond to the child's idea

  • If the child chooses adult help, honor it. Do not say, “Solve it yourself first.”
  • If the child suggests taking an object or pushing, calmly say, “I won't let anyone get hurt or have their things taken. We can ask for help or find another safe way.” Offer safe examples without making the child repeat your answer.
  • If the child suggests a safe alternative you did not expect, consider whether it is available and respects both people's boundaries. Accept it if so.
  • If the child becomes uneasy, stop the story and offer comfort, space or their chosen ordinary activity. No explanation or disclosure is required.

End after a useful brief exchange, or sooner if the child wants to stop. There is no required number of trials. You have completed meaningful preparation even when there is no real conflict to observe.

If a real disagreement later happens naturally

Care comes first. Never provoke a dispute, ration materials or wait for distress to increase. If there is danger, bullying, harm or escalating distress, intervene immediately and follow your safeguarding procedures; this is not an assessment opportunity.

In an ordinary manageable disagreement, stay responsive. Notice whether the child seeks help or chooses a safe response. Listen to each person's account without demanding a joint conversation. You can say, “I'm here to help. What do you need?” This is support; record the prompt if interpreting the child's initiative. Protect belongings and boundaries. The other person need not agree, apologize or resume playing for the child's action to be meaningful.

For three-year-old source evidence, the conflict must involve another child. A calm practice request or a disagreement only with you does not show that particular outcome. The four-year-old source says “others,” not only children, but do not deliberately create an adult-child disagreement. A familiar situation and a familiar learned phrase are valid if the child meaningfully chooses the action rather than merely following an immediate answer prompt.

What to note, privately

When everyone is safe and supervision allows, jot down the date/context, what the child actually communicated, what help or prompts you gave, whether adult support was available and received, and what happened next. Separate the child's action from your interpretation. Use minimal private identifiers; never put real child records in the public lesson repository.

“Used the help sign after I pointed to it” is supported practice. “Approached me and selected help during a natural peer disagreement” is a possible source-action observation, not proof of general mastery. A later different context can add transfer evidence; it is not required to recognize today's valid action. No opportunity, an unclear signal or your immediate safety intervention means the relevant independent action was not observed. An adult failing to respond is not a child failure.

Return on a genuinely later day if useful, with the child's preferred activity and access supports. Do not arrange a disagreement to produce a revisit. These are written teaching plans, not field-tested results.

Getting help when a disagreement happens: teach a class

Purpose and fit

Children learn how to reach a responsive trusted adult and explore safe choices during disagreements. For the three-year-old guideline, look for the child's help-seeking in a real conflict with another child. For the four-year-old guideline, look for the child starting a safe problem-solving strategy with others and seeking adult help when needed. Necessary help can be the first strategy. Neither age group has to handle danger alone, apologize, share personal belongings or win agreement.

One teacher can introduce and support this lesson during ordinary supervised activity. Do not promise to observe every child in one sitting. The class may complete worthwhile calm preparation with no real-conflict evidence at all.

Arrange a feasible, safe session

Use familiar, adequately supplied activities already in the room. Keep clear sightlines, accessible movement and the usual supervision arrangements; do not open extra stations just for this lesson. If you cannot safely supervise the arrangement, simplify it or postpone the optional teaching. No assistant, device, printer or manufactured scarcity is required.

Make the real help route explicit: where you will be, which familiar signal children can use, and what they can do if they cannot get your attention. Keep communication aids accessible throughout. An alternate adult is an option only if actually present, reachable and responsible. Do not send a child unsupervised to find someone. If you are handling another urgent need, use the setting's established help/coverage procedure rather than promising an immediate individual conversation you cannot provide. Physical and emotional safety take priority over observation notes.

Ask families privately about useful help words/signs and access needs when needed, without collecting stories of family conflict. A child may identify a different trusted adult. Listen seriously to reports of unsafe adults and follow safeguarding procedures; never promise secrecy.

Introduce the idea while children are comfortable

A brief whole-group invitation is optional. You can also introduce it to a small nearby group during ordinary activity while maintaining supervision. Say, “You can ask me for help. You do not have to get the words just right.” Show where you are and acknowledge a few volunteered signals. Children may listen, use their existing communication aid or decline a demonstration. This is not an individual skill score.

Narrate this explicitly pretend story yourself: “Two children both want a wide piece of paper. An adult is nearby. One asks for help. The adult listens and helps them find a safe way.” Do not recruit children as arguing actors or take anyone's materials. Ask, “What could help?” Consider help, space, an available alternative or a mutually welcome turn. Do not present turn-taking as compulsory or require a chorus. No second adult is needed.

Respond to a harmful suggestion with a clear boundary and useful alternatives: “I won't let anyone be hurt. We can get help or find another safe way.” Welcome different safe ideas, signed/home-language responses and silence. Do not ask children to disclose or reenact their own disputes in front of peers.

Return to ordinary meaningful activity

Children choose from the usual safe activities rather than wait for individual testing turns. Where supervision permits, check that one or two children's existing help routes really work: a child may signal for help opening a book or locating a material and receive a response. No fixed quota is required. Spread these calm access checks across days so quiet children and children with less visible signals are not overlooked. Do not confuse such rehearsal with real-conflict evidence.

Keep children who are not speaking meaningfully engaged in their normal activities. No classmate is assigned to cause a dispute, act as an assessor, translate private distress or enforce another child's compliance. If helping a child would break supervision, pause the optional activity and use the setting's established safety arrangements. Write notes later, never while safety requires attention.

Respond when a disagreement naturally arises

Intervene immediately for threats, bullying, physical harm or escalating distress. Stop assessment and provide safety and care. Do not prolong conflict to see who can solve it.

For an ordinary manageable disagreement, listen and support without requiring both children to talk together. “I'm here to help. What do you need?” can open supported teaching. Respect personal belongings, bodily boundaries and the choice to leave an interaction safely. A child may choose adult help immediately; do not insist on a failed independent attempt. If a suggested alternative is unavailable, explain that and help the child consider feasible options. Do not credit your selected solution as the child's initiative.

Honor signals in any language or communication mode. When a signal is ambiguous, check it privately rather than guessing or labeling the child defiant. A child may use a familiar phrase or strategy autonomously in a familiar real event. Neither novelty nor successful reconciliation is required for the source action.

Keep evidence individual and modest

Afterward, when supervision allows, privately record the child's exact action or communication, real context/date, adult prompts and access supports, adult response and any uncertainty. Do not photograph distressed children or display ratings publicly. A class discussion, correct chorus or another child's response is not evidence for everyone.

For the three-year-old outcome, a real other-child conflict is essential. For the four-year-old outcome, the source says “others”; note who was involved without inventing a peer-only rule. Separate child-initiated action from an immediate adult answer prompt. A learned phrase or child-selected communication aid is not automatically a prompt. If no relevant event occurs, write not observed. Do not mark a child unsuccessful because the child has not had a conflict. If an adult is unavailable despite a request, repair the access failure rather than blaming the child.

One meaningful real observation can be a source-action candidate, not mastery. A different context may provide transfer evidence and a genuinely later date may provide revisit evidence; neither can be filled in ahead of time. Review missing opportunities without seeking conflicts: continue supportive teaching, check access and observe only when ordinary life supplies a safe event.

The optional original choice mats and blank observation records are adult-supported aids. Speak or adapt choices with familiar tactile/communication supports instead of requiring print reading. Stop or shorten the introduction when children need movement, quiet or another activity. No child trial or classroom effectiveness has been established by this written design.

Method 01 — A responsive trusted adult helps with real conflict

Target, source and honest scope

This lesson builds a usable, child-chosen route to trusted adult help. It offers meaningful calm preparation with one adult and one child or one teacher and a class, and supportive observation only if an ordinary real disagreement naturally occurs. It never creates a conflict to obtain evidence.

The child need not obtain agreement, an apology or a resolved dispute for their own help-seeking/strategy to be meaningful.

Safe preparation and materials

Use a comfortable ordinary play/reading/art setting with adequate familiar supplies, clear sightlines and accessible movement. No second adult, extra child, device, printer, worksheet or manufactured shortage is required for calm instruction. Keep usual child-selected language, sign, gesture or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), sensory and motor supports available. Follow applicable individual support plans without making a diagnosis a prerequisite for access.

Identify a genuinely reachable responsive trusted adult and a usable help signal. A child may prefer another adult; do not tell a child whom they must trust. If a child reports an adult is unsafe, follow safeguarding procedures and never promise secrecy. If the intended adult cannot supervise and respond, simplify/postpone the optional activity or arrange established authorized coverage; do not leave children unobserved to obtain a score.

The optional original help-choice resource contains adult-supported text choices and fictional stories, not independently readable preschool picture symbols. Read or describe it aloud; substitute familiar tactile objects or an existing communication system where needed. The blank private observation record is an optional educator aid. No child writing or printing is required. See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for family progression, subject to the source/transfer distinctions above.

Do not take belongings, ration toys, stage rejection, require touching/eye contact/apologies, deny comfort or care, or force a child to disclose distress. Threats, bullying, harm or escalating distress trigger immediate adult intervention, not assessment. Respect safe withdrawal and every participant's boundaries.

ENTRY — notice existing communication without waiting for conflict

At a comfortable moment ask, “If you wanted my help, how would you let me know?” The child may show a familiar signal, approach, select a symbol, speak/sign or decline. Respond to the signal with actual attention: “I noticed. I'm here.” Check ambiguous meaning gently; do not infer defiance, lack of understanding or lack of trust from silence.

If no signal is established, model one accessible route while calm. A harmless real need such as opening a book can let the child try the route and experience a caring response. This is preparatory communication practice, not I.C.5 real-conflict evidence. No real disagreement is required before teaching begins. If an ordinary manageable conflict already happens naturally, help immediately; note child action only if doing so does not delay care.

MODEL — one adult, an explicitly fictional example

Say, “This is a pretend story. Two children both want a wide piece of paper. An adult is nearby. One child asks for help. The adult listens and helps them find a safe way.” Narrate both the request and responsive care yourself. Do not recruit children as actors or take their materials. Think aloud: “Getting help is a useful choice. A child could ask for space or choose another available activity.” The modeled action is teaching, never evidence that a child managed real conflict.

DURING — child meaning, responsive feedback and continued access

Ask, “What could help the pretend child?” Accept safe divergent ideas, home-language/sign/AAC responses, drawings, attentive listening or opting out. Do not turn the story into a one-answer test. If the child proposes pushing or taking belongings, set a boundary and teach a usable alternative: “I won't let anyone get hurt or have things taken. We can get help or find another safe way.” Do not credit a repeated answer as independent initiative.

For an unavailable suggestion, explain the real constraint and explore a feasible alternative. For unclear communication, check the intended meaning and access route rather than supplying a solution. For unease, stop and offer comfort, quiet or the child's chosen ordinary activity. For a safe unexpected idea, recognize it without insisting on your preferred script. Fade immediate answer prompts as readiness allows, never essential communication, sensory, motor or co-regulation support. A familiar phrase used meaningfully later is not automatically an echo.

A brief useful exchange may be enough; stop at the child's signal or before attention/regulation is strained. No trial count, fixed duration, public performance, reward for compliance or mandatory retest is required. The payoff is experiencing that help can actually be reached and received, not earning a badge for calmness.

EXIT — end teaching honestly; observe authentic source action only if available

The calm lesson can end with the child knowing/trying a help route and returning to an ordinary activity. A real-conflict outcome remains NOT_OBSERVED if no relevant event occurred. Do not extend the session or engineer a dispute to complete a form.

If a genuine manageable conflict naturally occurs, remain available and notice the child's action without delaying help. “I'm here to help. What do you need?” is legitimate support; record the prompt rather than claiming an uncued response. Listen without forced joint discussion, protect belongings and safe space, and help when needed. Adults remain responsible for safety.

For PK3, look for a meaningful child help request during real conflict with another child. For PK4, look for an initiated relevant safe strategy and needed adult-help seeking. A necessary help request can be the first strategy. Familiar settings are valid. Record child action, immediate answer prompts, access supports, adult availability/response and uncertainty separately. If the adult intervenes before any child action, no child-origin action has been observed; this is not a child failure. If a child asks but no adult responds, repair the adult/context failure rather than labeling the child unable to seek help.

A changed natural context can support a separate transfer observation. It is not a prerequisite for recognizing an authentic source action. One event never establishes general mastery, diagnostic status or durable competence.

REVISIT — optional, genuinely later, never manufactured

On an actual later calendar date, renew the child's preferred help route or calm story if useful. Observe real source action only when life supplies a naturally occurring safe opportunity. Familiar recurrence may inform continuity; a materially different context may inform transfer. Until such evidence exists, REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED. Do not prefill dates or claim retention from a planned activity.

Flexible teaching examples, not ranks

PK3 H3-1: Locate a responsive adult and accessible help signal during calm preparation. PK3 H3-2: Try a meaningful help request for an ordinary harmless need. PK3 H3-3: When a peer disagreement genuinely occurs, seek adult assistance; adult co-regulation is appropriate. PK3 H3-4: If a later naturally changed peer context occurs, observe whether the help route transfers. These are options, not a mandatory four-step test.

PK4 H4-1: Explore a safe opening action. PK4 H4-2: Consider an available alternative or mutually welcome turn, never forced sharing. PK4 H4-3: Communicate a boundary or choose safe space. PK4 H4-4: Initiate necessary adult help without needing to fail alone. PK4 H4-5: If a naturally changed context occurs, consider whether a safe strategy transfers. Select examples for actual need, not a fixed age or rank.

Human teaching organization and future routes

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: Complete calm signal practice and fictional exploration with one adult and one child. No peer is recruited. A caregiver-child disagreement alone cannot establish PK3's other-child source, although the teaching remains valuable. PK4's source is broader, but adults never create a disagreement for assessment.

CLASSROOM: One teacher can briefly introduce the help route to a group or nearby small group while maintaining supervision. Children then return to ordinary meaningful activities, not a testing queue. Check access routes across children/days without a quota; quiet signals must not be overlooked. Observe only naturally occurring events, keep notes after safety needs are met, and record individual actions rather than a chorus. If supervision is insufficient, simplify/postpone optional activity and use the setting's established coverage procedures. No child serves as conflict prop, assessor or enforcer.

CO-PLAY: A caregiver can explore the fictional story, honor child-selected communication and provide actual responsive care. They cannot invent a sibling dispute or choose the child's answer and call it child-origin evidence.

SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: Future child-controlled stories might rehearse help choices with accessible narration and controls. No such software or parity is established here; fiction cannot prove real interpersonal source action. Current human teaching does not depend on it.

False credit, false rejection and private records

False credit: a teacher makes a peer take a toy, waits for distress and scores a scripted apology. Stop the harmful procedure, restore safety/belongings and provide care. A puppet response, calm help-card tap or teacher-selected solution cannot be called real child-conflict evidence. A class chorus is not individual performance.

False rejection: a child independently uses a familiar AAC help message in a familiar peer disagreement, but the teacher rejects it because it is not novel, spoken or solved alone. The meaningful child choice and real context matter; continued access and responsive care do not erase them.

Use minimal private notes for authorized instruction: date/context, actual action, support/prompts, adult response, uncertainty and next supportive step. No real child records, photos or sensitive disclosures belong in public source files. No conflict, declined participation or ambiguous action is not observed evidence, not deficiency. This is an authored design under review, not a child trial, clinical instrument, independent educator validation, physical/tactile print validation or published acceptance.

Lesson 01 facilitator guide — reachable, responsive adult help

Use with the complete technical lesson. This is an uncounted companion, not another lesson. The family reference and developmental sequence provide optional neighboring methods, not prerequisites or mandatory ranks.

Know what counts

The Texas Education Agency, 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, PDF page 16, states PK3.I.C.5: “Child seeks adult help when experiencing conflicts with another child.” PK4.I.C.5: “Child initiates problem‐solving strategies when experiencing conflicts with others and seeks adult support when necessary.” These support planning; they are not diagnostic or readiness tests. Exact research-cell hashes are recorded in the technical plan and matched to the archived official PDF text on November 13.

One adult and one child can complete calm preparation. Only naturally occurring genuine conflict can supply the real-conflict evidence. Another child is essential to the PK3 evidence context, not to the preparatory lesson. PK4 may initiate necessary help as the first strategy. No failed solo attempt, new situation, spoken sentence or resolved dispute is required. A familiar phrase/communication aid can be child-chosen; an immediate answer prompt is supported teaching.

Prepare

Choose an ordinary comfortable activity with adequate safe supplies, accessible space and usual communication supports. Identify the child's reachable, responsive trusted adult and signal. Never demand that a child trust you; follow safeguarding procedures for reports of unsafe adults and never promise secrecy. No second adult, child actor or printout is required. Optional original text-choice mats are read aloud or adapted with familiar tactile/communication supports; they are not preschool reading tests.

In a class, keep normal sightlines and supervision. If you cannot attend safely, simplify/postpone optional instruction or use the setting's established coverage procedure. Never leave children unsupervised to record evidence. Do not withhold materials, stage rejection, force apologies/touch/gaze or invite private conflict disclosures.

ENTRY: find a usable help route

Ask, “If you wanted my help, how would you let me know?” Respond to the child's chosen signal. Check unclear meaning, or model an accessible signal when needed. A request to open a book can rehearse a real caring response without conflict. Label it practice. Do not wait for a real disagreement before teaching. If the child declines, keep help available and try another comfortable time.

MODEL: tell a clearly pretend example yourself

“This is a pretend story. Two children both want a wide piece of paper. An adult is nearby. One asks for help. The adult listens and helps them find a safe way.” Narrate both parts; do not recruit an arguing child or take anyone's paper. Explain that help, safe space or an available alternative can be useful choices. This model is not a child's real-conflict evidence.

DURING: listen and respond

Ask, “What could help?” Accept safe different ideas, listening, signs, home language, AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) and refusal. For a harmful suggestion, say, “I won't let anyone get hurt or have things taken. We can get help or find another safe way.” For an unavailable choice, explain the constraint and explore feasible options. For unclear signals, check access/meaning rather than assume inability. For distress, stop and provide care. Do not force an immediate retest or count repeated adult answers as initiative.

Fade answer hints when appropriate, not access or adult care. End after a useful brief exchange or sooner at the child's signal. In class, return children to ordinary meaningful activity; do not create a waiting line for conflict tests. Check quieter children's help routes across days where supervision permits, without a fixed quota.

EXIT: end preparation; observe real source only if naturally available

No real disagreement means NOT_OBSERVED for the official conflict action. The calm lesson can still be complete. If an ordinary manageable disagreement occurs, respond and keep children safe. Record the child's actual help request/strategy, immediate prompts, access supports, adult availability/response and uncertainty only when supervision allows. “I'm here to help. What do you need?” is useful support, not evidence of an uncued action. The other person need not agree or resume play.

Danger, bullying, harm or escalating distress: intervene immediately, stop assessment and follow safeguarding procedures. If adult intervention precedes child action, source action is unobserved, not failed. If the child asks but the adult is unavailable, fix the support failure rather than blame the child. Familiar genuine events can yield source-action evidence; changed context is an optional transfer claim. A single event is never general mastery.

REVISIT: later, conditional and honest

Renew calm practice on a genuinely later date if useful. Observe real action only if an ordinary safe opportunity naturally occurs; REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED until then. Do not arrange a dispute or prefill records. Familiar recurrence and changed-context transfer are different claims.

Delivery routes

ADULT-LED: One educator with one child uses the complete calm sequence and conditional later observation. No additional child is recruited. CLASSROOM: One educator with several learners uses a brief optional introduction, normal meaningful activities, sustained supervision and private individual notes, not a chorus or quota. CO-PLAY: A caregiver supports child choice and actual responsive help, never engineers sibling disagreement. SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: Future accessible fictional stories are proposals only; they cannot establish real interpersonal source action or proven teaching parity.

Private notes and honest limits

Use the optional blank record or a brief private note: actual date/context, learner communication/action, immediate conceptual prompts, access supports, adult response and uncertainty. Avoid diagnoses, public rankings, photos of distress, forced personal stories or public real-child data. No opportunity, ambiguous signal or declined participation is not evidence of deficiency.
