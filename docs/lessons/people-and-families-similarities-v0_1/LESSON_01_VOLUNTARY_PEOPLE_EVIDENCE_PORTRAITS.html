Teach 1-on-1: Evidence Portrait Studio

Children notice how people are alike and different using actual known details, not guesses about identity. They can build a drawing or simply tell/show a comparison. Separately, compare family characteristics in stories. This supports Texas PK4.VII.A.1 and PK4.VII.A.2; younger children can explore without a PK3 Social Studies score. Begin at an accessible concrete comparison, not at the whole outcome at once. The technical lesson explains the full evidence boundaries.

Set out only what you need

One adult, one child, two blank sheets or a board, a broad washable marker, and the accounts below are enough. Printing is optional. Use a comfortable accessible table/floor space with no small cut pieces. You can make two large areas for alike/different and a third for not known. Read or sign aloud; a child may point, sign, use a communication aid, speak any language or direct your marks. No reading, drawing or spoken explanation requirement. Follow the child's established support arrangements and give time/breaks responsively.

Original fictional people: - Tali: “I like making pictures from leaves. When I visit my uncle, I sing a welcome song with him.” - Dev: “I like making pictures with pencils. When I visit my neighbor, I wave hello.”

Both make pictures and greet someone; the described materials and greetings differ. These accounts do not say all their habits or which language/religion/country they belong to. “Different” never means better. If using original story sheets in Resources or blank canvas/record in Resources, read the actual wording, not meaning inferred from decorative drawings.

Both person boxes on the optional canvas accept a known real person or a clearly labeled fictional character. Note whether each detail was freely shared, neutrally noticed without inspection, or stated in a story. Record self, genuine classmate and other-person coverage separately; using fiction never fills missing genuine classmate evidence.

Begin with the child's idea (ENTRY)

“Would you like to draw, tell or show what these people share and what is different? You can watch or do something else.” Read one account at a time, repeating on request. Ask “What do you notice?” Follow the child's chosen relation. Offer a safe own interest only if wanted. A pass is not a failure. Neutral visible characteristics can be valid voluntary noticing; never infer culture, ability or personal history from them, require body inspection or pressure disclosure. Without a genuine classmate fact available by consent, keep that part unobserved; do not call a puppet, adult or fictional character a classmate.

Help just enough (MODEL / DURING)

If needed, use an adult example: “I use a blue pen, and an imaginary artist uses a red pen. We both use pens; the colors differ.” Explain how the facts support that relation. Return to the child's selected people, with no expectation to reproduce your exact words. Familiar examples are fine; distinguish a copied answer from the child's own idea.

Let the child arrange, draw or dictate. If they give one accurate relation, keep it and offer another opportunity, not an all-wrong judgment. If they guess a language from a name, say “The name doesn't tell us. We can leave that unknown.” If they say both sing, reread each account: only Tali's singing is stated; Dev might sing but we do not know. Child-origin comparison of the two stated greetings remains valid. Repeat/sign/translate to remove memory or language barriers without supplying the relation. If you do supply the comparison, note the help and value the practice. Offer a simpler two-object comparison, rest or another activity when needed.

Families are a separate invitation

Read these fictional accounts: “Ari and Aunt Jo go to the library and read together after dinner.” “Bea and her grandfathers read together each evening and make paper lanterns on Saturday.” Ask what their families share and what differs in the accounts. Both read together; the described caregivers/other activities differ. We do not know Ari's residence, all caregivers or anyone's beliefs. A family can span more than one household; no arrangement is better by having more adults. No child needs to discuss their real family. If distress or a sensitive disclosure arises, stop public questioning, respond calmly and follow the setting's safeguarding practice privately.

Notice, stop and return (EXIT / REVISIT)

“What would you keep or change in your portrait?” can be the check. Familiar accounts, play-led comparisons and access support are valid; no surprise materials, timed answer or compulsory number of responses. Note the child's actual words/action, compared people or families, known facts, alike/different relations, access and conceptual hints. A child's accurate relation remains evidence even when another part is missing. Keep people and family observations separate. Record unavailable/declined/unclear/unobserved honestly. A supported answer is not independent, and a drawing is not mastery. Explanation can clarify but is not required when the child's gesture/action is clear.

Finish when useful thinking is expressed, the child wants to stop or needs a break. Return when meaningful, in the same session or later; record actual time if claiming retention. Planned returns remain NOT_OBSERVED. Do not turn familiar practice into claimed novel transfer or a single successful moment into durable learning.

Written teaching design only, not evidence of actual learner outcomes. No child-only app is provided.

Teach a class: Our evidence portrait studio

One teacher helps children compare people using known details, not identity guesses, and separately compare characteristics of families. This supports Texas PK4.VII.A.1 and PK4.VII.A.2. Children need not already do the whole outcome: begin with one accessible relation, including familiar play objects if needed. Younger participation is enrichment, not PK3 Social Studies credit.

Arrange a studio you can supervise

Keep the whole group in one familiar area, with seated/floor drawing or story play within your sight. Use the usual safe washable drawing tools, one blank sheet per child who wants one and one shared board or large paper. No printing, cutting, photos, device, extra adult or remote stations required. Keep your private observation note beside you, not on a public chart. Children who prefer not to draw can tell, point, sign, use a communication aid or direct a scribe. Narrate facts and pictures, use home languages and established access supports, arrange comfortable reach and avoid small loose pieces for children who mouth them. Do not mistake handwriting, gaze, reading or English fluency for comparison skill.

Prepare these original fictional accounts to read or sign in short pieces: - Tali: “I like making pictures from leaves. When I visit my uncle, I sing a welcome song with him.” - Dev: “I like making pictures with pencils. When I visit my neighbor, I wave hello.”

Both make pictures and greet someone. The stated materials and greetings differ. We are not told all their habits or their language, religion or nationality. A welcome song is one narrated family/social practice, not proof of a named culture. The optional Resources story sheets and comparison canvas can be enlarged or replaced by your board; their drawings do not supply personal facts.

Both person boxes on the optional canvas accept a known real person or a clearly labeled fictional character. Note whether each detail was freely shared, neutrally noticed without inspection, or stated in a story. Record self, genuine classmate and other-person coverage separately; using fiction never fills missing genuine classmate evidence.

Open with a choice, not a round of disclosure

Say “We can make portraits of what we know about people. Would you like to draw, tell, show or listen?” Read the accounts, then ask “What do you notice is alike or different?” A child may watch or choose a familiar quiet activity. Do not require each child to introduce their family, name a culture or tell an interest in front of others. A child may choose an innocuous fact about themself. Only use a genuine classmate's willingly shared fact for the agreed purpose, and respect a quiet pass or later withdrawal. Known familiar classmate facts can be used with honest provenance and current consent; a new peer encounter is not required. Missing classmate information remains unobserved, not a reason to pressure anyone.

If useful, model with a different adult example: “My pen is blue; an imaginary artist's pen is red. We both use pens, but the colors differ.” Explain the relation, then let children choose what belongs in their own portrait. They may draw or dictate “alike,” “different” or “we don't know,” with no template or quota required. A child-led comparison during ordinary play can be the lesson.

Teach while the group has meaningful activity

Children continue comparing, drawing or telling the fictional accounts at nearby tables, or use the familiar quiet alternative. Ask one focal child to show a chosen idea while you still supervise everyone. Other children's learning does not depend on waiting for their assessment turn. Listen briefly, note the idea and help, and move on when practical. Keep a private list of children not yet heard and offer them fair later opportunities. Do not promise an individual observation of every child in one session. If a child needs extended support or supervision demands attention, pause the observation; never leave others to run interviews or new stations unattended.

Respond specifically: - One valid relation: “You noticed both make pictures.” Keep it even if the difference is not yet expressed. Offer another fact or a simple model, not an all-wrong judgment. - Guessing culture from names/looks: “That doesn't tell us their language or customs. We can leave it unknown.” Neutral visible characteristics voluntarily noticed, such as both having curly hair, are valid; body inspection, ranking and inferred identities are not. - “They both sing”: reread both accounts without supplying the comparison. Tali's singing is stated; Dev's is unknown. Comparing the stated wave and song is valid without claiming neither ever uses another greeting. - Memory or language strain: repeat, sign, translate or break the account into small pieces. Access is not an answer hint. If you supply the comparison, record the conceptual help and value the supported learning. - Distress, refusal or fatigue: offer a break or other activity. No penalty and no public announcement of who passed. If sensitive information is disclosed, end public questioning, respond calmly and follow safeguarding procedures privately.

A separate family story opportunity

At another natural pause or session, offer: “Ari and Aunt Jo go to the library and read together after dinner.” “Bea and her grandfathers read together each evening and make paper lanterns on Saturday.” Ask “What do these families share? What differs in the stories?” Both read together; stated caregivers and other activities differ. Do not infer Ari's residence, everyone's relatives or beliefs from lanterns. Families may include different caregivers, chosen relationships or more than one home. No arrangement is better because it has more adults. Children may compare the fictional accounts without telling anything about their own family. A portrait of unrelated people is not automatically a family comparison.

Notice individual thinking, then stop or return

Ask a focal child “What would you keep or change?” Familiar material is valid; no surprise, timed test or compulsory number of answers. Record the child's actual words/action and what facts they used, which people/families were compared, alike/different relations, access and any conceptual help. A gesture can clearly convey a relation without a spoken explanation. Keep observed parts, supported parts and unavailable parts distinct. No group chorus, attendance or shared poster proves each child's thinking. Self/classmate/other-person and cultural-influence evidence may emerge over several opportunities; fiction does not replace an actual classmate. Keep family comparison evidence separate. Unknown origin stays unknown, and a refused opportunity is not failure.

Stop when meaningful thinking has happened, children want to finish or supervision/comfort requires it. Return in the same session or later when relevant; record actual time and context, rather than claiming a planned revisit occurred. Familiar stories may support learning; changed stories may explore transfer, but neither proves durable mastery. Children can extend by inventing a clearly fictional person or revising a portrait after a known fact changes. Do not present inventions as real classmate observations.

This is a written design, not a classroom trial or evidence of actual learner outcomes. No child-only app, extra staff or universal family disclosure is promised.

Evidence Portrait Studio: alike, different, and still unknown

DOT58 revised written design, November 13, 2026. Candidate work only. Children build, tell or revise a portrait of what people actually share and how they differ. A portrait can be spoken, signed, arranged, drawn or directed through a communication system; drawing skill is not the goal. Individual facilitator guide is the adult entrypoint. DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md gives optional routes, not prerequisite tests.

Source and scope

2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, original publisher PDF printed pages 65–66, PDF indices 64–65: - PK4.VII.A.1: “Child identifies similarities and differences between himself, classmates, and other people through specific characteristics and cultural influences.” - PK4.VII.A.2: “Child identifies similarities and differences in characteristics of families.”

The PK3 column explicitly says no outcomes for this domain. Younger children may enjoy concrete comparisons as enrichment, not PK3 VII credit. These are developmental learning opportunities, not individual pass/fail readiness tests. A1 covers self, classmates and other people across meaningful opportunities; a single fictional pair cannot establish every referent. A2 concerns families, separately. Neither requires novel materials, a new classmate, a calendar-day delay, English speech, written explanations or a fixed count of answers. Both similarities and differences matter across the evidence gathered; neither one isolated missing part nor a declined opportunity erases an observed part.

Official A1 examples include drawings with distinguishing eye/hair characteristics, understanding different languages, and comparing own customs with a read-aloud character. Neutral, voluntarily noticed visible characteristics are valid. Do not equate a visible characteristic with cultural identity, judge bodies, inspect children, require self-disclosure or suppress a child's accurate benign observation. A child saying “We both have curly hair” is not the same claim as “We speak the same language because of our hair.” Specific cultural influences require known or narrated experiences, not inference from a name, skin, accent or clothes. Read-aloud fiction is a legitimate bridge to other people, explicitly labeled fiction. It is not a real classmate or an observed real household.

Materials and preparation

For one adult and one child: two sheets of plain paper or one wipe-clean board; a broad washable marker/crayon; these two short fictional people accounts, read aloud or signed; optional printed story kit and canvas/record kit. No printer, photos, device, cutouts, special purchases or additional adult is necessary. The adult can draw two spacious areas and label them “alike” and “different,” with a third “not told” area. The child may respond without any marks. Narrate all information; text decoding, color discrimination, sight and fine-motor control are not prerequisites. Offer accessible reach, uncluttered space, familiar communication supports and child-directed scribing. Use large pieces if the child chooses objects; keep small items away from children who mouth them.

Both person boxes on the optional canvas accept a known real person or a clearly labeled fictional character. Note whether each detail was freely shared, neutrally noticed without inspection, or stated in a story. Record self, genuine classmate and other-person coverage separately; using fiction never fills missing genuine classmate evidence.

Read these original fictional accounts verbatim or accurately translate: Tali: “I like making pictures from leaves. When I visit my uncle, I sing a welcome song with him.” Dev: “I like making pictures with pencils. When I visit my neighbor, I wave hello.” Both make pictures and greet someone; materials and described greetings differ. Neither story establishes a whole population's customs, exact nationality, all family members or whether either person also uses the other's greeting. Singing with an uncle is one described social/family practice, not evidence of a named culture.

For separate family comparisons: Ari and Aunt Jo go to the library and read together after dinner. Bea and her grandfathers read together each evening and make paper lanterns on Saturday. Both stories describe reading together; caregivers and other stated activities differ. Do not infer Ari's residence or other caregivers, Bea's beliefs, or a festival from lantern making. Family need not mean everyone living in one house. Families can include chosen relationships, varied caregivers and more than one home; the narratives are examples, not definitions.

Use an actual classmate's harmless chosen activity only if that person willingly shares it for this purpose. The teacher may preserve a previously volunteered fact only within its consent and relevant time/context, checking if it has changed. No covert collection, photos, names on public displays, sensitive family interviews or demands for cultural representatives. In one-on-one work without an available genuine classmate fact, teach self/other and family comparisons and record the classmate part not observed. An adult or puppet must not impersonate a classmate. A safe familiar fact from a genuine classmate may be recalled or narrated with honest provenance; a new live peer is not compulsory.

ENTRY: invite a child-owned direction

Offer: “Would you like to make a picture, tell a story, or show what these people have in common and what is different? You can also watch or choose something else.” Read one account at a time and repeat on request. Ask “What do you notice?” If the child is ready, “What is alike? What is different?” Offer a safe own interest as an option, never a condition of joining. A child's self-chosen fact can join a portrait; if they prefer fiction only, respect that and limit the self inference. Start with a single concrete relation or even two play objects if needed. A story comparison can emerge in play instead of a formal entry check. Wait responsively rather than timing a performance. Record an actual first response only if it occurs; do not withhold needed explanation or communication access to secure baseline data.

MODEL: show the comparison, not a script to copy

If helpful, use a short separate adult-owned example: “I use a blue pen; this imaginary artist uses a red pen. We both use pens; the colors differ.” Point to the facts while saying how they support your observation. Explain “Alike in one way can also mean different in another. Different does not mean better or worse.” Return to the child's chosen people/accounts. A model is instruction, not child evidence. Separate examples can help distinguish copying, but familiar materials remain valid. If using the same example, note what was supplied and do not call the echoed relation independent.

DURING: build and revise a meaningful portrait

Let the child select which known fact to represent or which story to hear again. They can move a large card, draw, gesture, speak, sign or direct a scribe. Say “You noticed they both make pictures. What could we add?” rather than demanding the adult's preferred feature. An accurate physical comparison is welcome when safe and voluntary; a stereotype is not.

  • If the child gives one relation only, retain it. Contrast another fact or model on pens, then invite another attempt. Do not mark all the child's thinking wrong.
  • If the child says “Their names mean they speak the same language,” say “A name doesn't tell us that. We can ask respectfully, or leave it not known.” Use a known example of different people sharing an activity, without assigning anyone a culture.
  • If the child says both sing, reread both accounts neutrally: Dev says wave, not whether Dev ever sings. “We know one greeting each. We don't know all their greetings.” A child may validly compare the stated greeting examples without claiming mutually exclusive identities.
  • If the child says “We both like drawing” from a genuine voluntarily known classmate interest, retain that relation even when a cultural-influence comparison is not yet available. Do not invent the missing part.
  • If a child ranks a family as better because it has more adults, calmly distinguish a stated arrangement from worth: “That tells us who is in this story, not which family is better.” Avoid insisting all real families feel safe; respond supportively if distress surfaces and follow the setting's safeguarding procedures privately.
  • If memory/language is the barrier, repeat or sign the account, present one fact at a time, or accept home-language/AAC choice. Access is not conceptual answer supply. If a hint supplies the comparison, record the help; supported learning remains useful.
  • If the child declines, looks distressed or loses comfortable engagement, stop or change activity without penalty. Do not manufacture a peer encounter or family disclosure to finish the record.

Extend only when invited: child invents a clearly fictional character with a shared activity and a different greeting, or revises a portrait after learning a fact. Invented people exercise comparison and imagination, not real-child evidence. The payoff is a more accurate, child-owned portrait, not a prize for agreeing or revealing personal information.

EXIT: notice what is warranted, not a test ritual

At a natural pause ask “What would you keep or change in your portrait?” or “What do these two families share, and what differs?” Use familiar or different accounts according to purpose. No required withheld set, number of trials or all-components-in-one-turn threshold. A familiar response may be child-origin; an unfamiliar one may still be cued. Record exact words/action, compared referents, actual known facts, similarity/difference separately, who supplied the relation and access/help. “I know because...” can clarify ambiguous reasoning but a spoken explanation is not compulsory if action already makes the relation clear.

A1 observations: self, genuine classmate, other person/explicitly fictional character, specific characteristic, described cultural influence, and actual relations observed. A2 observations: separately which family characteristics were compared. Do not award A2 from a picture of two unrelated children, or A1 full span from fictional families. Partial, supported and unknown evidence stay visible. U means child-origin without conceptual help in this opportunity; A means access-only support with child-origin thought; H means conceptual help; X means unclear origin. These describe this observation, not children. Record unavailable, declined, unsafe or unobserved separately rather than a low score. No synthetic helper, attractive portrait, chorus or single response establishes mastery, diagnosis or retention.

REVISIT and stopping

Stop when the child has expressed a useful relation, asks to finish, needs a break or adult supervision requires it. Return when it is meaningful: the same session after play, another day, or a genuine later encounter. Keep familiar examples when they help; vary only to explore transfer. Record when it actually happened and elapsed time if making a retention claim. A planned return is NOT_OBSERVED. Same-session practice is not evidence of long-term retention; a changed story alone is not durable transfer. Revisit consent, interests and information rather than treating a prior preference as permanent identity.

Two human delivery routes and future modes

ADULT-LED / CO-PLAY, one adult and one child: sit together with two accounts and a blank canvas; follow the child's chosen representation, model only as needed and document the scope actually available. Missing classmate information limits that strand, not participation or valid A2 learning. A child-led play conversation is equally legitimate. The paired facilitator guide gives the runnable route.

CLASSROOM, one teacher and multiple children: keep all children in the same supervised area using their familiar drawing/story play, not remote interview stations. Offer the two accounts to the group, then invite voluntary portraits at tables within sight. Children not receiving a focal observation continue meaningful drawing, telling or comparing the fictional accounts, or another normal quiet activity. Model a comparison if needed, then listen to one child's contribution at a time while retaining whole-group supervision. Prioritize children not yet heard over later opportunities; do not promise every child an assessment in a single session. If individual listening prevents supervision, pause the observation. Classmates choose whether to share a harmless interest; anyone may pass without being singled out. Do not display private records. A group portrait or shared answer is learning participation, not proof of each child's idea. Capture the focal child's words and help, record others not yet observed and return fairly. No second adult or concurrent managed stations required.

SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: no child-only app or automatic cultural judgment is delivered. Human guides can read text and support nonspoken choices; this is not a claim of implemented audio software, specialist validation or equal mode effects.

False-positive, false-rejection and limits

False-positive: a child copies an adult's “both have the same culture” claim from portraits, then receives A1/A2 credit. Reject the unsupported inference and teaching claim; repair the adult explanation and retain any separately grounded child observations. False-rejection: a child signs that two known classmates both wear glasses, uses familiar pictures, or notices a family comparison with adult narration. Preserve the warranted relation; access and familiarity do not invalidate it. There is no need to reveal diagnosis, prescribe glasses as culture, or force an additional difference on demand. Written design is not observed effectiveness: no real child data, classroom trial, physical-print testing, cultural specialist approval or measured reliability is claimed. Shared family repairs do not qualify sibling lessons 02/03.
