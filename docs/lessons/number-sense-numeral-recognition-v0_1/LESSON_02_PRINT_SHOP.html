Teach the Number Sign Print Shop

Teacher-friendly guide. The print shop is a playful way to identify numerals across different fonts, not just match two shapes.

What the child learns

A written number keeps its identity when its font, size or location changes. Children may play printer and customer while practicing which numeral is actually printed. The symbol for zero is different from a blank sign; ten is a complete two-digit numeral.

Prepare the printing desk

Use large neutral symbol cards in different readable fonts, blank sign frames and an adult-only answer key. Keep correct symbols out of the child's view before their first answer. Never rely on permanent card color, fixed sign position or an adult-made model that displays the test answer.

Start 3-year-olds with printed zero, one and two, then three through five. Revisit zero to five with 4-year-olds before adding six through eight, nine and ten. Full later assessment across six or eleven symbols can be divided over sessions.

Let the child play printer

1. Look at a first unfamiliar sign

Show a previously unseen printed numeral.

Say: “What number is on this sign?” Or name one number and let the child choose among a few shuffled neutral symbols.

Record the first genuine recognition or nonspoken selection. Don't point to the answer first.

2. Show your own sample sign

On separate practice examples, show the same two in two fonts beside a three.

Say: “Both of these say two, even though they look a little different. This sign says three.”

For the appropriate later stage, compare upright six and nine, and whole ten with one. A printed zero differs from a blank frame.

3. Run a small print shop

Offer a new shuffled selection of symbols.

Say: “Our customer wants a sign that says [number]. Which printed symbol should we put here?”

Let the child select or direct placement. Switch roles and let the child choose a numeral for you to print, then ask them to read a different sign. If they only match shape pairs without knowing the number, keep that as visual matching practice rather than automatically awarding numeral recognition.

4. Check a genuinely new print

Put practice signs away. Show a fresh readable font or changed sign layout.

Say: “What number is this?”

Record the identity recognized without answer-giving, along with any access assistance. The formal later full-range check covers zero-to-five for the 3-year-old goal or zero-to-ten for the 4-year-old goal, not only a few favorite symbols.

5. Return with new signs another day

Use a different familiar print style or sign. Only real later child answers demonstrate a revisit.

If the child gets stuck

Observation Next step
Matches same-looking cards but can't say what number they are Model the number's name on another symbol and offer new identification
Confuses zero with blank Contrast actual printed zero and unprinted sign
Mixes six/nine or one/ten Show a different upright practice pair
Needs a nonspoken response Accept sign, pointing, gaze or accessible selection
Recognizes only one familiar font Vary the readable style and location without adding distracting color cues

Teach the Number Sign Print Shop with a Class

Classroom teaching pathway for preschool educators working with multiple children. The companion Teach 1-on-1 path serves one adult and one learner. These are educational written designs, not evidence of observed classroom effectiveness. Classroom structure and group size may be adapted to real staffing and children's needs.

What children are learning

A written number keeps its identity when its font, size or location changes. Children may play printer and customer while practicing which numeral is actually printed. The symbol for zero is different from a blank sign; ten is a complete two-digit numeral.

Prepare the classroom

Provide large printed numeral signs, safe broad writing or stamp tools, individual blank signs and a separate adult demonstration. Children can print, place, select or direct a matching numeral without requiring mature handwriting. Prepare fresh unnumbered target prompts for individual checking.

Introduce the idea together

Invite, “Where could a number sign help someone? Which number does this sign show?” Briefly show how you would make a different teacher sign for a meaningful purpose; do not pre-label the child's exit sign.

Give children meaningful practice

Create a pretend print shop with groups making original signs for an imaginary town, swapping roles as designer, printer and reader. Each child needs opportunities to identify real printed numerals themselves, not only decorate an assigned card. A teacher circulates through stations and sees a few individual first symbol identifications each time.

Observe individual learning fairly

For a focal learner, show a new printed numeral alone and ask what it represents before any group answer or object count is revealed. A child producing a beautiful traced numeral may demonstrate mark-making, not independent numeral recognition. Record the authentic identifying response and stage, with different exemplars later.

Include, respond, stop and revisit

Support various visual contrasts, tactile/large print and nonspoken labels, while preserving the task's symbol identity. Include intentional zero and gradual progression toward the age-appropriate range; don't demand polished printed handwriting. Let unobserved symbols remain pending, not failed.

Teacher's recording rule: Whole-group talk and collaborative practice count as learning opportunities, not automatic proof of every child's independent understanding. Record only the action, context, support and developmental stage actually observed. For children whose independent opportunity did not occur, use not yet observed and schedule a suitable later chance; a planned later-day revisit is not already completed. No child should be deprived of meaningful participation just because the educator cannot assess everyone at once.

Required developmental teaching sequence: PK3 foundation → PK4 extension. Apply this ordered sequence in SOLO, CO-PLAY, ADULT-LED and CLASSROOM routes; SOLO remains a written specification only.

B. Lesson sequence — entry, model, during, independent exit, later revisit

ENTRY E-PRINT, 2–3 min. Before introducing the print shop, the adult quietly shows an unknown standard printed 2 then an unfamiliar 0 to PK3; PK4 adds previously unseen 6, 9 and 10. “What number do you read on this sign?” If speech is not a usable response, narrate a target name and offer 3–4 neutral upright numeral-only choices, with positions changed between trials. Write down first actual response, access mode and whether any clue preceded it. Entry covers only samples, not the entire official range.

MODEL M-PRINT, 4 min. Place two sample signs with 2 in different fonts/sizes alongside a 3 foil. “These two signs say two even though the shape changes a little. This one says three. We don't decide by color or card size.” Show a real 0 on one sign and a blank sign next to it: “Only this printed shape is the numeral zero.” For PK4 demonstrate upright 6 versus 9, then two distinctly styled whole 10 signs versus single 1. Also model PK4 7 and 8 on separately labeled practice signs. Adult labels are teaching, not independent learner evidence. Child chooses the next font/size transformation.

DURING D-PRINT, 8–10 min. Child becomes the printer: adult verbally commissions a numeral and child selects the corresponding unsigned sample from a rotating 3–4-card field to place in blank sign frame A or B on PRINT_SHOP_MATS.html. Then adult replaces it with a different font of the same numeral plus a different numeral. Child decides what still says the same number and explains/chooses how they know. After several turns reverse roles: child commissions, adult prints, then child reads an adult-selected novel practice sign before adult gives the answer. Work through all PK3 0–5 or all PK4 0–10 in short turns; the child chooses when to pause. For child who primarily sorts matching glyphs: “You found a pair. Can you tell me the number they mean?” If not, teach the name separately; mark visual sorting, not independent recognition. Child-made/traced signs are engagement, not evidence that a fresh numeral is recognized.

Misconception-specific support. If child points to blank for zero, show real printed 0 and blank on model-only examples. If child reads a 9 as 6, show two upright conventional fonts and compare curve-tail placement; do not rotate either. If child calls 10 “one,” show whole 10 beside one numeral 1, explain that 10 is read as one number, not a loose pair of cards, and then practice on a new 10. If child confuses 2/3/5 or 3/8, compare their actual printed shapes and have child choose what changes; don't say “try harder.” After feedback, a coached correct answer is HELPED, and next independent evidence requires another genuinely unseen variant.

EXIT X-PRINT, 5 min or split. Close/cover the shop windows and all demonstrated signs; present unseen alternate standard font exit samples. In counterbalanced order ask “What number is this?” for each in-grade identity 0–5 PK3 or 0–10 PK4, or narrate the numeral and let child select from a new shuffled neutral 3–4-symbol field. Do not show a named pair beside a target, let another child call an answer, reveal card correctness before a response or prompt with a quantity picture. Record first answer, target symbol, font and actual foils, speaking/sign/AAC/receptive modality, neutral access support vs content hint. Missing/incorrect symbols must not be silently scored correct, and using 3 options has guess-chance limitations. A single correct pair sort cannot replace each identity-by-identity blind recognition.

REVISIT R-PRINT, actual later date. Another readable unfamiliar font, new positions and unsigned sign formats; again independently test all six or eleven source identities. Note actual date, responses, cues, and changed access. No invented retention/child learning until the later session occurs.

C. Four fully written delivery routes

ADULT-LED SINGLE CHILD — complete 22-minute parent/tutor plan

0–3 prepare one safe flat table and blank sign frames, hide exit cards, probe PK3 2 then 0; for PK4 probe 2 then 0, followed by 6, 9, 10 (five initial identity tasks), before model/label cues. Record exact first identity for each shown glyph; when a child opts for a shorter beginning, unattempted entries remain NOT_OBSERVED, not correct or failed. This baseline is never a full-range 0–5/0–10 source claim. 3–7 model two-font same identity and one foil, printed zero versus blank; for PK4 model whole 10 versus one and upright 6/9. Ask child which sign transformation they prefer and explicitly narrate how one numeral can change physical size without changing name. 7–16 hand child a blank job mat as “print shop manager”; commission numerals with your spoken number, offer 3–4 unsigned options, let child choose/print and then read your alternate-font result. Rotate commission/reader roles after a few examples. Optional child-led purpose: the child chooses a tiny real or pretend destination such as numbered toy-room doors, shelf labels or spaces for child-made art. The child reads a genuinely unknown conventional numeral sign independently (spoken, sign, AAC or neutral receptive choice) before helping the toy or sign reach its numbered destination; then the child may commission a fresh sign for the adult to read. Vary legible typography without color/location answer cues; honor children who prefer direct quiet sign sorting and don't require pretend play. This meaningful practice never replaces untouched individual-glyph EXIT. If the child copies a model, congratulate design effort but do not mistake it for independent symbol identification. Address a specific false identification using the correction paragraph above and practice on different forms. Continue coverage over several short rounds rather than rushing eleven names. 16–21 take out exit deck never seen that session, have the child identify every age-appropriate symbol, recording by identity, first reply, and aid. Stop respectfully for fatigue; remaining = NOT_OBSERVED, not assumed mastery. 21–22 child chooses a favorite blank sign and tells you where a sign would be useful; book later-day independent revisit.

CO-PLAY — child as commissioning partner, not a passive follower

Begin with the main E-PRINT child-first age-specific baseline: for PK3 independently identify fresh upright printed 2 and 0; for PK4 2, 0, 6, 9 and whole 10. Adult must not disclose the correct identity before a first expressive response; in receptive mode the named target is a question within a neutral 3–4-glyph field, not an answer spoken next to a single card. Child may freely choose to start with fewer or different meaningful sign identities; record actual shown glyphs and mark skipped planned entry prompts NOT_OBSERVED, not a complete baseline. Then adult and child jointly model a known numeral in two fonts and a misleading other-number foil. During practice child gives spoken/sign/AAC commission; adult makes a different style numeral sign and child names/reads it; then adult commissions and child selects a sample. Each has genuine information to contribute. If the child selects a sign, adult does not smile/point to correct one prematurely. Include comparisons 0/blank, 1/10 and upright 6/9 for PK4; choose additions from actual child preferences. Exit is not the jointly made sign: adult privately presents new alternate-font symbols, child's own first reading precedes any feedback, across the complete grade range. Later revisit in another natural location with new shapes. Preserve child's right to choose break/not sure and record helping separately.

TEACHER-LED CLASSROOM — feasible stations and individual accountability

For an illustrative class of 20, prepare four reusable print stations with large upright sample signs and two blank print frames each; teacher model signs on display; separate locked/covered new exit decks; private 20 × six/eleven first-response grid and optional AAC/switch/tactile formats. 0–3 teacher privately checks a few baseline symbols; choral names only show class engagement, not individual evidence. 3–7 whole-group explicit model same-number two-font signs, zero/blank, 6/9, 1/10 as age appropriate. 7–16 groups of about five rotate printer, commissioner, reader and change-checker roles; peer feedback allowed as helped practice. Teacher ensures an actual child reads before peers call out, and observes/records only responses individually attributable. 16–22 independent exits with unfamiliar font cards/private response sheets or individual pull-asides, not twenty children shouting the answer. Every in-grade glyph must have an actual recorded attempt per child for complete-range candidate; spread PK4 trials across more than one appointment if necessary. Later actual session: repeat with new signs and record what changed, not a fictitious same-day retention mark.

D. Assessment rubric, accommodations and ethical limits

Evidence I: correct first individual independent identity (receptive/expressive distinguished); E: independently attempted other numeral; H: correct only after answer cue/hint; N: NOT_OBSERVED/no accessible response; A: neutral access accommodation annotated separately on I/E. Note date, literal numeral, actual font/size, array/foils, initial response and support. Correct first answer on every 0–5 or 0–10 symbol in new print is provisional internal recognition evidence, not a Texas mandated cut score, specialist review, longitudinal retention, or validated educational effect. When an answer is uncertain, preserve raw observation rather than converting to mastery. Separate different modalities rather than claiming expressive language from receptive pointing.

Universal/individual: upright legible original enlarged high-contrast symbols; optional tactile raised true numeral outlines, child-selected close distance, non-speech AAC/home-language signed names, large motor-safe materials, no handwriting requirement, no fast flash or forced participation. If IEP, IFSP or Section 504 accommodations apply, review them with the appropriate educator/family before real use; note what adaptation was actually provided and whether access to print shape was possible. A prerecorded answer-label triggered by touch on a candidate itself disqualifies that trial as independent recognition unless the task measures something explicitly different; do not diagnose a child from inaccessible font choices. A motor proxy must move the card only at the child's indication.

Adversarial no-credit cases: Child correctly sorts two unnamed identical 7s by appearance yet cannot identify seven; child makes a perfect copied 4 but reads new 4 as five; child knows ten counters but reads whole printed 10 as “one”; child guesses left-hand sign every time; child correctly sees only four of six PK3 values; child is told number answer by a classmate; unreadably ornamental font creates a false failure. Each is not a complete source action. Other official PK3/PK4.V.A.2/.3/.4/.6 have their own separate evidence requirements.
