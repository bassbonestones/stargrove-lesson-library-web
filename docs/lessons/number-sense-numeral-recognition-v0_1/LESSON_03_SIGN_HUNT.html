03 — The Sign-Finder Walk: read numerals where people use them

Required developmental teaching sequence: PK3 foundation → PK4 extension. PK3: teach printed glyphs 0–2, then 3–5, then mixed 0–5; PK4: explicitly retrieve 0–5, teach 6–8, then 9 and whole 10, then mixed 0–10. Do not teach or assess all eleven as one first exposure. The existing full-grade EXIT is a later source-range check, not the first teaching session. Apply this ordered sequence in SOLO, CO-PLAY, ADULT-LED and CLASSROOM routes; SOLO remains a written specification only.

MATH-PK-NUM-03 · 2026-10-30 · Original universe-independent E2.2 four-mode written lesson, NOT IMPLEMENTED as SOLO software. This method differs from the two prior lessons: rather than primarily discriminating isolated symbols or transforming fonts in a pretend print shop, a child must locate and independently identify printed numeral identities in functional, varied and distraction-bearing environments, then read the symbol with no counting cue. Neither an alphabetic reading test nor an actual navigation safety challenge.

B. Lesson through real instructional phases

ENTRY E-HUNT (3 minutes): Place three not previously shown adult-made functional cards in disordered positions: for PK3 a shelf sign with 0, seat sign with 3, door sign with 5; for PK4 four signs featuring 0, 6, 9 and 10, all in neutral readable type. “There's a number symbol somewhere on this sign. Can you show it and tell me its name?” Child may point/gaze/switch to specific numeral while adult records first answer before reacting. If the child knows the context but not the numeral, record that distinctly. Do not show a number line that visually orders answers.

MODEL M-HUNT (4 minutes): Move entry cards aside. Introduce different original sample signs. “This sign has letters, shapes and a printed number. Let me look for the numeral — here it is. This one says two.” Place a visually busier sample next to a simple printed 2 and demonstrate that its identity remains the same. Explicitly show a printed 0 versus empty label and explain only the actual loop is zero. For PK4 display a room sign labeled 10, read it as “ten” (not merely a 1 and a 0), and compare an upright 6 and 9. The teacher explains while child can choose which sign to decorate with a nonnumeric border; decoration cannot become an identity cue. Model using different numerals/signs than exit.

DURING D-HUNT (8–10 minutes): At three tabletop stations (or three simulated zones) place fictional numbered door/shelf/seat signs face up, with no ascending route. Child chooses the station, then hears “Find a written number. What does it say?” If the child is overwhelmed, begin with one clear card before adding moderate surrounds; lower visual noise and picture/text demands while keeping the real numeric form. Adult occasionally gives a name (“Can you find the zero symbol?”) and lets child choose among 3–4 different signs with neutral position; other times child names the discovered printed identity. Keep routes distinct: locating a numeral amid signs and identifying it must both be observed. Practice all 0–5 for PK3 or all 0–10 for PK4, with new combinations and randomly placed signs. After error, model on a different practice sign, then offer a different new practice sign; a corrected answer after teacher identifies it is helped. Optional child-led, purposeful route: Let the child choose a tiny meaningful task (help a favorite toy find its numbered resting place, decide which numbered shelf to use for a picture, or simply guide an adult through two safe tabletop sign stations). Present a genuinely unannounced conventional numeral on the destination sign: the child first locates and independently identifies the printed glyph, then directs the toy/object/person to that sign. Let the child later set an unlabeled safe sign for the adult to inspect, a reciprocal unscored role; never use that rehearsed sign at independent EXIT. A child who prefers quiet plain-card reading without pretend play receives an equally valid learning pathway. Child can choose route and where signs go, but may not see final assessment sign layouts before exit.

Misconception repair: Child describes a colored door rather than printed numeral → “That's the door picture. Here is the number symbol. It has its own name. Let's read a different one together.” Child reads 0 as “nothing” or points at a blank spot → distinguish actual 0 printed glyph from absence of a symbol. Child calls printed 10 “one” → adult demonstrates whole label 10 vs single 1 in model materials then unannounced variant later. Child calls 9 “six” → compare upright loop and tail with another safe conventional font; no rotated foils. Child can count objects on a sign but cannot name printed 4 → remove countable icons, teach the glyph itself. Child knows sign because it always appears in same room/location → reorder and use another printable sign context. An inaccessible busy image should trigger a presentation adjustment, never a disability inference.

EXIT X-HUNT (6–8 minutes, may be split): Put away all classroom practice signs. Present a new neutral functional sign for every in-grade numeral with context/position/font changed and no accompanying object sets, then ask each child to find numeral token and independently name it (“What numeral is on this sign?”). For nonoral option say the identity and let child select among multiple novel neutral signs; record matching receptive evidence only. Alternate a single bare glyph trial with at least some realistic signs to test whether surroundings distract. Probe PK3 0,1,2,3,4,5 or PK4 0–10 explicitly without teacher pointing to target, teacher read-aloud numeral, spatially fixed order, peer response leaks, correct-answer location, counting clues or drawn quantity pictures. If only bare-glyph stimuli from the separate deck were presented, report bare glyph transfer only, not authentic-context generalization. Log every numeral identity and context type separately. A four-question hunt across eleven numbers is partial, not source-wide success. Child may stop; untested outcomes remain NOT_OBSERVED.

REVISIT R-HUNT (new actual date): Use an unfamiliar but safe new class set of simulated menus/shelf/locker labels, not a real child's personal room number, address or phone, plus fresh neutral single cards. Child independently locates and names all six/eleven glyphs in scrambled order without a preceding model or countable hints. Record date, exact response, context, assistance, access and missing identity rather than inventing retention from an intended future plan.

C. Four substantive delivery routes

ADULT-LED SINGLE CHILD (20–25 minutes)

0–3: Prepare actual E-HUNT three PK3 unknown signs 0,3,5 or four PK4 unknown signs 0,6,9,whole 10, not an unexplained three-card default for both grades. The child chooses the order, and the adult records the first located numeral and independently named/selected identity before teaching. If the child chooses a shorter introduction, unattempted original entry identities stay NOT_OBSERVED, not correct/incorrect or a completed entry baseline. 3–7: Adult models how one numeral appears as part of a real-world sign and one as an isolated card; names 0 versus blank; for PK4 demonstrates whole 10 and upright 6/9. Child traces a route with their finger, not necessarily the glyph, and chooses which pretend room they'd like to inspect. 7–17: Child conducts a simulated “inspection walk” among three desk stations with changing unlabeled fiction-room cards. Adult prompts both find-and-name and name-and-find, shifts 3–4 candidate signs after each answer, gives targeted feedback on separate examples and asks child to show a different symbol after supported teaching. Ensure every in-grade identity has appeared in practice. 17–23: Put practice away; adult issues a genuinely new set, one at a time, with no nearby answer/counted objects, and asks every identity separately. Use recorded correct/incorrect/helped/access codes and optional fresh neutral glyph to disentangle context from identity. If child needs break, log NOT_OBSERVED without punishment. 23–25: Child chooses where they would want a real safe sign (e.g., play storage), but does not go unaccompanied to find it; set actual later session for independent fresh revisit.

CO-PLAY (20–25 minutes)

A caregiver and child are two “sign inspectors.” ENTRY: Offer the same planned uncoached PK3 0,3,5 or PK4 0,6,9,whole 10 unfamiliar original signs, inviting the child to choose order and independently find and identify each numeral before caregiver supplies any label. A child who prefers just one or two cards can stop; log which exact glyphs were actually attempted and mark the remaining planned entry glyphs NOT_OBSERVED. This partial and legitimate co-play participation is not presented as full baseline or grade-range evidence. MODEL adult demonstrates reading a different familiar 2 sign; child chooses an ordinary sign context where another example might fit. DURING child creates one neutral sign for adult to locate and name, then adult designs different unseen practice signs and child takes the reader role; they compare whether a bare glyph and a numeral in a fictional shelf label share identity. The adult may help figure out signage meaning but cannot give numeric labels before independent response. EXIT adult privately prepares unseen cards for child to locate/read all source identities without teacher labels, child-owned answer first; adult never counts objects to disclose answer. REVISIT uses safe new household/classroom labels a different day and full grade-range check, documented by identity; no true public-space wandering needed.

TEACHER-LED CLASSROOM (22–28 minutes)

For a sample 20-student room, prepare three reusable tabletop sign stations for groups of ~6–7, one large teacher-only projected example, a small stack of fresh unshown exit cards per learner or small-group rotation, accessible large/tactile options, and a one-row-per-child × six/eleven private ledger. 0–3 ENTRY: Prepare grade-specific unknown sign targets PK3 0,3,5 or PK4 0,6,9,whole 10 for focal children. Teacher may privately check only a manageable subset initially, noting exactly which numeral and child first response were actually observed; remaining pupil/sign identities are NOT_OBSERVED pending a later turn. Choral or group responses are participation, not attributable independent identity evidence. 3–7 MODEL: teacher demonstrates distinguishing printed numeral from surrounding lettering/decorations, zero as glyph, whole ten and upright 6/9 where age appropriate. 7–17 DURING: groups rotate three simulation stations, taking roles as sign holder, sign finder and independent reader; teacher checks actual first responses before peers shout, permits helpful group talk after the first attempt, and intentionally varies sign layouts. This is not a quiet reading or English vocabulary test. 17–25 EXIT: every child receives unmodeled individualized functional sign cards and separately records or communicates identity on private response sheet/AAC; teacher watches recorded attempts, follows up with learners for unclear voice/gaze or accidental copying. A full eleven-symbol check may require subsequent private short sessions; outstanding identities remain unknown. After another date REVISIT: new projected/printed sign formats, private individually attributable answers, accommodations and all identities logged. Do not mark group consensus as twenty validated outcomes.

D. Evidence discipline, differentiation and accommodations

For each individual and each actual source numeral record date/context ID, literal printed numeral and whether 10 was presented whole, font and orientation, how symbol was situated amid neutral surroundings, independent first oral/sign/AAC or receptive symbol response, whether the child first located the numeral, adult/peer hints, any alternate accessible modality, exact false response, and missing trials. I = independent correct recognition, E = incorrect independently attempted, H = correct only after numeric help, N = NOT_OBSERVED/no valid access, A = neutral access assistance, attached without inflating accuracy. A candidate all-range reading requires a valid first independent observation for every 0–5/0–10 glyph; it is not an adopted cut score, educator approval, durable mastery, evidence of same understanding in all modes or an assessment tool supplied by TEA.

Differentiate: For child just beginning, use only two clear signs with familiar numerals, then expand deliberately through complete range over short sessions. For child already confidently reading numeral identities, increase relevant but non-number environmental distractors, keep original numeric shapes legible, never require reading labels. For child affected by visual crowding, lower clutter or offer one sign at a time, record presentation changes, and repeat with usable access; difficulty filtering pictures must not be mislabeled “does not recognize 4.” For multilingual children use known home-language names/AAC; a spoken-English retrieval problem isn't equivalent to a visual recognition failure. For motor needs child directs adult proxy or uses gaze/switch with confirmation, not independent pencil tracing.

Accommodations: Review any child's relevant IEP, IFSP, Section 504 and family/educator communication, sensory, vision and motor plan before actual use. Offer enlarged high-contrast signs, safe raised numerals, close viewing, accessible child-directed selector and extended processing time without timed search pressure. No uncontrolled public walks or sensitive personal signage; no camera/geolocation/recordings/biometric data. Where meaningful visual form is inaccessible, document an alternative supported tactile-symbol identification task and its evidence limits rather than diagnosing or simply failing the child.

Adversarial counterexamples (not source credit): Child counts four pictured apples on a sign without reading 4; locates a red frame memorized as “four” but cannot name the numeral after location changes; reads the word “door” while unable to identify numeral; sees a printed 0 but selects blank as “zero”; recognizes 1 and 0 on separate signs but has never seen whole 10; calls any curled digit nine regardless of 6/9; identifies only five of six PK3 numerals; answers correctly after classmate announces it; guesses among a predictable fixed row. All may be meaningful participation, but do not independently establish full PK3/PK4.V.A.5.
