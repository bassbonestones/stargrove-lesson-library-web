Teach 1-on-1: sunflower word garden

What your child is learning

Some words contain other meaningful words: sun + flower makes sunflower. With the three-year-old target, say the whole word and help your child recognize its parts. With the four-year-old target, offer familiar word parts with pictures or gestures so your child can make the compound. These are developmental directions, not age-placement tests. No reading, spelling, new-word quota or English-only speaking is needed.

Familiarity boundary: For formation, the two base meanings must be familiar; the resulting meaningful conventional compound may be new to the child. Do not reject a first-time SUNFLOWER construction by a child who knows SUN and FLOWER. Recognition begins with an interpretable genuine whole-word prompt; a child hearing STARFISH for the first time may recognize familiar STAR and FISH. Prior familiarity with the completed compound is not a requirement for either target. “Familiar word” checking below is an available comfortable option, never a PK4 prerequisite that the completed compound was previously known.

Get ready

You need one adult, one child and a few comfortable minutes, often about 5–10, shorter if needed. Use broad sun/flower and rain/coat drawings or gestures. Optional picture mats include book/shelf for a model. Keep sheets whole or cut large pieces yourself; children do not need scissors. Printed words are adult labels, not a reading task. Cover distracting neighboring pictures if helpful. No tablet, outdoor trip or special supplies. Do not look directly at the sun or handle unknown plants.

Check that the child knows the meanings: “Show me a flower”; “What do we wear in rain?” Pointing, sign, gaze or a familiar communication device can answer. If words are unfamiliar, enjoy exploring their meanings today rather than testing compound knowledge. Choose a linguistically valid compound using familiar base meanings in the child's language; English combinations do not always translate directly. Use relevant individual access plans, quiet space and comfortable positioning.

Invite a word game

“Would you like flower words, rain words, or to watch first?” Your child can choose another known pair or stop. For recognizing parts, say “Sunflower. What words do you hear inside sunflower?” For making a word, show or gesture sun and flower: “Sun ... flower. What word could we make?” Repeat input naturally and wait without a countdown. Let the child speak, sign, point, use AAC or direct you to move a card. Confirm an unclear choice without choosing for them.

A child's purposeful communication-device symbol can show a word; it is not disqualified merely because the whole compound is visible. Look for the child's use of the two base meanings, not just pressing a button you highlighted. If using picture choices for recognition, offer known meaningful alternatives without an overwhelming array; filling the only two slots by routine does not by itself show word recognition.

Show how, then play

If an example helps, say “Bookshelf. I hear book and shelf. Book is a word; shelf is a word.” Indicate each picture. Then demonstrate “Book ... shelf ... bookshelf, a shelf for books.” Model to your child, not another adult. If book/shelf are unfamiliar, teach the chosen familiar pair directly. Copying you is useful practice, just not independent formation evidence.

Invite the child to make a pretend word garden, tell about rainy weather or simply play with the pictures. Stay with whole→parts for recognition and parts→whole for making compounds. If you say “rain” and the child offers “bow, rainbow” or “drop, raindrop,” welcome that valid choice and gesture/draw the meanings. The child need not choose your planned coat answer.

Respond to what happens

  • Only “sun”: “Yes, sun is in sunflower. Let's listen again.” If needed, teach flower and practice together. Note the part recognized and the help, not full recognition.
  • Three claps for sun-flow-er: “Those are sound beats. We are finding words that mean something: sun, flower.” Show the meanings; don't shame a different kind of sound play.
  • An echo of your whole word: acknowledge practice. Later offer the bases without immediately saying the complete answer.
  • “Dograin” or “flowersun”: welcome a funny story name, then distinguish the familiar conventional compound. Check family/language use before deciding a word is not real.
  • Accurate and interested: notice how the meanings joined; offer another chosen pair or finish happily.
  • Frustrated, tired or uninterested: reduce distractions, teach meanings, watch together, take a break or stop. Never remove communication support or require spoken English to make the response count.

Take one honest look and finish

If willing, let your child try a familiar word again without your immediately supplying its answer. Recognition: you say sunflower, child identifies sun/flower. Formation: you give sun/flower with pictures or gestures and the child makes sunflower, or contributes a second base after your first. A taught word can show the skill. An immediate echo or your moving both cards does not prove the child formed/recognized it.

If rain/coat are already familiar and the child wants more, try raincoat as a different example. This checks generalization; it is not mandatory to credit the familiar-word action. No meaningful response or no opportunity means “not observed,” not “unable.” Unknown support means uncertain evidence, not failure. Stop for fatigue, distress, disinterest or enough useful practice, not a correct-answer quota.

On a real later day invite the familiar word again for retention, or another known compound for generalization. Keep those claims separate. Optional observation sheets are adult notes, not child worksheets. A few private notes about date, words, child action and help are enough. No photos/recordings are needed; don't put identifying child notes in shared files.

This is a written teaching design, not observed child learning, a tested classroom intervention or a working child-only app. Both routes share the same source targets; the technical plan gives the TEA 2022 p.32 references.

Teach a class: a shared word garden

Choose the target and a manageable invitation

Children explore meaningful words inside other words: sunflower contains sun and flower. The three-year-old target is recognizing the parts of a whole compound; the four-year-old target is making a compound from familiar bases with pictures or gestures. These are not rigid age-placement rules. A child can revisit word meanings, observe quietly or use sign/AAC/gaze without being labeled unsuccessful. No reading, spelling or English-only speech requirement.

Plan a short shared invitation and brief focal observations across normal opportunities, not a promise to assess everyone in one sitting. One educator is enough only while the familiar room/activity remains safely supervised. If your current class needs your full attention, teach together and defer individual evidence.

Familiarity boundary: For formation, the two base meanings must be familiar; the resulting meaningful conventional compound may be new to the child. Do not reject a first-time SUNFLOWER construction by a child who knows SUN and FLOWER. Recognition begins with an interpretable genuine whole-word prompt; a child hearing STARFISH for the first time may recognize familiar STAR and FISH. Prior familiarity with the completed compound is not a requirement for either target. “Familiar word” checking below is an available comfortable option, never a PK4 prerequisite that the completed compound was previously known.

Prepare one visible area

Have one large book/shelf model and sun/flower or rain/coat pictures, drawn or from the optional picture mats. Use broad whole sheets or adult-cut large cards, never small loose pieces or child scissors. Labels are for adults; cover irrelevant neighboring pictures when distracting. Children may gesture instead of sharing a scarce card. Extra sets for pairs are optional, not required supplies. No screen, headphones, second adult, separate assessment room, plants or weather trip.

Keep everyone in your normal line of sight. Use existing safe familiar quiet play for children not in a focal turn, not a newly introduced unsupervised station. Offer a quieter position within supervision, accessible pictures/meaningful objects, stable communication devices and child-directed movement. Follow individual plans. Establish familiar meanings through conversation or accessible pointing before interpreting compound skill. Choose linguistically valid compounds with familiar base meanings; don't assume every child knows snow, a bedroom or English compound structure. Ask family language knowledge when readily available, without making family homework an entry condition.

Launch together

“Want to play with flower words, rain words, or watch?” Model BOOKSHELF if book and shelf are familiar: “Bookshelf. I hear book and shelf. Book ... shelf ... bookshelf.” Point to meanings. Otherwise model a known pair, including the target itself when that is what helps. A distinct model is useful for checking, not a rule forbidding direct teaching.

Invite children to make a pretend garden/rain scene with word pictures, gestures or talk, or continue the familiar safe activity. No craft product is required. A shared answer is enjoyable practice; never mark everyone successful from a chorus.

Follow play and take a brief focal turn

Move attention among willing children while keeping the group visible. Let the child choose flower or rain words, another known compound, watching or a break. For part recognition: “Sunflower. What words do you hear inside it?” For formation: show/gesture “Sun ... flower. What word could we make?” Wait at the child's pace, repeat the input if requested and permit their communication route. The child may contribute BOW or DROP after your RAIN and form RAINBOW/RAINDROP; accept valid alternatives rather than insisting on COAT.

You can confirm an unclear gaze/point without choosing. Use interpretable picture choices, not the only two slots filled by routine. A purposeful AAC compound symbol is not automatically an answer supplied by the adult; consider whether the child is using both base meanings. If language/meaning cannot be interpreted, record the limit and teach accessible meaning rather than marking inability.

If a peer supplies an answer, use it as teaching. Do not count the focal child's echo as their own formation or punish the peer. Later offer a conversational turn in sight of the class, possibly with the same taught word without an immediate answer cue. A fresh word is optional, not required evidence purity. If conflict, movement or another supervision need arises, stop the observation, help the class and mark the interrupted opportunity not yet observed.

Respond without turning play into a test queue

A child identifying only SUN has shown one part: acknowledge it, listen again and teach FLOWER if needed. Syllable claps are sound beats, not meaningful base words: contrast gently using pictures. An invented word may be welcome in a story while you separately explore conventional compounds and family language usage. An immediate whole-word echo is supported practice, not independent formation. An accurate child can choose another pair or stop. A struggling/tired child may observe, have meanings explained, use quieter access or stop; do not remove AAC, insist on speech or publish rankings.

Give substantive participation to others through chosen word play, gestures, listening or the familiar activity. Do not leave a long waiting line while testing children one by one. Across later ordinary opportunities invite those not yet heard, including quieter children, with permission to decline. No fixed class size, tested staffing ratio or same-day completion quota is claimed.

Close and return honestly

Close with any willing child's favorite word or your own example, without calling the class display individual mastery. Privately note only what you actually observed: child, date, language/word input, response, exact adult/peer help and what to invite next. A familiar taught-word response can show source action. A different known compound can add transfer evidence; unfamiliar vocabulary cannot fairly test that. A later day with the familiar word may inform retention. None is pre-earned by attendance or a completed worksheet.

The optional observation sheets are private adult prompts, not a mandatory form. No photos/recordings are needed; keep child identifiers outside shared repositories. Unknown assistance stays uncertain; no accessible opportunity stays not observed, not failed.

This is a written classroom design, not an observed teacher trial, child outcome, physical-print accessibility validation or functional child-only app. The technical plan identifies the TEA 2022 p.32 sources. Lessons in the wider family still need their own review.

Sunflower word garden: hear the parts, make a compound

Purpose, source and scope

A compound word joins meaningful words into another word. SUN and FLOWER are words; the three syllable beats in sunflower are a different sound activity. This short, playful lesson offers two separate developmental targets, not a grade-placement test:

  • PK3.III.B.3: “Child recognizes the individual words in a compound word.” Say SUNFLOWER; the child identifies SUN and FLOWER, by speech or an interpretable accessible response.
  • PK4.III.B.3: “Child uses two familiar base words to form a compound word with pictorial or gestural supports.” Offer SUN and FLOWER with pictures or gestures; the child forms SUNFLOWER. A child may also contribute a second familiar base to the adult's first, as the publisher's RAIN → BOW/RAINBOW or DROP/RAINDROP examples show.

Prepare a safe invitation

Use one adult and one child, at a comfortable table or floor space, for roughly 5–10 minutes if enjoyable, shorter if needed. A teacher can instead use the classroom arrangement below. No second adult, device, reading ability, outdoor trip or purchased supplies is needed.

Have one set of broad SUN, FLOWER, RAIN and COAT pictures from original picture mats, or draw them/gesture their meanings. Page 2 has BOOK and SHELF for a model. Keep pages whole or cut large cards yourself; scissors and small pieces stay away from children. Page 3 is adult teaching advice, not a child worksheet. The optional observation pages are confidential adult notes, not an assignment. Words printed on pictures are adult labels, never a reading test. Cover an irrelevant neighboring picture or use separate broad drawings when it distracts.

Check meanings conversationally: “Show me a flower”; “What do we wear when it rains?” A child need not define words verbally. If sun/flower are unfamiliar, explore their meanings first with safe familiar pictures, objects, gestures or language support; defer any inference about compound skill. Do not look at the sun or require touching plants. Use BOOKSHELF only if book/shelf are familiar; otherwise model a familiar pair such as RAINCOAT and choose SUNFLOWER for practice. One meaningful compound taught well is enough for this session.

Agree how the child will answer: speak, sign, AAC, eye gaze, point or direct an adult to move a picture. Confirm ambiguous choices without supplying the answer: “Did you mean this one?” Stable AAC vocabulary may already contain a compound symbol; that is not automatically cheating. Look for the child's purposeful use of both bases, not whether an answer exists on a screen. For PK3 picture recognition offer relevant known choices with an optional known distractor, or let the child name/gesture parts; two cards placed in the only two slots do not by themselves distinguish recognition from copying a routine. Never make a larger array a memory or motor hurdle.

ENTRY: invite, notice, do not test endurance

“Would you like to play with flower words, rain words, or watch first?” Accept a different valid familiar compound, quiet observation or a break. For PK3 say “Sunflower. What words do you hear inside sunflower?” Repeat the whole naturally if requested. For PK4 show/gesture SUN and FLOWER and name each: “Sun ... flower. What word could we make?” Wait at the child's pace. Record what was actually said/shown beforehand and the child's response if useful; no response may mean unfamiliar language, inaccessible input, not ready or no interest. Do not diagnose.

MODEL: make the relationship visible

If the child needs an example, model BOOKSHELF while the child watches, not to an imaginary second adult. “Bookshelf. I hear book and shelf. Book is a word; shelf is a word.” Indicate each meaningful picture. For formation: “Book ... shelf. Together: bookshelf, a shelf for books.” Then invite a try with SUN/FLOWER or another familiar pair. It is also fine to model the same word when that is what helps; label immediate repetition as supported practice rather than independent formation. A distinct model is useful for checking, not a ban on effective teaching.

DURING: let the child grow a word garden

The child chooses a pair and where it belongs in an imagined garden or rainy-day scene, or simply experiments with the cards. No drawing, craft or story performance is required. With PK3 start from the whole word and invite its parts. With PK4 start with the two base meanings and support combining them. A child can offer a second word after “rain”: accept RAINBOW or RAINDROP when the child knows the meanings; do not insist on COAT because it was in the adult plan. Use gestures or quick drawings for the chosen bases. Illustrations and pretend contexts can support real language learning; actual weather or physical sunflowers are not required.

Notice and respond: - Child identifies SUN only: “Sun is a word in sunflower. Let's listen again: sunflower.” If still unsure, explain FLOWER and demonstrate. Record one recognized part and help given, not full recognition. Return to a comfortable chance later. - Child claps sun-flow-er: “You heard sound beats. This game is about words that mean something: sun, flower.” Show both meanings and try together. Clapping is legitimate play, but not the same target evidence. - Child repeats your whole PK4 answer: warmly acknowledge practice, then later offer the familiar bases without speaking the completed answer immediately. Do not erase learning because the word was taught before. - Child says FLOWERSUN or invents DOGRAIN: “That could be a funny name for your story. In this word game we are making words people use; sun and flower make sunflower.” Explore whether the child's family/language actually uses the offered compound before rejecting it. Creative play is welcome; its lexical status is a separate observation. - Child is accurate: notice the relationship (“You joined rain and coat into raincoat”), invite another chosen example or stop while it is enjoyable. No need to raise difficulty merely because time remains. - Child struggles or withdraws: reduce distractions, revisit meanings, retain access supports, offer watch/stop. Never publicly compare children, demand English pronunciation or remove AAC to make a check “independent.”

EXIT: one honest look, not a pass/fail ceremony

When the child is willing, offer one compound using familiar base meanings again without immediately supplying the relevant answer. PK3: whole SUNFLOWER then the child indicates its meaningful parts. PK4: SUN and FLOWER with pictures/gestures then the child forms the word; alternatively the child supplies a meaningful second base to RAIN and forms the compound. Do not score a teacher's completed answer, copying a peer or unobserved turn as the child's own action. Record whether the response followed a direct answer cue, neutral access, or uncertain support. A correct supported teaching response remains a supported response, not erased; an uncertain response remains uncertain, not failed.

If meaningful RAIN/COAT are already familiar and the child wants more, change to RAINCOAT as an optional transfer check. If unfamiliar, teach meanings instead and do not report transfer. The child need not explain definitions in speech or solve a new compound to show the stated source action. End for fatigue, distress, disinterest or adequate useful practice. Note “not observed” when there was no interpretable opportunity, not “unable.”

REVISIT: return on a real later occasion

On another day or naturally later encounter, invite the familiar word again, noting the date, prompt and support. This can inform retention. A different known compound can inform generalization; these are separate claims. At authoring both remain NOT_OBSERVED. Do not prefill results or claim mastery from a single answer.

Optional progression, not published stages

PK3 C3-1: explore known base meanings and recognize parts of SUNFLOWER. C3-2: repeat recognition with RAINCOAT if familiar. C3-3: explore BOOKSHELF; no required fixed order. C3-4: optional changed-example generalization. PK4 C4-1: combine SUN/FLOWER with supports. C4-2: combine RAIN/COAT. C4-3: form BOOKSHELF or child-chosen familiar compound. C4-4: contribute a second base to an offered first. C4-5: optional transfer across familiar contexts. All are local teaching options; do not convert them to age quotas. Older children may revisit a prerequisite without stigma.

Four delivery routes and actual implementation boundaries

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: follow the invitation, model only as needed, supported play, one interpretable check and later return above. CO-PLAY: caregiver joins the child's chosen garden/rain play and takes genuine turns, while keeping adult answers separate from child evidence. Neither route requires an app.

CLASSROOM: One educator keeps all children in the same familiar supervised area. Start with a brief BOOK/SHELF demonstration visible to the group, then invite children to explore picture pairs/gestures together or continue an already safe familiar quiet activity. Use one large display set; optional extra broad sets go to pairs only if already available. No compulsory printing per child. Children without cards may gesture, tell a word, listen or watch without being labeled unsuccessful. Avoid unsupervised new centers. The teacher takes a focal child's response while still seeing the group; do not use headphones, a separate room or turn away for prolonged testing. If supervision needs attention, stop the observation, help the class and mark the focal turn not yet observed. Across ordinary later turns invite children not yet heard, including quiet children, without forcing them. A chorus is shared practice, not individual evidence. If a peer blurts the answer, teach with it and later offer a private conversational opportunity in sight of the group; never silence peers punitively or require an unheard word. Record only observed individual actions and real supports. No class size, staffing ratio or completion-in-one-session guarantee is asserted.

False credit, inclusion and limits

False credit includes counting syllable claps as word recognition, picture placement chosen by an adult, a group chorus as each child's formation, or an immediate whole-word echo as independent combination. False rejection includes refusing a taught compound, the publisher's child-supplied second-base example, a purposeful AAC choice, a home-language compound or continuing supports. Keep lexical content meaningful in the language used; English compound structure does not translate mechanically. With family input when readily available, choose true familiar compounds; do not make family response or specialist availability a prerequisite to inclusive participation. When the adult cannot interpret the language, record that uncertainty and seek ordinary qualified language help rather than inventing success/failure. Follow relevant individual access plans; this document does not diagnose or replace specialist advice.

Notes can be a few private words: date, language/word input, child action, exact help, interpretation and next invitation. Keep names, recordings and child data out of repository/public pages. No photographs or recordings are needed. Print/digital quality checks concern documents only. What observations mean

Write plain facts rather than U/H/A/X codes: what adult/peer supplied, what the child did, access retained, and whether meaning was interpretable. An immediate echo is not formation evidence; a later purposeful response on the same word may be. A child-controlled AAC symbol is not disqualified simply because it displays the compound. No opportunity/decline/inaccessible language is NOT_OBSERVED; uncertain support is uncertain, not adult-only and not child failure. Picture selection must communicate the lexical relation rather than an adult-directed matching routine. No diagnosis or mastery claim from one trial.

One educator with a class

Use a short familiar book/shelf model in a shared visible space. Children explore broad pictures in optional pairs, gesture or participate in an established safely supervised quiet activity. No extra adult or separate testing room. Take a brief focal response while retaining sight of the class. Stop observing when supervision requires attention. Rotate future opportunities fairly across willing children, including quieter children; record unobserved turns as such. Peer answers and choruses are teaching, not each child's individual result. A later individual conversational response can clarify evidence without punitive silence or compulsory new words.
