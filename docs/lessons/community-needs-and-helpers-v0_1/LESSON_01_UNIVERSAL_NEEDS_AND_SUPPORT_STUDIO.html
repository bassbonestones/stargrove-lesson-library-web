Needs and Support Studio: teach one child

Make a small fictional scene together, then explore needs shared by people and what helpers do. One adult and one child can begin with paper and conversation; inventing a story, reading and drawing are not prerequisites.

What this teaches

The Texas older-preschool objectives are understanding that all people need food, clothing and shelter, and discussing the roles and responsibilities of family, school and community helpers. These are two separate goals. A younger preschooler may join as enrichment, but this domain has no published younger-preschool outcome. Use the child's starting understanding rather than age as a gate.

Do not try to establish everything in one sitting. Start with one need or one helper context, then build across suitable encounters. Naming a favorite food is not yet all three shared needs. Saying a job title alone does not explain a responsibility. A familiar learned explanation is worthwhile: neither novelty nor original invention is required. These guidelines inform teaching, not a pass/fail readiness checklist.

Prepare in a few minutes

Have two blank sheets and a thick marker for yourself; offer safe drawing tools if the child wants them. One sheet is yours for examples, the other is for the child's choices or your child-directed marks. Narrate or sketch anonymous people at a pretend home, school or library. No special illustrated cards, food, costumes, personal photos, addresses or household questions are needed. Optional printable example pages contain teaching answers, so use them for practice and set them aside for a learning check. Blank paper is enough.

Check the materials and space for the child's safety and comfort. Allow home language, familiar sign or communication aids, spoken ideas, pointing with neutral clarification, or directing your drawing. Augmentative and alternative communication means tools or methods that support expression when speech is not the chosen route. Use established access supports; do not take them away to test independence. Describe anything the child cannot see or read. Do not guess the meaning of an unclear gesture.

Plan roughly 5–12 flexible minutes, shorter if needed. No actual teacher trial has established a required duration.

Invite a choice

“Would you like to make up people at home, school or the library? Or somewhere else?” Accept choosing an offered setting. If needed, start a neutral pretend story and invite changes. The child can also pass.

Ask one question: “What kinds of things do all people need?” Or, for a helper focus, “Who might help in this pretend school, and what might they do?” Wait and listen. You have explained the task; there is no need for a surprise test before teaching. Silence is not proof of inability. Offer another communication route, teach, or stop.

Show an example when useful

On your sheet, say, “These people like different foods, but they both need food. They can wear different clothes, but both need clothing and shelter.” People still have needs when they do not have enough resources. Do not rank houses, diets or families, and do not imply every outing requires eating immediately.

For helpers, offer a role and action: “A school custodian helps keep rooms usable. A caregiver might prepare a meal. A librarian can help find information.” These are examples, not the only correct people or tasks. One person may hold several roles; helpers need not meet every basic need. Inviting another plausible idea keeps the discussion open.

Let the child shape the scene

The child can draw, narrate, sign, use a communication aid or direct your marks. Explore one part at a time. If they mention bread only, acknowledge that bread is a kind of food, then teach clothing or shelter as needed. A food preference is not a nutrition or morality test. Do not count your category prompts as the child's independent ideas.

If the child says only a job name, ask, “What could they do?” Accept the meaning without a precise title. If a stereotype appears, explain that different adults can do the task and explore an alternative without shaming. Do not frame unknown adults as safe because of a uniform. For real safety concerns, use the child's identified trusted adult.

If the task overwhelms, work with one concrete need or helper and return later. If understanding is already clear, offer an optional different context or another plausible solution. Do not demand difficulty or novelty merely to award credit.

Notice what was actually learned

If the child is willing, set aside your answer examples and ask an open question about the part taught. A familiar setting is fine. Record whether the answer was immediately echoed, copied, prompted, or independently communicated using learned knowledge. When uncertain, say uncertain rather than guess.

Needs evidence must concern food, clothing and shelter as shared human needs. Helper evidence should separately reflect family, school and community roles and responsibilities over suitable encounters. A real child discussing fictional people is having an actual discussion; no helper visit is required. A copied page or imagined future app is not evidence of child learning. Do not infer unobserved parts from a strong response in another part.

A brief private note can record actual date, fictional setting, communicated meaning, which part it supports, and help. Separate help accessing the task from help supplying the answer. Unknown help or an unclear response means no independent conclusion, not lack of knowledge. No opportunity means not yet observed. Do not collect household details or audio, and do not diagnose or assign a mastery score.

End and return later

Thank the child and stop before fatigue or distress. Revisit on an actual later day using a familiar or varied fictional setting if helpful. A planned return is not evidence of retention; a new context is optional and should be named separately if used to explore transfer.

If the child volunteers a difficult home situation, thank them, avoid public questioning or promises of secrecy, and stop or return gently to fiction. Do not use it as curriculum evidence. Follow the setting's established safeguarding and support procedures.

This is a written candidate teaching design. No child outcomes, teacher usability trial, physical-print accessibility acceptance or functioning solo game is claimed.

Needs and Support Studio: teach a class

Children explore made-up scenes while one teacher supports learning and listens to manageable individual contributions. This is not three simultaneous stations that require extra staff.

Goal and age fit

For older preschoolers, the Texas objectives are understanding that all people need food, clothing and shelter, and discussing the roles and responsibilities of family, school and community helpers. Keep those goals distinct. Younger preschoolers may join as enrichment; this domain has no published younger-preschool outcome. Flexible starting points matter more than a rigid age gate.

Full coverage can develop over several encounters. A single food choice does not show all shared needs; naming a job does not by itself discuss responsibility. Familiar learned meanings and actual discussion about fictional people can be valid. Children do not have to invent a new story or meet all parts in one session. The guidelines are not a child-failure or readiness checklist.

Set up for the staff you actually have

Use one large sheet or board for your example, blank paper for participating children, and safe drawing materials available without crowding. Prepare enough paper for each child who wants it, or let children narrate or direct an adult's marks instead. Keep children and materials within your normal safe sightline. No food, costumes, real family photos, personal addresses or internet contacts are needed. Optional printable examples have answers: reserve them for teaching and supported practice rather than use a completed sheet as a score.

Pick a brief fictional home, school or library setting. You can narrate and sketch simple anonymous figures; elaborate illustrations are not supplied or necessary. Read or describe needed text and images. Use established language, sign, augmentative communication and access supports. Do not require English speech, reading, drawing, touching materials or public performance. If one adult cannot safely provide the needed access and oversee the group, simplify to group practice and postpone individual observation rather than assume a second adult.

Plan a short introduction and flexible practice period using the class's usual safe routine. The one-child guide's 5–12 minutes is not a validated whole-class schedule. Do not promise to observe everyone within a fixed session.

Start together without a public quiz

“Today we can make up people and places. You can choose a pretend home, school, library or another safe idea.” Children can listen, suggest a choice, use familiar communication supports, or pass. You may offer a setting without supplying the target answer.

Invite one focus: “What kinds of things do all people need?” or “Who might help in this pretend school, and what could they do?” Listen without requiring a chorus. If children need teaching, provide it; a cold independent baseline is not a prerequisite.

On your own example, explain that people may eat different foods and wear different clothing but all need food, clothing and shelter. Needs persist when resources are unavailable. Do not compare actual homes or diets. For helpers, show role plus responsibility: a custodian keeps school spaces usable, a caregiver may prepare a meal, a librarian helps find information. Different people can share tasks; no gender, family structure or job is the only acceptable answer.

Give everyone meaningful participation

Children can make their own fictional scene on paper, describe it to a nearby willing partner within the supervised area, or quietly watch and contribute through an established access route. Drawing quality is not the objective. Partners may share ideas without being labeled failures, but a peer's answer does not become another child's evidence.

While these activities continue safely, have one short conversation with one focal child. Ask about a need or helper in their scene. Do not run simultaneous individual assessments or demand the teacher write and supervise beyond capacity. If the room needs attention, pause the observation and attend to the group. Record that turn as incomplete if needed.

Choose different focal children in later sessions using a private simple rotation reminder, including quiet children and those using aided communication. Others are not yet observed, not unsuccessful. Avoid public scoreboards and compulsory turns. A child may opt for a quieter conversation later. If even a brief focal exchange compromises oversight, do meaningful group learning only and defer individual inference.

Respond to what children mean

If a child names one food, acknowledge it as food and teach the other categories when useful. Do not rank “good” and “bad” foods or inspect home meals. If they name a job only, ask “What might that person do?” Accept a plausible task without requiring a formal title.

If a stereotype appears, offer a different possible adult/helper and discuss without shame. If an unsafe responsibility is suggested, clarify gently; an occupation or uniform is not permission to approach an unknown adult. Follow established trusted-adult safety routines in real situations.

If concepts or language overwhelm, use one concrete need or helper and return later. If a child communicates understanding readily, an optional different fictional setting can extend discussion. Novelty is not a requirement for source evidence. Preserve access supports while gradually reducing answer-bearing prompts, and record when you supplied content.

Keep individual observations honest

A group chant, your completed example, attendance or one volunteer does not establish every child's understanding. If a willing focal child has a fair opportunity, put away answer examples and invite their own meaning. A familiar setting is fine. Note prior modeling and current support; immediate copying remains practice, while independently used learned knowledge can be informative. Ambiguous communication or unknown help means uncertainty, not inability.

Needs observations concern all people and food, clothing and shelter. Helper observations separately concern family, school and community roles and responsibilities over suitable encounters. Do not infer missing parts from another strong part. Real child discussion about fiction is actual discussion; no live helper visit is needed. Keep a private minimal note with date, fictional context, actual contribution and help. Do not record private household details, audio or diagnoses, and do not turn notes into a mastery/readiness score.

Close, protect dignity, and revisit

Thank the children for ideas and stop before fatigue or distress. Plan another encounter only when useful. On an actual later date, a familiar or changed fictional scene can support continued learning; a scheduled return is not already observed retention. Optional changed-context transfer is a distinct inference, not a mandatory test.

If someone discloses hardship, respond calmly, thank them, avoid public probing or a secrecy promise, and shift to fiction or stop. Do not use the disclosure as lesson evidence. Follow the setting's existing safeguarding/support procedures.

This is an internally written candidate design, not a tested class-size/staffing claim, live teacher trial, child outcome study or completed digital game. The one-child route and this classroom route share the same targets but do not imply proven equal effectiveness.

B.1 concerns all people and three categories, not merely one favorite meal. Foods, garments and shelters vary; people still have needs when resources are unavailable. A library visit does not mean a person needs to eat at that moment. Shelter does not mean only a detached owned house. B.3 concerns a helper's role and related responsibility in family, school and community contexts. An occupational label alone is insufficient, but a familiar learned explanation may be valid. One person may hold several roles. Responsibilities need not be unique to a job, gender, family structure or paid employment. Do not require every helper to supply every basic need.

Prepare honestly available materials

Use two blank sheets of paper, a thick pencil or marker for the adult, and optional large safe drawing tools for the child. An adult can narrate and draw two simple anonymous figures; no bought cards or detailed illustrations are required. Offer a familiar pretend home, school, or library, or the child's safe imaginary choice. The adult's sketch quality is irrelevant. No real food, costumes, money, photos of children, household records, addresses or internet contact are needed.

Optional original stage cards and community scenes are adult-readable paper supports, not a prereader app. Their example pages contain teaching answers and are for MODEL or supported practice. Use a blank sheet for a check; keep answer-containing examples out of sight and record prior exposure. Read or describe anything the child needs; do not assess reading. Adult-provided neutral narration is access, but supplying the target category or helper action is conceptual help.

Check materials for local safety and actual access needs. Keep paper pieces large, clear the work area, offer a seated or hands-free route, and follow the child's established communication and support plans. No mandatory touching, eye contact, English speech, drawing, acting or public performance. Neutral translation, familiar AAC/sign, child-directed gaze or adult scribing can preserve the construct. The adult must not invent meaning from an ambiguous gesture: neutrally clarify or record uncertainty. Never remove ongoing access supports just to make a check harder.

ENTRY — a safe invitation, not a prerequisite test

Say, “Let's make up people for a story. Would you like a home, school, library, or somewhere else?” The child may select, adapt or decline the choice. If choosing is hard, say, “I can start a pretend library story, and you can change it.” Choosing an adult-offered setting is not failure and does not supply the target answers. Explain the activity before observing; there is no requirement for a cold unfamiliar test.

For a needs focus, ask, “What kinds of things do all people need?” If useful, show two simple fictional people of different ages and ask whether their needs have anything in common. For helpers, ask, “Who could help in this pretend school, and what might they do?” Listen to the child's meaning before adding content. An initial silence may reflect language, attention, uncertainty or unwillingness, not absence of understanding. Offer another communication route, model if wanted, or stop. Record only what was actually communicated, not a guessed score.

MODEL — show the reasoning when it helps

Use your own small scene, visibly separate from the child's work: “These pretend people like different foods. They both need food. They may wear different clothes, but both need clothing and shelter. Having a need is not the same as having enough of it.” Do not compare real children's circumstances or rank families, diets, homes or jobs.

On a separate helper example say, “A school custodian helps keep rooms usable and safe. A caregiver might prepare a meal. A librarian can help someone find a book. More than one kind of person can do these things.” These are possibilities, not mandatory job assignments. Explain role plus action. Invite disagreement with a plausible alternative. Modeled or echoed answers are supported learning, not independently generated evidence. Teaching is valuable even when the child needs the complete example.

DURING — the child controls the scene

Let the child draw, narrate, sign, use AAC or direct your marks. Ask how people in the scene meet needs, then listen. If the child mentions bread only, say, “Bread is one kind of food. People can eat different foods.” Teach clothing and shelter when needed without marking that prompted sequence independent. Food preference is not a moral or nutrition test; do not contrast candy with “good” people or inspect diets. Later offer an open question without naming the missing category.

Explore a helper responsibility in one context at a time. “What could that person do to help?” Accept a plausible action expressed without the exact job title. Ask a genuinely open follow-up, such as “Tell me more,” rather than feeding the action. If a child says only mothers cook, respond, “Many different adults can prepare food,” then explore a non-stereotyped example. If a role is unsafe or implausible, gently clarify responsibility; this is not training to trust unknown adults. In a real concern, seek the child's identified trusted adult, not a costume or occupational label.

Contingent choices: If categories overwhelm, teach one concrete need and return to the others later. If all needs are understood but helper discussion is absent, work on one helper context without withholding needs evidence. If a helper idea is clear but needs are not, record the helper part separately. If the child already communicates both, invite an optional different setting or alternative solution; do not add arbitrary difficulty to earn credit. Familiar correct learned meaning is not disqualified for lacking novelty. Child-created stories enrich agency, but originality is not either published source objective.

EXIT — an honest small learning check

Only if the child is willing, put away your answer examples and ask one open invitation relevant to the part taught. “What do people need, even when their lives look different?” or “Who helps in this pretend school, and what do they do?” A familiar setting is fair. An optional changed setting tests transfer but is not a prerequisite for the source action. Record whether the setting and answers were previously modeled. Immediate echo or copying does not warrant independence; a child can independently use previously learned knowledge without inventing a novel answer. If uncertain, record supported or unclear evidence rather than guess.

For B.1, document actual indication of food, clothing and shelter as shared human needs, with appropriate context or neutral clarification. One picture selection or a prompted three-item chant cannot alone establish universality. For B.3, document actual discussion of role and responsibility for each family, school and community component across suitable encounters. A real child discussing fictional people with an adult can satisfy the discussion context; a nonexistent avatar simulation or adult-authored script cannot provide child evidence. Speech is not mandatory, but expressive accessible discussion must be attributable to the child.

False-positive challenge: A child copies three teacher-labeled symbols and repeats “librarian”; the finished page looks complete, yet neither full objective is shown. Record the copied work as practice, teach as needed and observe a later fair opportunity. False-rejection challenge: A child uses familiar AAC to explain that a caregiver cooks and a school custodian cleans; these are source-relevant components even without a new story, English sentence or novel job title. Do not award unobserved community or needs components.

Record, stop and REVISIT

Use a private minimal note: actual date; fictional setting; source component; child's words/actions or directed communication; adult model/cues; access; and next teaching step. Do not record identifying household details or audio. Mark child-owned contribution U, neutral access A, conceptual help H, or unclear provenance X. Unknown help means no independent conclusion, not proof the child lacks knowledge. No opportunity or inaccessible/ambiguous response is NOT_OBSERVED. Partial coverage stays partial. End with “Thank you for your ideas.” Stop rather than repeatedly probe for a score. On an actual later date, revisit a familiar or changed safe setting and record it then; a planned date is not retention. Keep REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED until it actually happens. No percent cutoff, diagnosis or mastery claim is supported by this design review.

If a child spontaneously shares a difficult home situation, respond calmly, “Thank you for telling me.” Do not question them publicly, promise secrecy, or use the disclosure as lesson evidence. Shift back to fiction or stop. Follow the setting's established safeguarding and support procedures if a concern requires adult action.

Human delivery routes and future game boundaries

ADULT-LED ONE CHILD: Follow the sequence above with one adult and child, one scene and one focus at a time. Offer the choice to draw, narrate, direct marks or pass. The companion facilitator guide provides a short run sheet. Model when needed, preserve access, and record only actual contributions.

CLASSROOM: One teacher can run this at a supervised table or whole group, not three unattended specialist stations. Prepare one large paper scene and ordinary safe paper/drawing materials within the teacher's sightline. Give a brief fictional introduction; other children draw or discuss their own made-up scene while the teacher listens to one focal child. Participation remains meaningful without waiting for a public quiz. Observe only as many children as supervision permits; record unobserved children as NOT_OBSERVED and rotate focal turns in later sessions. If safe oversight cannot be maintained, use group practice only and defer individual inference. Peers can contribute, but a choral answer, copied page or another child's response is not every child's evidence. Offer quiet/private communication and no forced household sharing.

CO-PLAY: Adult and child contribute different ideas to a shared fictional scene. The child can change the adult's suggestion or choose a different plausible helper. Attribute contributions honestly; co-authorship is not a defect, but an adult-supplied action cannot be relabeled independent child evidence.

SOLO — NOT IMPLEMENTED: A future accessible digital experience would need child-controlled narration, non-stigmatizing examples, usable expression, contingent feedback and truthful provenance.
