Teach 1-on-1: Choose something useful

What your child explores

A consumer is someone who gets or uses goods or services. Your child chooses useful supplies for their own art idea, then explores another person's use of something. No buying, money counting, reading, or memorizing “consumer” is needed. This addresses the Texas prekindergarten-four consumer-understanding goal. Younger children can enjoy it as enrichment, without a formal prekindergarten-three social-studies score.

Start with a short invitation and follow interest. You do not need to finish every step in one sitting.

Get ready

Gather two sheets of paper, two safe washable drawing tools (or the child's familiar accessible alternative), one large block or simply a spoken building story, and paper for your own notes. Inspect tools, loose caps and surfaces for the child's safety needs. No printer, screen, shopping trip, food, small toys or real money is needed.

Use the child's familiar language, signs or communication system. They may point, enact, direct you to move materials, or choose not to touch. You may keep the blanket story below for an optional new-context question. Familiar taught examples can still show understanding; do not confuse prior familiarity with an immediately supplied answer. The family print sheets contain practice answers and are optional adult references, not assessment cards.

1. Hear their idea first

Put out paper and tools. Say, “This maker wants to make a picture. What could they use? Show me what they could do.” Wait. Let the child choose a material or suggest a different useful idea, including folding instead of drawing.

Notice what they actually do before you explain. You may ask “Show or tell me more.” Avoid selecting the answer for them. Watching, declining, or needing a better way to communicate is not proof they do not understand.

2. Show a different example

With a large block or an imagined one, say: “I want to build a shelter for my toy. I borrow a block and use it to build. I'm a consumer of the block. Someone provides it. I don't have to buy it.” You can play both roles; no child has to lend anything.

Explain the connection between the purpose and the item, not just the label. Someone may provide something in one situation and use something in another.

3. Make something their way

“What would you like to make? What could you use?” Let the child decide and change their mind. Help with safe access without taking over the purpose. Ask how the item helps when that fits the play, not after every mark. The result belongs to the child, not a model to copy.

If the child talks only about a brand or coins, acknowledge the interest and show purposeful use of another safe item. If the choice surprises you, ask them to demonstrate before calling it wrong. If it does not help their stated purpose, explore another option together. If they become frustrated, offer one familiar purpose with two accessible options, or stop.

You may offer to hold paper when the child requests that help and directs the work. This can introduce a service. Record any idea you supplied as teaching help. Moving an object exactly as the child directs can be access instead; both kinds of support may occur together. Keep necessary access when you stop giving hints.

4. Notice their understanding; try another story only if useful

If the child already clearly shows how someone obtains or uses something for a purpose in their project, record that limited evidence and any help. No extra task is required. For an optional new-context question, you can use: “Lee borrows a blanket from Jo to make a comfortable place for a toy to rest. What is Lee doing? What is Jo doing? How does the blanket help?” You do not need a real blanket or toy. This is not a real sleeping arrangement.

The child can show, sign or tell the relationship. If they clearly show Lee getting/using the blanket for a purpose, that is relevant evidence even without the word consumer or a complete spoken answer about Jo. A sensible alternative purpose may also work. If they only name the blanket, ask neutrally for more; do not supply the relationship and call it independent. If the scenario is confusing or inaccessible, change to an accessible familiar context or defer. Familiarity does not itself invalidate understanding.

For an optional changed-context question, another example is Sam receiving help from Ren fixing a torn pretend paper sign. Ask what Sam receives and what Ren does. After an answer hint, teach warmly and record help; immediate repetition is not independent transfer. Later meaningful child-owned understanding in a familiar case can still be relevant evidence. A new example is needed only for the separate new-context claim.

What to note and when to return

On a private note write the date, scenario, actual action/message, prior exposure, access provided, any idea you supplied, and what remains uncertain. Do not upload names, recordings or household shopping information. A copied word, coin count or adult guess is not evidence of consumer understanding. Goods or services may each provide an example; do not require every extension as a formal checklist. Meaningful child-directed action need not be spoken.

Stop for refusal, tiredness, distress or inadequate access. On a genuinely later day, offer a meaningful familiar or changed useful-object or helpful-service situation. Record what happens then; changed context can add transfer information but is not required for a later revisit. Until then, later learning is not yet observed. These notes guide teaching; they are not a diagnosis, validated score or proof of general mastery. This lesson is a written design, not a record of a child trial.

Teach a class: Our useful art choices

Goal and starting point

Children explore being a consumer: someone who gets or uses goods or services. They choose supplies for their own art purpose and show how a person uses something for a purpose, in a familiar or new situation. The Texas prekindergarten-four target is consumer understanding, not money arithmetic, speaking the word consumer, English fluency, or identifying every kind of service. Younger children's participation is enrichment, not a formal prekindergarten-three social-studies score.

This route assumes one educator supervising the group. Teach the group today and collect brief individual observations over later days when needed; no promise of assessing everyone in one session.

Set up a manageable space

Use the usual art/play area where you can see and attend to all children. Provide safe paper and familiar washable tools for every participant, or use an already workable sharing arrangement. Do not create a shortage to test children's choices. Check caps, size, surfaces and actual individual safety/access plans. A large block or oral building story supports your demonstration. No shop queue, cashier station, money, small toys, food, printer or second adult is required.

Offer a quiet option in the same supervised area and the children's familiar languages, signs, communication systems and child-directed motor help. They can dictate an idea or direct your hand without giving up ownership. Keep your observation slip private. Family print sheets are optional adult practice references containing answers; do not display them during a first-response check.

The blanket and sign-repair stories below are optional new-context questions, not required evidence tasks. An immediately repeated peer answer is supported, not independent meaning; later child-owned understanding in a familiar case can still be valid. Do not withhold helpful instruction to preserve a test.

Open together, with room for different ideas

Invite: “This maker wants to make a picture. What could they use? Show me what they could do.” Let willing children offer ideas without demanding a chorus or public turn. Notice any actual first response, but don't infer that quiet children share a volunteer's understanding. Folding, marks, tactile arrangement or another useful purpose can be legitimate.

Then model elsewhere: “I want a shelter for my toy. I borrow a large block and use it to build. I'm a consumer of the block. Someone provides it. Buying isn't required.” Use an imaginary block if necessary and play both roles yourself. Explain purpose and use, not a label to copy. People can provide in one event and consume in another.

Let each child make choices

Children choose what to make and which supplies help. They may change their plan, share ideas with willing peers, direct a proxy, or take the normal quiet alternative. Ask “How does that help your idea?” when it fits the work. Do not interrupt every mark or require identical products.

If a child names a brand or counts coins, acknowledge the interest without treating it as proof of consumer understanding; demonstrate purposeful use on another object. If an unusual choice serves their purpose, preserve it. If unclear, invite a demonstration rather than supplying the explanation. Simplify choices or offer a break if frustration grows. Optional service practice is child-requested help holding paper while the child still directs the work.

Keep language and motor access while fading hints. An adult may provide both access and a conceptual idea; record both rather than calling all assistance neutral. Never take away essential supports to test independence.

Observe one child only when supervision permits

During familiar art/play, notice a nearby willing child's actual understanding or offer an optional short story while maintaining supervision. Everyone else continues meaningful art or familiar play, not a silent assessment queue. If a child needs care or you cannot safely attend to both tasks, pause the check. Defer individual observations rather than create an unattended station. Track who still needs an opportunity across days, privately and without ranking.

If the child has already clearly shown the consumer relationship in their own work, record that evidence and help without another task. Optional transfer story: “Lee borrows a blanket from Jo to make a comfortable place for a toy to rest. What is Lee doing? What is Jo doing? How does the blanket help?” No real blanket, toy or sleep arrangement is necessary. Children may enact, point through their established communication method, sign, use home language, or direct a proxy.

Clear child-owned demonstration of Lee obtaining/using the blanket for a purpose is relevant evidence. Saying consumer, answering every question and naming the provider are not mandatory separate cutoffs. A blanket label alone is ambiguous; ask “Show or tell me more,” not “Lee uses it, right?” If vocabulary/context is unfamiliar, use an accessible familiar situation or defer. A new context is not a prerequisite for consumer understanding. A sound different use is not automatically wrong.

If a separate new-context question would help, another example is: Sam receives help from Ren fixing a torn pretend paper sign. Ask what Sam receives and what Ren does. If the answer was just supplied, record help. Prior familiarity alone does not invalidate later genuine child-owned meaning; use a different case only for a separate transfer claim, or leave that claim unobserved. A correct chorus, peer's response, teacher demonstration or class display cannot be assigned to every learner. After hints, record supported practice, not independent transfer.

Record honestly and return gently

Record actual date, local child identifier, scenario and exposure, exact action/message, access, conceptual help, uncertainty and next teaching step. No uploads, recordings, diagnoses or family financial questions are needed. Distinguish independent limited demonstration, supported practice, partial/unclear evidence and not yet observed. Real child reasoning about a fictional story can show a concept; the written story itself is not child data or a real purchase. An observed good or service can be meaningful evidence without requiring all extensions.

Stop for refusal, fatigue, distress, lost access or supervision needs. Return on a genuinely later date with a meaningful familiar or changed context; do not call same-day repetition retention. Family sharing is optional and never requires supplies, spending or private household information. This is a written teaching design, not evidence of tested classroom effectiveness or universal mastery.

Consumer Choice Studio: choose something useful

Revised written-design candidate, November 13, 2026. Not internally accepted, field-tested, or published.

Target and source boundary

Texas Education Agency, 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, PK4.VII.B.2: “Child demonstrates an understanding of what it means to be a consumer.” Official PDF: https://tea.texas.gov/academics/early-childhood-education/2022-texas-pkg-comprehensive-guide.pdf . The archived research-cell SHA-256 is 342b998cc373082fbb7b1cf9635b68338e115319b1a5969112345648653f256c. The archived official PDF (SHA-256 d935c5614f16bbb49a52edfbf18b95afb45b57e97085c16a612f68cb7b4fb3f4), printed page 67, was independently text-compared for this candidate. It explicitly lists identifying a consumer in a read-aloud story as one possible behavior and says no PK3 outcomes for this domain. That supports conceptual story reasoning without requiring actual purchasing; the quoted goal does not mandate every listed example. This verifies the relevant passage, not the entire book or actual child outcomes.

A consumer obtains or uses goods or services. The child explores this relationship by choosing a useful item for an invented art purpose and showing who uses it and how. Paying, owning, naming brands, saying the word consumer, reading, counting money, and speaking a sentence are not prerequisites. Borrowing and sharing can also make someone a consumer. Providing and consuming describe roles in a particular event, not permanent kinds of people. The person lending a marker might later use paper and be a consumer too.

The goal is consumer understanding, not a test of preferred artistic materials or verbal sophistication. A meaningful child-directed enactment can demonstrate the relationship without a spoken explanation. Distinguishing goods from services, comparing options, and identifying a provider extend teaching; they are not three mandatory official subtests. One observed example is limited evidence, never standardized mastery. A child choosing paper to fold rather than a marker to draw may offer a sound alternative.

There is no published PK3 social-studies VII outcome in this source inventory. Younger children may freely explore safe useful materials as PK3 enrichment only. Developmental levels are starting suggestions, not age locks. See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for family context. Familiar taught contexts can show consumer understanding when the child meaningfully chooses the relationship rather than immediately reproducing an adult-supplied answer. A new context is useful for a separate transfer claim, not a prerequisite for source evidence or later revisit. The allocation below keeps an optional transfer story distinct from teaching.

Materials and repeatable setup

For one child: two sheets of plain paper, two age-appropriate washable drawing tools (or an equivalent familiar, accessible mark-making method), one large reusable block or a spoken toy-building story for the adult demonstration, and one adult observation slip. Check sizes and materials against the actual children's safety plans; avoid loose caps, small props, sharp tools, ingestion hazards, and unstable equipment. No shopping trip, purchase, brand, food, money, screen, worksheet, printer or second adult is necessary.

For a class: enough paper and usable drawing tools for every participating child, or an established safe sharing arrangement that does not manufacture scarcity. Keep normal classroom supervision and access supports. If tools cannot safely be shared, use each child's own familiar materials or defer. Arrange an accessible work surface within the teacher's sightline, with a quiet participation option in the same supervised area. Children may dictate, point, sign, use their usual augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), or direct an adult to handle objects. Never remove necessary access to make a check “independent.”

Suggested examples, with optional transfer kept distinct: - ENTRY: a fictional maker wants to make a picture; actual paper/tools are available. Ask for an idea before teaching. - MODEL: an adult wants to build a pretend shelter for a toy and borrows a large block from a provider. No art or exit example is modeled. - DURING: the child chooses their own art purpose and uses or directs use of supplies. Optional service practice: the child asks the adult to hold paper still while the child directs the marks. - Optional transfer at EXIT: speak a new story of Lee borrowing a blanket from Jo to make a comfortable place for a toy to rest. Use no actual blanket or toy if unavailable. This is not a child care or real sleep arrangement. Ask what each person does and how the blanket helps Lee. No answer labels or prior demonstration. - Optional alternate transfer if the blanket relationship was just supplied: Sam receives help from Ren fixing a torn pretend paper sign. Ask what Sam receives and what Ren does. Do not introduce this story during teaching. - REVISIT: on a different actual date, observe a meaningful familiar or changed consumer context, such as obtaining an art supply for a chosen purpose. Record what the child understands then. A changed context can additionally inform transfer; it is not required for retention.

The family HTML sheets are optional adult reference/practice resources, not a ready-made independent assessment or required learner worksheets. They contain instructional labels and library examples. Do not show a supplied answer while claiming the child independently selected that answer. Prior familiarity alone is not conceptual help. If an adult or peer immediately supplies the relationship, record supported practice; later genuine child-owned meaning in a familiar context may still be valid. For a separately claimed fresh transfer probe, use another accessible case rather than rehearse its answer.

ENTRY — invite an idea, not a vocabulary quiz

Allow a brief invitation, usually a minute or two, with no forced timer: “This maker wants to make a picture. What could they use? Show me what they could do.” Offer the paper and drawing tools neutrally. Do not ask the child to define an unfamiliar word before allowing the action. Wait in the child's familiar communication rhythm. Record the actual first response before an answer-bearing hint. If they choose folding, texture, or a different useful option, ask “Show me how it helps,” not “Pick the marker.”

If the child watches, declines, asks for clarification, or needs an inaccessible resource, record that context rather than lack of understanding. Offer a break or a later invitation. Explain ordinary procedural directions without selecting the good, role, or purpose for them. If the first response is ambiguous, ask once, “Tell or show me more.” If meaning remains uncertain, record it as unclear; do not infer a complete idea from gaze alone without the child's established communication method.

MODEL — make the relationship visible elsewhere

With a block or an oral imaginary scene say: “I want to make a shelter for my toy. You have a block I may borrow. I get the block and use it to build. I am a consumer of that block. You are providing it in this story. I can return it; buying isn't required.” Show the reasoning between purpose, obtaining and use, not a rule that the person on the left is always the consumer. No real child must lend a possession. If needed, the adult plays both fictional roles.

Invite a question or disagreement. If the child says a consumer must pay, explain: “People can buy things, and they can also borrow or use shared things.” Do not require repetition of the word. An adult demonstration is teaching, not an observed child answer.

DURING — let the child make something they choose

Invite: “What would you like to make? What could you use?” Let the child choose a picture, folded form, marks, imagined design, or an equivalent purposeful project. Help obtain safe supplies. Ask what they are using and how it helps only when it supports their play; don't interrupt every mark with assessment. The payoff is a child-owned result and a meaningful resource choice, not a token for correct jargon.

If the child says “I'm the shopkeeper” while showing their own use, acknowledge that people can have different roles: “You can provide something, and when you use this paper you are also a consumer.” If they only count coins or name a brand, acknowledge that interest without crediting the target, then model a new purposeful use of a different safe object. If they choose a tool that does not serve their stated aim, ask them to try or show how; do not reject an imaginative purpose merely because the adult expected drawing. If frustration grows, simplify to one familiar purpose and two accessible options, or stop.

Optional service extension: the child may ask the adult to hold paper still, with the child directing what is made. Explain the help received without turning assistance into a mandatory dependency. Receiving conceptual hints is supported learning. Receiving neutral motor assistance is access when the child still owns the decision. Access and conceptual help can co-occur: record both, not a forced either/or code. Fade answer hints, never safety, translation, sensory supports or communication equipment.

EXIT — notice consumer meaning; optional transfer if useful

First notice whether the child has already clearly shown who obtains or uses something and how it serves a purpose during their own project. Record that limited evidence with any help; no extra story is compulsory. If a separate transfer question would be useful and the child is willing, offer the blanket story without supplying its answer. “Lee borrows a blanket from Jo to make a comfortable place for a toy to rest. What is Lee doing? What is Jo doing? How does the blanket help?” The child can enact, point using a complete balanced communication display, sign, speak in any understood language, or direct a proxy. Do not demand the term consumer. Do not force all three questions if the child's first enactment already makes the relationship clear.

A child shows Lee getting/using the blanket for the resting place: record the actual demonstration as limited consumer evidence. A correct response about Jo providing it can add role information, but failure to name Jo does not erase clear consumer understanding. A child reports “blanket” with no relationship: record partial evidence and ask a neutral clarification, not “Lee uses it, right?” A child chooses a different safe use and coherently shows consumption: preserve the sound alternative. Silence, unclear translation, unavailable communication options, an overheard solution, or adult-authored meaning is not independent negative evidence.

If the story is unfamiliar in an inaccessible way, use an accessible familiar context or defer. A familiar context can show understanding; changed context matters only for the separate transfer claim, never as obscure vocabulary or a trick. If the adult gives a content cue, teach supportively and mark this response helped. Do not label immediate repetition of the supplied answer an uncued or fresh transfer response. A later meaningful child-owned response in a familiar case can be recorded with its history. If fresh transfer is specifically sought, use another accessible case such as the alternate, or leave transfer unobserved. A real child's reasoning about fiction is legitimate conceptual evidence for this broad target; fiction does not equal fabricated child data. This written plan contains no actual child responses and proves no real transaction occurred.

CLASSROOM — one educator, honest individual opportunities

Begin together only while normal supervision is workable. Offer paper and tools, invite a maker's idea, then give the separate block demonstration. The class practices art choices with individual or safely shared supplies in the usual supervised activity area. No moving shop queue or pretend cashier is needed. Let children describe ideas to peers without pretending their answers are independent after a peer supplies them.

During an already familiar, supervised art/play routine, the educator may invite one nearby willing child to a brief individual observation while retaining sight and attention to everyone. Other children continue their chosen work, describe changes, or use a normal quiet alternative; they are not required to wait silently for assessment. Check only as many children as supervision and regulation allow, not a quota. If there is no safe observation opportunity, teach today and defer checks. Use a private teacher checklist of who was observed and who still needs an opportunity over later days. Never leave children alone or create a separate unsupervised station. If others immediately repeat an overheard solution, record that support, not independent meaning. Familiarity alone does not invalidate a later genuinely child-owned explanation or enactment. A new case is needed only to claim new-context transfer. Do not withhold teaching to preserve a test.

Group enthusiasm, chorus, a volunteer's answer, attendance or a class display are not evidence for every child. Record each child's action and support separately; unobserved children remain not yet observed. No public ranking or exclusion. Learners who need familiarization, alternate language, sensory quiet or more time still participate, with independent inference deferred when necessary.

Observation and decisions

On a private adult slip record: actual date; local classroom identifier (not an uploaded name); scenario and prior exposure; exact child action/message; who obtained/used what and for what purpose, when shown; neutral access used; conceptual help and whether it preceded the response; uncertainty/interpretation; next teaching move; later revisit status. Store by the educator's normal privacy practice; no recording, photo, financial history, disability inference, or online data is required.

Useful descriptive outcomes: independent limited demonstration, supported practice, partial/unclear evidence, not yet observed. These are observations for planning, not validated developmental scores. Evidence about goods or about services may demonstrate understanding; both are not mandatory in one sitting. Neither coin arithmetic nor a copied word alone establishes the relationship. A false-positive case is a child who repeats “consumer” after the adult supplies both role and purpose: praise participation, record help, and offer responsive teaching before another meaningful opportunity, familiar or changed; only a separate transfer claim needs changed context. A false rejection is a nonspeaking child who directs the proxy to obtain a needed tool and uses it purposefully while indicating the user; no spoken explanation is needed.

REVISIT and stopping

Stop when the child declines, is fatigued, distressed, or when supervision/access is inadequate. A short successful stage does not require completing every extension. On a genuinely later calendar date, offer a meaningful familiar or changed scenario and record the actual evidence then. Familiar recurrence can inform continuity; changed context may additionally inform transfer. Until actually observed, REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED. Same-session success is not retention, a printed future date is not an event, and one later example is not proven general mastery. Offer family sharing only optionally, using classroom supplies and without homework purchases or disclosure of household finances.
