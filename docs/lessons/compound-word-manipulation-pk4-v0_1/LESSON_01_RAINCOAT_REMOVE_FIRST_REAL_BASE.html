Raincoat without rain: play with a whole word

Preschool instructional-design materials · PK4

PK4.III.B.4
Written design reviewedNo classroom outcomes implied

Teach 1-on-1: raincoat without rain

What your child will explore

A whole word can contain meaningful smaller words. You and one PK4 child will play with taking the first word away: raincoat without rain leaves coat. Use pictures or meaningful gestures, not written-word reading. Allow about 5–8 minutes, or stop sooner. This small game addresses part of Texas PK4.III.B.4, not the whole outcome or a mastery test. There is no PK3.III.B.4 outcome to score.

Gather and prepare

Draw six large pictures on quarter sheets: book, shelf, rain, coat, sun and flower. One adult set is enough. You can also use the original support packet's pictures, but an adult must separate the picture areas from the answer equations and text before use. Keep adult answers hidden. Children do not need to cut, read, or hold small tokens. Use a clear reachable space and safe paper without sharp fasteners. No printer or device is required.

Check meanings through ordinary conversation or familiar objects. A shelf drawing can be confusing; point to a real shelf first or draw one clear shelf. Use raincoat only when the child knows the word and the rain/coat meanings. Sunflower is an optional later example, not a vocabulary surprise. Keep the child's usual AAC or signed communication available. Large high-contrast pictures, nearby meaningful gestures or accessible tactile/object references can help; preserve accessible picture/gesture support and a meaningful linguistic response, not just object movement. If access is unresolved, teach comfortably and do not score.

Two hands/meaningful gestures can replace pictures: each represents a known word, they come together for the whole, then one moves away. A child with motor needs can direct your neutral movement. Let the child choose pictures/gestures and their response mode. In another language, use a genuine compound verified by someone who knows that language; English parts do not always translate into a compound. A family's familiar word suggestion is welcome, not required homework or a purchase.

Start with a choice

“Want to play a word-changing game, watch me, or come back later?” Watching first is fine. If the child wants a first try, say the whole word RAINCOAT, show the rain and coat supports, and ask: “Say raincoat without rain. What word is left? You can tell, sign, or show me.” Do not point toward coat or say its first sound. Wait for the child's usual response time. No first answer is needed to earn a demonstration.

Show your turn, then give the child theirs

Put rain/coat aside. With BOOKSHELF, show book and shelf, say the whole word, and explain: “Bookshelf without book.” Move the book support away and say/sign “shelf.” “I took away a whole word. Shelf is left.” This is your model, not their evidence.

Bring back RAINCOAT and both supports. Ask again, “Raincoat without rain. What word is left?” Let the child own the word change. They can say/sign coat, use their familiar AAC, indicate the meaning, or direct a neutral proxy. Moving a card is optional and does not by itself prove word manipulation. Both pictures are allowed; they are source supports, not cheating.

Follow the response, not a script

  • If the child communicates COAT clearly, acknowledge the change: “You took rain away from raincoat.” Ask whether they want another turn, rather than demanding an explanation sentence.
  • If they say the whole word or both parts, say, “That's the whole word,” and show your bookshelf example again. Invite practice if they want it. Record any answer you supplied rather than calling its echo independent.
  • If they move a card without a meaningful response, ask neutrally what word remains, using their usual communication system. If meaning is still unclear, write uncertain; do not force repeated trials.
  • If they say rain or seem confused, check vocabulary and access before assuming a misunderstanding of removal. Rebuild the whole word, then model on bookshelf.
  • If they decline, turn away or tire, stop warmly. No score is necessary. Lack of an accessible opportunity is not evidence of inability.

Finish or return later

If welcome, offer raincoat once more after a natural pause, without giving the remainder. A familiar practiced word can still show an independent action; record rehearsal and possible echo honestly. For a different-word opportunity, use SUNFLOWER without SUN only if familiar and its answer has not just been supplied. This explores transfer, not a compulsory requirement. A one-off forced choice can be a guess; preserve uncertainty when you cannot distinguish naming/guessing from word change.

You can revisit on another real day. Same-word performance may inform retention; a different known word may inform transfer. A planned revisit is not observed learning. There is no required count of correct answers, deadline, or need to remove useful pictures, gestures or AAC.

A private note, if useful

Write the date, local child identifier, word/removal prompt, language, exact response, picture/gesture and access supports, any adult/peer answer, rehearsal, uncertainty and next teaching choice. Keep it private under your setting's normal records rules. No photos, recordings or uploads are needed.

A clear child-owned supported response is a source-action candidate, not mastery. A supplied answer followed by repetition is supported practice. Ambiguous guessing or picture naming is uncertain. Declined/deferred/inaccessible opportunities are not observed. An understood accessible attempt that does not yet show the change is observed, not yet shown. These describe opportunities, not children.

Do not credit a chorus, reading an answer equation, copying your motion or following a card-removal instruction alone. Do not reject signs, AAC, accent, familiar words, extra time or child-directed motor support. Fade answer hints, not access.

Keep the play meaningful

An optional invitation is “Can we change a word without changing a real coat?” The child's discovery is the payoff, not a sticker or a speed race. They can choose to watch, stop, change supports or try a familiar different word. Funny invented words can be enjoyable creative play; distinguish them from evidence about known compounds. If ready and interested, another lesson can explore last-word removal or substitution. Nothing here makes those compulsory stages.

The official PK4 outcome is “Child manipulates compound words with pictorial or gestural support.” Its examples include other operations as well. This guide is a written design: no child-learning results, educator field validation, physical print validation, delayed retention, or working child-alone software are claimed.

Teach a class: a word changes, one child's thinking at a time

Plan a short invitation, not a whole-class test

This PK4 word game explores removing the first meaningful word from a familiar compound: raincoat without rain leaves coat. Pictures/gestures remain available. It addresses one slice of PK4.III.B.4, not every operation or mastery. Do not invent a PK3.III.B.4 score. One educator can teach this without extra staff or devices, but cannot reliably assess every child in one short session.

Use a 2-minute shared demonstration, then optional individual turns of roughly one minute during an already safe supervised routine. A small group of 3–4 can use one six-picture set in about 6–10 minutes, with flexible timing. For a full class, spread quiet individual opportunities over later routines or days. If the room cannot remain safely supervised, postpone individual observations. Silence is not a deficit and a missed turn is not a failure.

Set up for supervision and access

Prepare one teacher set of six large pictures: book, shelf, rain, coat, sun and flower. Quarter-sheet drawings work. If using the original support packet, an adult separates picture areas from equations and directions. Never display an answer key during an individual opportunity. No child scissors, tiny pieces, magnets or fasteners. Keep the surface reachable and the entire class in sight.

Choose a familiar, already established seated/play routine that children can safely continue within your supervision. Do not introduce a new independent station just to make assessment time; no headphones, screens or unsupervised corner are assumed. Position individual turns so you can see and respond to everyone. If behavior or care needs make that impossible, teach together and collect no individual evidence today.

Check word meanings in conversation before the game. A real shelf can clarify a confusing picture. Do not use SUNFLOWER for transfer unless the child knows the whole word and parts. Prepare the child's usual sign/AAC and picture/gesture access. Meaningful gestures can replace cards; neutral physical assistance can follow a child's direction. Large/high-contrast pictures and familiar tactile/object references can support access, but mere object movement is not linguistic evidence. If access cannot be made workable, pause assessment and seek appropriate support rather than scoring inability.

A knowledgeable speaker can verify genuine compounds in a child's home language. Do not assume literal English translations preserve word structure. Families may offer familiar examples without compulsory homework, purchases or private disclosure. No accent, reading skill or oral explanation sentence is required.

Shared invitation and adult model

Invite watching, trying, or returning later. “We can change a word without changing a real coat. Want to see?” Use BOOKSHELF for your demonstration: say the whole word, show book and shelf meanings, remove book, and say/sign “shelf.” “Bookshelf without book leaves shelf.” Model slowly enough to follow, not as a speed challenge.

Children may join or comment. A chorus is worthwhile practice, not individual evidence. Keep SUNFLOWER answers out of this demonstration if you plan to use that optional transfer opportunity. Children can choose a response mode and whether to participate. Do not make public rankings or announce who is behind.

Individual child-owned turns

During the established supervised routine, invite one willing child to a quiet nearby turn. Keep peers from answering for them without shaming helpful classmates: “This is their thinking turn; you'll have your own if you want.” If a peer supplies the answer, record supported/contaminated practice, not independence, and defer a fresh opportunity. Do not require silence from the whole class for a long testing block.

Show RAINCOAT's rain and coat pictures or agreed gestures and say the whole word. Ask: “Say raincoat without rain. What word is left? You can tell, sign, or show me.” Do not point to coat, provide its first sound, or move the answer before the child decides. Wait for their usual processing time. A child may communicate the remainder without touching cards or direct a neutral proxy. Moving a named card alone does not establish word-level manipulation.

If a child wants to try before the shared model, allow an optional first invitation; no entry performance is required for instruction. Use the same private observation distinctions whether the turn occurs before or after modeling.

Respond and keep the room safe

  • Clear COAT response: acknowledge the word change and ask whether the child wants another turn. Record the actual prompt and response, not a mastery badge.
  • Whole word/both parts repeated: check understanding, model BOOKSHELF again and invite supported practice. Do not count an answer you just supplied as independent.
  • Only a card moved: neutrally ask what word remains with usual AAC/sign access. If naming/guessing and manipulation cannot be distinguished, record uncertain.
  • Wrong remainder/confusion: check word familiarity, hearing/communication and task meaning; rebuild the whole word and teach. Do not diagnose from one response.
  • Child declines or room needs your attention: stop. Return to supervision immediately. Mark the opportunity not observed and offer a later invitation without penalty.

No quota is worth compromising safe supervision. Rotate invitations fairly across routines, including quieter children and children needing more time, rather than repeatedly selecting the quickest responders. Unseen children remain unobserved, not assumed to share the group's result.

Independent opportunity and later return

An optional later turn with RAINCOAT can show an independently owned supported response even though practiced. Record rehearsal and possible echo. If the child wants a different familiar word, SUNFLOWER without SUN can explore transfer only when its answer has not just been supplied. Novelty is not required to count every legitimate source action. Avoid repeated forced choices to manufacture certainty; picture naming/position guessing remains uncertain when not resolved.

On another actual day, the same word may explore retention and a different known word transfer. Record what really happens. A planned revisit or same-session repeat does not establish delayed retention. First-word removal need not be mastered before other meaningful word play; last-word removal/substitution are optional next lessons, not an imposed age ladder.

Private individual record

Use a blank note or observation form: local child identifier, date/phase, language, whole and removed word, exact prompt and child response, pictures/gestures, neutral access help, any adult/peer answer, rehearsal, alternative explanations and next teaching choice. Keep records secure; no public scoreboard, photos, recordings or uploads are necessary.

Distinguish: source-action candidate, not mastery (clear child-owned supported change); supported practice (answer supplied); uncertain (ambiguous picture choice/echo); not observed (declined/deferred/inaccessible/interrupted); observed, not yet shown (understood accessible attempt did not show the change). These are opportunity descriptions, not child labels. Signs, AAC, accent, familiar words, processing time and child-directed motor assistance are valid alternatives. Fade answers, never access.

Scope and honest limits

PK4.III.B.4 says “Child manipulates compound words with pictorial or gestural support.” Its examples also include adding, substitution and combining gestures. This first-removal game does not cover the whole outcome or the whole developmental program. Belonging, agency, communication and enjoyable discovery matter more than a quick score. Child-invented funny words can be creative play without being scored as manipulation of known compounds. Raincoat without rain: play with a whole word

Purpose and fit

Planning label PK4 M4-1. Teach a PK4 child to remove the first meaningful word from a familiar compound and communicate the remaining word, with pictures or gestures. Say a whole word, make a change, and discover what remains. This is word play, not a test of reading, pronunciation, speed, fine-motor skill, or obedience. Invite a child who wants to play; a child may watch, choose the support or response mode, pause, or decline without losing access to teaching.

Official source: PK4.III.B.4: “Child manipulates compound words with pictorial or gestural support.” The archived 2022 Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines, printed page 33, includes deleting a first or second part and stating the remaining word; it also includes adding, substituting, and combining gestures. This lesson teaches just first-word removal, not the whole breadth of that outcome. PK3.III.B.4 says “Three-year olds do not typically develop word manipulation skills, so no outcome is included.” Do not invent a PK3 score or a universal age deadline. See DEVELOPMENTAL_SEQUENCE.md for conditional follow-on choices, not mandatory stages.

Prepare in about five minutes

For one adult and one child: six large picture cards (book, shelf, rain, coat, sun, flower); a clear tabletop or floor space; a private scrap of paper for observations. Draw each meaning on a quarter sheet of paper, or use the picture areas in the original supports. Adult preparation only: cut around picture areas, excluding printed answer equations and directions. No child cutting, tiny tokens, magnets or loose fasteners. Rounded paper corners and a clear reachable surface are enough. The shelf picture must mean a shelf, not an unknown collection of books. If unclear, draw one shelf or point to a real shelf before the game. Adult answer key stays face down/out of the child's view.

No printer is necessary. Use two hands or familiar agreed gestures, one for each meaningful word; bring them together for the compound and move one away. For low vision, use clear nearby gestures if useful, large high-contrast drawings, or meaningful tactile/object references with language the child can access. A tactile arrangement alone is not proof of word manipulation; preserve a meaningful linguistic response and pictorial/gestural source support through accessible signing/gesture. If access is not workable, teach accessible vocabulary and record access unresolved, not a failed child. Use the child's usual AAC, signed communication or home language. No device is otherwise required.

Check before assessment that the child knows the whole compound and its parts through ordinary conversation or familiar objects. Do not drill the correct remainder. RAINCOAT is a coat worn in rain, BOOKSHELF a shelf for books, SUNFLOWER a familiar kind of flower. If sunflower is unfamiliar, omit that transfer probe. Do not translate English parts mechanically: a fluent adult must confirm a genuine compound and word-level operation in the child's language. Invite family vocabulary knowledge without requiring a purchase, a home worksheet, or disclosure. English unfamiliarity is not inability to manipulate words.

Teaching route: ENTRY, MODEL, DURING, EXIT, REVISIT

Allow about 5–8 minutes for one child, shorter if attention or comfort requires. Timing is an adult planning estimate, not a speed criterion. There is no minimum correct count.

ENTRY, optional first invitation (about one minute). Offer cards or gestures: “Want to play a word-changing game, watch me, or come back later?” If the child wants a first turn, say RAINCOAT as a whole, show rain and coat meanings without pointing to the answer, and ask: “Say raincoat without rain. You can tell, sign, or show me the word that's left.” Wait long enough for the child's usual processing and communication. Record the child's actual response if useful. An attempt is not a prerequisite for seeing a demonstration. If the child chooses to watch, begin MODEL and record ENTRY not observed.

MODEL, adult-owned BOOKSHELF (about one minute). Put rain/coat aside. Say: “Bookshelf. Book and shelf make bookshelf. I'm saying bookshelf without book.” Show both meaning pictures or agreed gestures, move the book support away, and say/sign “shelf.” Briefly explain: “I took away a whole word, book. Shelf is left.” This is the adult's answer, never child evidence. Do not insist on terminology such as compound, base, or lexical deletion. A child may look, ask, join, or take a break.

DURING, child-owned RAINCOAT (about two minutes). Restore the whole word and both supports. “Now raincoat without rain. What word is left?” The child controls the linguistic answer. Moving rain away may help, but physical movement is not required: a child can say/sign COAT, select COAT with AAC, or direct a neutral adult proxy. Do not give a starting sound, point at coat, finish the word, or move the answer first and label it independent. Both part pictures are permissible source supports; their mere presence is not answer-giving. Avoid treating a card-removal instruction alone as a word problem.

Respond to what happens: - Child communicates COAT after the prompt: acknowledge the thinking, “You took rain away from raincoat.” Note the exact prompt, support and response rather than awarding mastery. Ask whether the child wants another turn; no compulsory explanation sentence. - Child says RAINCOAT or both parts: “That's the whole word. Watch my bookshelf again: without book, shelf is left.” Record that help; return to raincoat only if interested. A repeated answer just supplied by an adult is supported practice, not independent evidence. - Child removes a card but gives no meaningful remainder: ask neutrally “What word is left?” Offer the usual response mode, not a newly narrowed single-answer button. If meaning remains uncertain, record UNCERTAIN. Do not infer failure or success from movement alone. - Child says “rain,” follows a position cue, or seems confused: check the meaning and prompt first. Rebuild the whole, then use the adult model. Do not label a disability or require a faster retry. - Child says nothing, looks away, or wants to stop: acknowledge and stop. Check whether communication, hearing, vocabulary, fatigue or access made the opportunity invalid. Record NOT_OBSERVED if there was no interpretable opportunity.

EXIT, optional independent opportunity (about one minute). After teaching and a brief natural pause, offer RAINCOAT again without supplying its remainder. An independently owned response can be source evidence on a practiced word; record rehearsal honestly, do not claim transfer. If the child wants a different familiar word, use SUNFLOWER without SUN with sun/flower pictures or gestures; do not pre-say FLOWER. This adds a transfer opportunity only if the child knows sunflower and has not just heard its answer. If a peer/adult has supplied that answer, record contamination and defer the independent observation. Do not demand a novel item to count every valid source action. A single forced choice or lucky picture selection leaves alternative explanations unresolved; describe it as uncertain unless the child's meaningful word-level response is clear in context. No repeated quizzing to force certainty.

REVISIT, optional later return. On another actual day, invite this word game in a comfortable routine. Record date, familiar word, support, help and actual response. A different known word can explore transfer; the same word can explore retention. Neither happens merely because it is planned. Until observed: REVISIT NOT_OBSERVED. Do not use an immediate repeat to claim delayed retention.

Evidence that respects the child

Use the private observation page or a blank note. Record anonymous/local child identifier, date, phase, language/communication, whole word and removed word, exact prompt, meaningful child response, picture/gesture support, neutral motor/AAC assistance, any answer given by adult/peer, familiarity/rehearsal, alternative explanations, and the next teaching choice. Keep notes in the setting's authorized private storage; no public scores, names on a display, photos, recordings or uploads are needed.

Use explicit decisions, not conflicting U/H/A/X shorthand: - SOURCE_ACTION_CANDIDATE_NOT_MASTERY: a clear child-owned word-level response after a first-word removal prompt, with accessible picture/gesture support. Record how it was shown and whether rehearsed. This is a bounded observation, not proof of mastery or of all III.B.4 operations. - SUPPORTED_PRACTICE: an answer was supplied/modelled for that attempt; teaching is worthwhile but independence is not established. - UNCERTAIN: an observed response could be picture naming, position guessing, echo or misunderstood language. Record the ambiguity and choose a later low-pressure opportunity, not a score. - NOT_OBSERVED: declined, deferred, interrupted, or no interpretable accessible opportunity. This is not a child deficit. - OBSERVED_NOT_YET_SHOWN: an accessible, understood opportunity occurred but the response did not show the requested change. Teach the relevant meaning/operation; do not equate this with inability.

False credit: a chorus, the adult moving and naming the remainder, reading the printed equation, mechanically copying a neighbor, matching colors, or removing a syllable without a meaningful whole-word result does not establish child-owned compound manipulation. False rejection: familiar words, signs, AAC, accented speech, processing time, neutral child-directed physical support and source-permitted pictures/gestures do not disqualify genuine responses. Fade answer cues, never needed access. The helper in evidence.py organizes synthetic examples only; it cannot observe intent or award mastery.

Two human modes and safe classroom operation

CO-PLAY / ADULT-LED one child: use the procedure above responsively; a caregiver does not need a school-style test, technical vocabulary, or purchased cards. CLASSROOM: a brief shared BOOKSHELF model can invite curiosity, but a chorus is practice only. With one educator, offer individual raincoat turns within an already safe familiar seated/routine activity that remains in sight. For a small group of 3–4, one teacher set of six cards suffices, with each child choosing a verbal, signed or gesture response. Peers may observe the model but cannot supply an assessed answer. In a full class, do not promise to assess everyone in one 8-minute session. Keep the established supervised activity going; take one willing child's quiet turn where all remain visible, then rotate across later routines/days. If a quiet individual turn compromises supervision, postpone it and record NOT_OBSERVED. Do not invent an extra adult, headphones, unsupervised stations or independent screen work. Offer everyone an invitation over time; no public rank or turn forced on a reluctant child.

SOLO: not implemented. A static page is an adult guide, not a working child-alone tutor. No digital narration, independent interface, or cross-mode parity is claimed.

Enjoyment, challenge and continuity

“Can we change a word without changing a real coat?” is an optional playful invitation, not a trick whose answer a child must guess. The payoff is the child's meaningful change, not a sticker or speed contest. Children can choose pictures versus gestures, response language, whether to play, and whether to try a familiar different compound. A made-up funny word is welcome as creative play; distinguish it from evidence about a known compound. Do not punish invention.

If the whole/part meanings are unfamiliar, return to meaningful vocabulary and ordinary word play. If first removal is comfortable and the child is interested, another lesson can explore last-word removal or substitution; there is no required lockstep stage. This small literacy lesson can support agency, communication and belonging but does not cover every developmental domain or establish a program's breadth. Family suggestions can improve word relevance; adults verify the actual linguistic structure, rather than calling all translations compounds.
